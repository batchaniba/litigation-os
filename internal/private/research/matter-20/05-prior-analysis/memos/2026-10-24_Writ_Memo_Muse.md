# Deep Legal Research — CA Traditional Writ of Mandate for Form I-918, Supplement B Certification under Penal Code § 679.10

**Date researched:** 24 October 2026. **Confidence key:** `verified live` = text read in full via browser_open in this session; `index` = proposition appears in a search snippet / secondary reproduction and must be re-checked on the primary source before filing. **No holding, quotation, or pincite in this report is invented**; where a pincite could not be verified in a primary text read here, it is labelled `index` and the gap is listed in "Could not verify."

**Bottom line, in one paragraph.** Penal Code § 679.10, subd. (g)(1) uses conditional "shall certify … *when* the victim was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity *and* has been helpful…" — so the *signature* is mandatory once those two predicates are found, but the statute gives the certifying official the job of *finding* them, and no California published case found in this research holds that a superior court has ordered a law-enforcement agency to sign a Supplement B, or that it may not. The viable writ theory is therefore two-track, in this order of strength: (1) **vacatur + controlled remand** — the denial applied the wrong legal standard, ignored the (g)/(h)/(k) rules, relied on a prohibited ground, or rested on materially inaccurate facts, so the court vacates and orders reconsideration under specified legal rules without dictating the outcome (Common Cause, Saleeby, CCSO); (2) **direct certification** — available only if the court can lawfully find *both* predicates itself on undisputed / properly found facts *and* that, on those facts, only one reasonable exercise of discretion remains (CCSO at p. 827, citing Bank of Italy; Common Cause's "no discretion to exercise" exception at p. 446). Direct certification is the high-risk prayer: it collides head-on with the federal guidance and Fifth Circuit law that signing is discretionary, and with the Supplement B attestation that the signer's agency is investigating or prosecuting the qualifying activity. Plead direct certification in the alternative, lead with controlled remand, and build the record so the remand order does the work even if direct relief is denied.

**Binding vs. persuasive, generally.** California Supreme Court cases (Common Cause, Saleeby, Western States, Mercer, Sanders) are binding on a California superior court. California Court of Appeal cases (CCSO, Jackson, Espinoza) are binding on a superior court in the absence of contrary Supreme Court authority, subject to the usual rule that a Court of Appeal decision from another district is still binding on a trial court. Fifth Circuit / federal district cases (Ordonez Orosco, Yoni) and USCIS guidance are *persuasive only* in state court, and — critically — they construe *federal* law, which does not displace a *state* statutory duty under § 679.10; they are the agency's best authority, not a jurisdictional bar. USCIS Administrative Appeals Office decisions are non-precedential agency adjudications.

## Q1 — Penal Code § 679.10 in full: subdivisions (a)–(k), plus (l)–(o) as now in force

**Primary source (verified live):** https://california.public.law/codes/penal_code_section_679.10 (2025 text). Cross-check against California Legislative Information before filing; the mission asks for (a)–(k), but the current statute runs to (o), and (l)–(o) matter to remedy and victim status, so they are included and flagged as beyond (a)–(k).

### (a) Certifying entity
Any of: (1) a state or local law enforcement agency (including UC, CSU, and school-district police); (2) a prosecutor; (3) a judge; (4) any other authority with responsibility for detection, investigation, or prosecution of a qualifying crime; (5) agencies with criminal detection/investigative jurisdiction in their area of expertise (including child protective services, the Civil Rights Department, and the Department of Industrial Relations). § 679.10(a).

### (b) Certifying official
(1) The head of the certifying entity; (2) a person in a supervisory role specifically designated by the head to issue Supplement B certifications; (3) a judge; (4) any other certifying official defined in 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(2). § 679.10(b). *Practice point:* the respondent must be the entity *and*, where possible, the designated official — a writ against a line officer who is not a designated official will be met with a "wrong respondent" defence.

### (c) Qualifying criminal activity — list
"'Qualifying criminal activity' has the same meaning as qualifying criminal activity pursuant to Section 101(a)(15)(U)(iii) of the federal Immigration and Nationality Act which includes, but is not limited to" the following (emphasis added — the list is expressly non-exhaustive, which matters for the "substantially similar" argument): (1) rape; (2) torture; (3) human trafficking; (4) incest; (5) domestic violence; (6) sexual assault; (7) abusive sexual conduct; (8) prostitution; (9) sexual exploitation; (10) female genital mutilation; (11) being held hostage; (12) peonage; (13) perjury; (14) involuntary servitude; (15) slavery; (16) kidnapping; (17) abduction; **(18) unlawful criminal restraint; (19) false imprisonment**; (20) blackmail; (21) extortion; (22) manslaughter; (23) murder; (24) felonious assault; (25) witness tampering; (26) obstruction of justice; (27) fraud in foreign labor contracting; (28) stalking. § 679.10(c).

### (d) Substantially similar activity
"A 'qualifying crime' includes criminal offenses for which the nature and elements of the offenses are substantially similar to the criminal activity described in subdivision (c), and the attempt, conspiracy, or solicitation to commit any of those offenses." § 679.10(d). This mirrors 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(9) (verified live, see Q5). *Use:* Penal Code § 146(a) (officer arrest/detention without lawful authority) is not itself listed in (c), so it must be pleaded, if at all, as substantially similar to false imprisonment / unlawful criminal restraint under (d) — by comparing *nature and elements*, not by label. Do not plead § 146(a) as if it were enumerated in (c); the agency will correctly point out it is not.

### (e) DOJ-accredited representative — definition
A person approved by DOJ to represent individuals before the Board of Immigration Appeals, immigration courts, or DHS, working for a recognised nonprofit/religious/charitable/social-service organisation, accreditation in good standing. § 679.10(e).

### (f) Police-report copy
On request of the victim, licensed attorney, or DOJ-accredited representative, a state/local agency with whom the victim filed a police report "shall provide a copy of the police report within seven days." § 679.10(f). *Use:* a freestanding ministerial duty; its breach supports a writ even apart from certification, and a refusal to produce the report cannot then be used to fault the victim for "inability to produce a crime report" (see (k)(3)(G)).

### (g) The "shall certify" provision — the centre of the case
> "(1) Upon the request of the victim, victim's family member, licensed attorney representing the victim, or representative fully accredited by [DOJ] …, **a certifying official from a certifying entity shall certify victim helpfulness on the Form I-918 Supplement B certification, when the victim was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity and has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful to the detection or investigation or prosecution of that qualifying criminal activity.** The certifying entity shall forward [the] completed … certification to the victim [etc.] without requiring the victim to provide government-issued identification." § 679.10(g)(1) (verified live).
> "(2) A victim … does not have to be present in the United States at [the] time of submitting the certification request or filing the petition … and may apply for certification while outside of the United States." § 679.10(g)(2).

**Is certification mandatory, or discretionary? — the correct, defensible answer.** The text is *conditionally* mandatory, not unconditionally mandatory and not purely discretionary:

- **Discretionary (predicate) stage:** the official must determine (i) victim of a qualifying activity and (ii) helpfulness (past, present, or likely future). The statute does not say "in the official's sole discretion," but it assigns the determination to the official, and the federal scheme (see Q5/Q6) treats that determination as discretionary. A court may not simply substitute its own view of a genuinely debatable predicate finding.
- **Ministerial (consequence) stage:** *once* those predicates are established (by the agency's own findings, by undisputed facts, or by a lawful court finding on review), "shall certify" leaves no residual discretion to refuse for other reasons — and (k)(3) then bars refusal on the listed grounds. The Commission on State Mandates reads the statute the same way in substance: the test-claim statute "mandates the certifying entity to complete a U Visa certification when it has determined in the normal course of existing law enforcement duties that the qualifying criminal activity occurred … and that the victim … has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested" (index: https://csm.ca.gov/matters/documents/CorrectedDecisionandPsandGswebfinal.pdf).
- **What is therefore reviewable:** whether the predicate determination was made under the correct legal standard, on relevant evidence, without reliance on prohibited grounds or materially inaccurate facts, and with the (h) presumption applied (see Q3). That is the traditional-mandate abuse-of-discretion / failure-to-proceed-as-required-by-law attack — not a claim that "shall" alone entitles the petitioner to a signature on demand.

### (h) Helpfulness and the rebuttable presumption
"For purposes of determining helpfulness pursuant to subdivision (g), there is a rebuttable presumption that a victim is helpful, has been helpful, or is likely to be helpful … **if the victim has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested by law enforcement. If the victim reasonably asserts they were unaware of a request for cooperation, their failure to cooperate does not rebut the presumption.**" § 679.10(h) (verified live). Three consequences: (1) the *agency* bears the practical burden of identifying a specific reasonable request and a refusal/failure; silence or absence of a request does not rebut. (2) "Likely to be helpful" is forward-looking — no completed cooperation, and no request yet made, is required. (3) Where no investigating entity requested information (a Q6 argument), the presumption is *stronger*, not weaker — there is nothing to have refused — unless the agency can show a request was made and refused. The federal helpfulness test in 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(b)(3) is to the same effect ("has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested") (verified live).

### (i) Written-denial procedure
> "(1) The certifying official **shall fully complete and sign** the … certification and, regarding victim helpfulness, include **specific details about the nature of the crime investigated or prosecuted and a detailed description of the victim's helpfulness or likely helpfulness**…" § 679.10(i)(1).
> "(2) **If a certifying entity does not certify** …, they **shall provide a written explanation for the denial**. The written denial **shall include specific details of any reasonable requests for cooperation and a detailed description of how the victim refused to cooperate.**" § 679.10(i)(2) (verified live).

*Gap to plead carefully:* (i)(2)'s second sentence, read literally, prescribes the content of a denial *premised on non-cooperation*. It does not in terms prescribe what a denial premised on "no qualifying crime" must contain. The petition should therefore **not** claim (i)(2) alone requires a crime-by-crime written analysis; instead, argue (a) a denial that invokes non-cooperation without the (i)(2) specifics is statutorily insufficient on its face; (b) a denial that rests on "no qualifying crime" with no reasoning at all is still vulnerable under Saleeby's findings principle (findings "sufficient to apprise interested parties and the courts of the bases for the administrative action," 39 Cal.3d at pp. 566–567) and as a failure to proceed as required by law; and (c) (i)(1)'s "specific details" duty for a *signed* certification shows the Legislature's intent that the decision be reasoned and particularised, not conclusory.

### (j) Processing deadlines
30 days from request; 7 days from the first business day after receipt if the noncitizen is in removal proceedings; 7 days on the same basis if the victim asserts a qualifying family member will lose eligibility in 60 days or fewer because a sibling will turn 18, a child will turn 21, or the victim will turn 21. § 679.10(j) (verified live). Delay beyond these periods is a separate ministerial breach and supports urgency / alternative-writ relief, but delay alone does not prove entitlement to certification.

### (k) Prohibitions and limits — including what is *not* required
> "(1) **A current investigation, the filing of charges, an apprehension of the suspect who committed the qualifying crime, closing of a case, and a prosecution or conviction are not required** for the victim to request and obtain the … certification." § 679.10(k)(1).
> "(2) A certifying official **shall not refuse** … **solely because a case has already been prosecuted or otherwise closed, or because the time for commencing a criminal action has expired.**" § 679.10(k)(2).
> "(3) A certifying entity **shall not refuse** … **for any of the following reasons:** (A) victim's criminal history; (B) victim's immigration history; (C) victim's gang membership/affiliation; (D) the entity's belief the petition will not be approved by USCIS; (E) victim has an open case with another certifying entity; (F) extent of harm suffered; (G) victim's inability to produce a crime report; (H) victim's cooperation/refusal in a *separate* case." § 679.10(k)(3) (verified live).

Note the asymmetry the agency will exploit: (k)(1) says no *current* investigation is required, but it does not say no *investigation or prosecution at all* is required — the (g) predicate still requires helpfulness "to the detection or investigation or prosecution" of the activity. The petitioner's answer is (h): likely helpfulness + presumption covers the case where investigation of the *officer's* conduct has not been opened, provided the victim has not refused a reasonable request and stands ready to cooperate with any such investigation. Do not overclaim (k)(1) as eliminating the helpfulness predicate.

### (l)–(o) — current text beyond the mission's (a)–(k)
- (l) A certifying official may withdraw certification *only* if the victim refuses to provide information/assistance when reasonably requested.
- (m) No disclosure of the victim/requester's immigration status except to comply with federal law/legal process or with authorisation.
- (n) Annual reporting to the Legislature (requests, signed, denied).
- (o) Victim categories: (o)(1) an entity *may* certify for direct, indirect, and bystander/witness victims; (o)(2) direct victim = person who suffered direct harm / directly and proximately harmed; (o)(3) indirect victim = qualifying family member of a direct victim who is incompetent, incapacitated, or deceased (spouse, unmarried children under 21, parents if direct victim under 21, siblings under 18 if direct victim under 21), who must cooperate but need not possess information about the crime itself; (o)(4) bystander/witness victim = individual not the direct target but who suffered unusually direct injury. (Verified live.) *Note:* (o)(1) says "may certify" for these categories — the agency will cite it as discretionary language; the response is that (o) expands *who* may be certified, while (g) governs *when* certification must follow once victimhood + helpfulness are found.

## Q2 — False imprisonment (§ 236) and § 146(a)

### Elements — § 236 (verified live via leginfo, index in this session)
"False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another." Pen. Code § 236 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=236.). CALCRIM No. 1240 (index: https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/1200/1240.html, cited in search results) states the prosecution must prove: (1) the defendant intentionally and unlawfully restrained, detained, or confined a person; and (2) the defendant's act made that person stay or go somewhere against that person's will. Felony false imprisonment adds violence, menace, fraud, or deceit (Pen. Code § 237(a)). No movement is required (that is what distinguishes § 236 from kidnapping, § 207), and no minimum duration is required — even a brief detention suffices if unlawful and non-consensual.

### § 146(a) (verified via leginfo snippet, index)
"Every public officer, or person pretending to be a public officer, who, under the pretense or color of any process or other legal authority, does any of the following, **without a regular process or other lawful authority**, is guilty of a misdemeanor: (a) Arrests any person or detains that person against his or her will…" Pen. Code § 146 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=146.&lawCode=PEN). Elements to plead: (i) public-officer (or pretended-officer) status; (ii) acting under pretense/color of process or legal authority; (iii) arrest/detention against will; (iv) absence of regular process *or other lawful authority*. § 146 is a misdemeanour; § 236 is a wobbler.

### Can an unlawful arrest/detention by a peace officer constitute false imprisonment?
**Yes, in principle — and the civil cases say so expressly.** "A police officer who makes an arrest without a warrant and without justification may be held civilly liable for false arrest and imprisonment" (Dragna v. White (1955) 45 Cal.2d 469, 471, quoted in search results, index); "false arrest is but one way of committing a false imprisonment" (Asgari v. City of Los Angeles (1997) 15 Cal.4th 744, 752 fn. 3, quoted in search results, index). The converse immunity confirms the principle: Pen. Code § 847(b)(1) provides there is no civil liability for false arrest/false imprisonment against a peace officer acting within scope of authority if "[t]he arrest was lawful, or the peace officer, at the time of the arrest, had reasonable cause to believe the arrest was lawful" (index: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-pen/part-2/title-3/chapter-5/section-847/). The statutory scheme therefore treats *lawfulness / reasonable cause to believe lawfulness* as the dividing line — an arrest that is neither lawful nor reasonably believed lawful is the paradigm false imprisonment.

### What "without lawful authority" / "unlawful" requires, and whether probable cause defeats the offence
**Probable cause — even if later shown debatable — defeats the offence, if it was objectively reasonable at the time.** This is the agency's strongest Q2 point and must be conceded in its proper scope:

- Probable cause is objective: "Whether probable cause exists depends upon the reasonable conclusion to be drawn from the facts known to the arresting officer at the time of the arrest" (Devenpeck v. Alford (2004) 543 U.S. 146, 152–153, quoted in 9th Cir. model instructions, index: https://ce9.uscourts.gov/jury-instructions/node/160). The officer's subjective motive is irrelevant; the question is whether a reasonable officer in the same position would conclude there was probable cause (Espinoza v. Shiomoto, index: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2017/e064252a.html — "the sole question is whether a reasonable officer in the same position would objectively conclude there was probable cause").
- "Debatable" probable cause therefore defeats a § 236/§ 146 predicate: the petitioner must show *no* reasonable officer would have found probable cause (or, for § 146, no regular process *and* no other lawful authority, e.g. no Pen. Code § 836 basis and no Veh. Code § 40300.5 exception). Mere proof that the DUI could not ultimately be proved beyond a reasonable doubt, or that charges were dropped, does *not* establish false imprisonment — probable cause is "a probability or substantial chance of criminal activity, not an actual showing" (District of Columbia v. Wesby (2018) 583 U.S. 48, 57, quoted in 9th Cir. materials, index).
- For a *misdemeanour* DUI arrest, probable cause alone is not enough: Pen. Code § 836(a)(1) requires the misdemeanour to have been committed in the officer's presence, unless a statutory exception (Veh. Code § 40300.5) applies. An arrest with probable cause but without presence and without a § 40300.5 trigger is "not lawfully arrested" in Mercer's sense — see Q4. Whether that *also* makes it a § 236/§ 146 crime has **no published California holding located in this research** (see "Could not verify") — Mercer was a licence-revocation case, not a prosecution of an officer.

### Fourth Amendment violation vs. statutory crime — do not conflate them
- A Fourth Amendment violation turns on objective reasonableness / probable cause at the moment of seizure. A § 236 conviction additionally requires intentional, unlawful restraint and, in practice, proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the restraint lacked legal justification; a § 146 conviction requires the color-of-authority + absence-of-lawful-authority elements above.
- A seizure can therefore be *unconstitutional* without necessarily being a *crime* by the officer (e.g. a good-faith but mistaken arrest may still be unreasonable yet lack the culpability / unlawfulness the criminal statutes require, and § 847(b)(1)'s "reasonable cause to believe lawful" standard protects the officer civilly in that zone). Conversely, a seizure can be *criminally* unlawful in the § 146 sense (no process, no authority) even if the Fourth Amendment analysis would focus on a different defect.
- **For certification purposes, the petitioner does not need to prove the officer's criminal guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.** § 679.10 asks whether the victim "was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity" — it does not prescribe a burden of proof for the certifying official's determination, and no case found sets one. The petition should argue the certification standard is the agency's determination in the normal course (cf. Commission on State Mandates, above) on the preponderance / reasonable-grounds showing that the elements occurred, while candidly flagging that the agency will demand a higher showing and that the court has not yet ruled on the standard. Do not plead "the officer committed § 236 beyond a reasonable doubt" as if that were the test; plead "on the facts found, the elements of § 236 / substantially-similar § 146(a) conduct are made out, and no lawful authority (presence or § 40300.5) existed."

### Strongest California authority FOR treating a warrantless DUI arrest lacking probable cause as false imprisonment
1. Pen. Code §§ 236, 146(a) text (above) — an arrest without lawful authority is literally within both.
2. Dragna / Asgari (civil false arrest = false imprisonment) + § 847(b)(1) (immunity *only* if lawful or reasonably believed lawful) — the negative implication is the petitioner's best doctrinal anchor.
3. Mercer (binding, Supreme Court) for the proposition that, without observed volitional movement, a misdemeanour DUI arrest is not a "lawful arrest" under § 836 — see Q4.

### Strongest authority AGAINST
1. Devenpeck / Wesby / Espinoza objective-probable-cause rule — if *any* reasonable officer on the pre-arrest facts would find probable cause *and* a § 40300.5 trigger existed, the arrest was lawful and there is no § 236/§ 146 predicate, however weak the eventual DUI case.
2. § 847(b)(1) "reasonable cause to believe lawful" — even an arrest that turns out to have been unlawful is shielded civilly if the belief was reasonable; the agency will argue the same reasonableness zone should defeat a criminal-activity predicate for certification.
3. The absence of any published case holding a Mercer-unlawful DUI arrest *is* a § 236/§ 146 offence — the petitioner is asking the court to extend Mercer from licence law to criminal-activity characterisation, and must say so openly.

## Q3 — Traditional mandate: what the court may do, and the standard of review

### Statutory framework (CCP §§ 1085, 1086, 1089, 1090, 1091, 1094) — index, Justia / California Public Law
- § 1085(a): "A writ of mandate may be issued by any court to any inferior tribunal, corporation, board, or person, to compel the performance of an act which the law specially enjoins, as a duty resulting from an office, trust, or station, or to compel the admission of a party to the use and enjoyment of a right or office to which the party is entitled, and from which the party is unlawfully precluded…" (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-3/title-1/chapter-2/section-1085/).
- § 1086: "The writ must be issued in all cases where there is not a plain, speedy, and adequate remedy, in the ordinary course of law. It must be issued upon the verified petition of the party beneficially interested." (https://www.casemine.com/act/us/6438e9f1d0293739de160ddc).
- § 1089: on the return date, the party served "may make a return by demurrer, verified answer or both" (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2021/code-ccp/part-3/title-1/chapter-2/section-1089/). **Note on terminology:** the "verified return" in § 1089 is the respondent's *pleading* (verified answer) to the petition — not, without more, a post-judgment compliance return. A post-judgment compliance return / retained jurisdiction must be built into the *judgment* (see (vi) below); do not cite § 1089 as if it itself authorised a compliance return.
- § 1090: "If a return be made, which raises a question as to a matter of fact essential to the determination of the motion, and affecting the substantial rights of the parties, and upon the supposed truth of the allegation of which the application for the writ is based, the court may, in its discretion, order the question to be tried before a jury, and postpone the argument until such trial can be had, and the verdict certified to the court. The question to be tried must be distinctly stated…" (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2020/code-ccp/part-3/title-1/chapter-2/section-1090/). Jury trial is discretionary and rare; the practical route to fact-finding is (v) below.
- § 1091: "On the trial, the applicant is not precluded by the return from any valid objection to its sufficiency, and may countervail it by proof either in direct denial or by way of avoidance." (https://california.public.law/codes/code_of_civil_procedure_section_1091). This is the textual basis for the petitioner putting on evidence to contradict the return — it is *not* a free-standing right to a full trial de novo, and it is qualified by Western States for quasi-legislative decisions (see below).
- § 1094: "If no return be made, the case may be heard on the papers of the applicant. If the return raises only questions of law, or puts in issue immaterial statements…, the court must proceed to hear or fix a day for hearing…" and a § 1088.5 petition presenting no triable issue of fact or based solely on an administrative record "may be determined … by noticed motion … for a judgment on the peremptory writ." (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-3/title-1/chapter-2/section-1094/).
- § 1094.5 is *administrative* mandate, not traditional mandate. It applies only where a hearing was required by law, evidence was required to be taken, and discretion in fact-determination was vested in the agency. A § 679.10 certification denial involves no statutorily required evidentiary hearing, so the petition is properly under § 1085, not § 1094.5 — but § 1094.5(b), (e), (f) language is still useful *by analogy* for abuse-of-discretion and remand-for-reconsideration framing (see below), and the petition should explain why § 1085, not § 1094.5, is the vehicle, to pre-empt a misclassification attack. § 1094.5(f) itself states the limiting principle: on set-aside, the court "may order the reconsideration of the case in light of the court's opinion and judgment and may order respondent to take such further action as is specially enjoined upon it by law, **but the judgment shall not limit or control in any way the discretion legally vested in the respondent**" (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-3/title-1/chapter-2/section-1094-5/). That proviso is the agency's best textual argument against *any* order that dictates outcome on remand — the controlled remand must therefore be drafted as legal rules + issues + findings to be made, not as a disguised direction to certify (see Q7).

### (i) Compel a mandatory duty
Common Cause, 49 Cal.3d at pp. 442–443 (verified live): "Mandamus will lie to compel a public official to perform an official act required by law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1085.) Mandamus will not lie to control an exercise of discretion, i.e., to compel an official to exercise discretion in a particular manner. Mandamus may issue, however, to compel an official both to exercise his discretion (if he is required by law to do so) and to exercise it under a proper interpretation of the applicable law." (Citing Shepherd v. Superior Court (1976) 17 Cal.3d 107, 118; Anderson v. Phillips (1975) 13 Cal.3d 733, 737.) And: "mandamus will lie to correct an abuse of discretion by an official acting in an administrative capacity" (id. at p. 442, noting the dissent). CCSO, 96 Cal.App.4th at p. 827 (verified live): "Two basic requirements are essential to the issuance of the writ: (1) A clear, present and usually ministerial duty upon the part of the respondent; and (2) a clear, present and beneficial right in the petitioner to the performance of that duty" (quoting People ex rel. Younger v. County of El Dorado (1971) 5 Cal.3d 480, 490–491); "the writ will not lie to control discretion conferred upon a public officer or agency."

### (ii) Order reconsideration under specified legal rules
This is the *core* Saleeby remedy, and the safest. Saleeby, 39 Cal.3d at pp. 566–568, 575 (verified live): the State Bar's CSF procedures were deficient because they denied applicants an opportunity to be heard and "sufficient findings to afford review"; findings must be "sufficient to apprise interested parties and the courts of the bases for the administrative action" (quoting San Francisco Ecology Center and Topanga Assn. v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 515–517 — findings "conduce the administrative body to draw legally relevant sub-conclusions supportive of its ultimate decision" and "minimize the likelihood that the agency will randomly leap from evidence to conclusions"). The Court *declined* to decide whether the award was improperly decided "because we have no basis on which to evaluate the bar's determination. Instead, we remand petitioner's application to the bar for such further consideration as may be appropriate following reformulation of the bar rules. We express no view on the merits of petitioner's claim" (id. at p. 568). The peremptory writ commanded the Bar to (1) reformulate rules to conform to the procedural requirements described, (2) afford petitioner a reasonable opportunity to be heard, and (3) provide sufficient findings (id. at p. 575). *Direct application:* a § 679.10 denial that is conclusory, applies the wrong helpfulness/qualifying-crime standard, or omits (i)(2) specifics is a Saleeby denial — vacate, remand for reconsideration under the correct (g)/(h)/(k) rules, with findings, expressing no view on the ultimate certification outcome unless the (iv) test is met.

### (iii) Limit issues / findings on remand
Common Cause at p. 446 (verified live) and Saleeby's rule-reformulation order both support issue-limiting: the court states the governing law and the questions the agency must answer, and the agency decides them. The § 1094.5(f) proviso (above) is the boundary: the court may "order reconsideration … in light of the court's opinion and judgment" but may not "limit or control … the discretion legally vested." In traditional mandate, the equivalent boundary is Common Cause's rule that the court "should not require a particular remedy unless the respective governmental entity has no discretion to exercise … or unless … it fails to exercise that discretion after being given a reasonable opportunity to do so" (id. at p. 446). A remand order that *forbids* reliance on a prohibited ground (e.g. (k)(3) grounds, or a legally erroneous probable-cause theory) is not "controlling discretion" — it is stating the law the discretion must be exercised *under*, which Common Cause expressly permits ("exercise it under a proper interpretation of the applicable law," id. at pp. 442–443). A remand order that *requires* a factual finding ("you must find no probable cause") *is* controlling discretion unless (iv) is satisfied.

### (iv) Order a particular result where only one lawful outcome remains
Two authorities, both to be cited together because neither alone is enough:
- Common Cause at p. 446 (verified live): a court "should not require a particular remedy unless the respective governmental entity has no discretion to exercise (as when the particular duty is made ministerial either by statute or by constitutional compulsion) … or unless, as in Reinecke, it fails to exercise that discretion after being given a reasonable opportunity to do so." *Note the posture:* Common Cause *reversed* an order requiring a particular (discretionary) programme — it is authority *against* direct certification where discretion remains, and *for* it only in the "no discretion" / "failed after reasonable opportunity" branches.
- CCSO at p. 827 (verified live): "Where a statute leaves room for discretion, a challenger must show the official acted arbitrarily, beyond the bounds of reason or in derogation of the applicable legal standards. … **Where only one choice can be a reasonable exercise of discretion, a court may compel an official to make that choice.** (Bank of Italy v. Johnson (1926) 200 Cal. 1, 31; Ferrill v. Ellis (1942) 50 Cal.App.2d 743, 746.)" This is the petitioner's best direct-certification sentence in California law. It must be proved, not asserted: on the facts *as found* (not as alleged), no reasonable official applying the correct (g)/(h)/(k) standards could deny. If any reasonable view of the probable-cause / helpfulness facts supports denial, (iv) fails and the remedy collapses to (ii).

### (v) Make factual findings / hold a focused evidentiary hearing
- Text: §§ 1090 (discretionary jury trial on an essential fact question, distinctly stated) and 1091 (petitioner may countervail the return by proof). Western States qualifies this for *quasi-legislative* decisions (see below), but expressly preserves it for *ministerial or informal administrative actions*: "we will continue to allow admission of extra-record evidence in traditional mandamus actions challenging ministerial or informal administrative actions if the facts are in dispute" (9 Cal.4th at p. 576, verified live; see also id. at fn. 6 and the No Oil / Lassen discussion at pp. 568–569). A § 679.10 certification decision is an *individual, informal* determination, not a quasi-legislative rulemaking — so the Western States bar for quasi-legislative decisions does not, by its own terms, govern it. The petition should make that classification argument expressly, and seek a *focused* hearing (declarations + the agency record + authentication of the recording/transcript, if needed) limited to the facts essential to the (g) predicates and to whether the denial rested on inaccurate facts.
- *Credibility:* a writ court *can* resolve credibility where it takes evidence / makes findings — that is what §§ 1090–1091 and the "findings supported by substantial evidence" review presuppose — but it may not simply disbelieve the agency's return on papers alone and substitute its own credibility finding without an evidentiary basis. The agency's "writ courts cannot resolve credibility disputes" argument is therefore *overbroad* as stated, but *correct* in its narrow form: on a pure paper record with conflicting declarations and no hearing, the court should either hold the focused hearing or remand for agency fact-finding, rather than resolving a swearing contest by assertion. See Q6.
- *Standard of review on appeal (and, by reflection, in the trial court):* CCSO at p. 827 (verified live): "the appellate court is ordinarily confined to an inquiry as to whether the findings and judgment of the trial court are supported by substantial evidence. However, the appellate court may make its own determination when the case involves resolution of questions of law where the facts are undisputed" (quoting Saathoff v. City of San Diego (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 697, 700). For traditional mandate generally: trial-court findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence; statutory interpretation and application of law to undisputed facts are reviewed de novo (index: CV Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 279–280, summarising this rule). For *administrative* mandate (§ 1094.5) — not this case, but the agency may invoke it — abuse of discretion is established if the respondent "has not proceeded in the manner required by law," the decision is not supported by findings, or findings are not supported by evidence (§ 1094.5(b), index).

### (vi) Retain jurisdiction and require a verified return (compliance)
**No CCP section verified in this research itself authorises a post-judgment "verified compliance return" in traditional mandate.** § 1089's "verified answer" is the return *to the petition*. What *is* supported: (a) Saleeby reserved jurisdiction — "Jurisdiction is reserved for the purpose of awarding attorney fees as may be hereinafter determined" (39 Cal.3d at p. 575, verified live) — showing a writ court may reserve jurisdiction for a defined post-judgment purpose; (b) a peremptory writ is enforceable by contempt / further order if disobeyed (general writ-enforcement principle; no specific CCP section verified here — do not cite § 1097 without checking it, as it was not verified in this session). *Drafting solution:* ask the judgment to (1) set a date certain for the agency's new decision, (2) require the agency to file and serve a *verified statement of compliance* (the new decision + the Supplement B or the new (i)(2) denial + the record relied on) by that date, (3) expressly retain jurisdiction to determine compliance and to entertain a motion to enforce / for contempt, and (4) state that the retention is for compliance only and does not authorise the court to direct the remand outcome. Present this as an exercise of the court's inherent power to enforce its writ, by analogy to Saleeby's reservation — not as a right conferred by § 1089.

### Additional directly applicable authority on abuse of discretion / inaccurate facts / failure to consider evidence / wrong legal standard
- **CCSO, p. 827** (verified live): discretion abused if the official acted "arbitrarily, beyond the bounds of reason or in derogation of the applicable legal standards." A denial that applies the federal "discretionary" standard instead of § 679.10(g)/(h)/(k), or that treats a (k)(3) ground as a reason to deny, is "in derogation of the applicable legal standards" on its face.
- **Common Cause, pp. 442–443** (verified live): mandamus to compel exercise of discretion "under a proper interpretation of the applicable law" — failure to apply the correct legal standard is itself a ground for the writ, without proving the ultimate outcome.
- **Citizens Assn. for Sensible Development of Bishop Area v. County of Inyo (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 151** (index: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/172/151.html): "if it is clear that considerable evidence was presented and that the evidence was not properly considered by the lead agency the reviewing court may find an abuse of discretion" (citing No Oil, 13 Cal.3d at p. 88; Brentwood Assn. v. City of Los Angeles (1983) 134 Cal.App.3d 504–505 — "an agency may not refuse to consider uncontradicted testimony based upon objective data"). *Application:* a denial that ignores the victim's submitted evidence of helpfulness / lack of refusal, or that rests on a single report while ignoring a contradictory transcript/recording that was before the agency, is vulnerable on this ground — provided the ignored evidence was actually *before* the agency or properly before the court under (v).
- **Saleeby / Topanga findings principle** (verified live, above): a decision without findings sufficient to show its mode of analysis cannot be meaningfully reviewed and may be vacated for that reason alone.
- **Ballard v. Anderson (1971) 4 Cal.3d 873, 884; Sego v. Santa Monica Rent Control Bd. (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 250, 255** (index, quoted in Ellena v. Dept. of Insurance (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 198, 206): "While mandamus will not lie to compel governmental officials to exercise their discretionary powers in a particular manner, it will lie to compel them to exercise them in some manner." Useful where the agency has simply refused to decide, or has decided on an impermissible ground, rather than exercising the (g) discretion at all.
- **Materially inaccurate facts:** no single California mandate case found states a freestanding "materially inaccurate fact = abuse of discretion" rule in those words. The argument must be built from the above: a finding with *no* substantial evidentiary support, or a decision that "randomly leap[s] from evidence to conclusions" (Topanga, via Saleeby), or that rests on a fact the record affirmatively disproves, is arbitrary / beyond the bounds of reason under CCSO. A *single* discrepancy that does not change the outcome is immaterial — see Q6. The petition must therefore tie each alleged inaccuracy to a *predicate* (probable cause, helpfulness, qualifying crime) and show the denial *depends* on it, not merely that the record contains an inconsistency somewhere.

### Western States — extra-record evidence, stated precisely (binding, Supreme Court)
Western States Petroleum Assn. v. Superior Court (1995) 9 Cal.4th 559 (verified live):
- *Holding (quasi-legislative):* courts "generally may not consider evidence not contained in the administrative record when reviewing the substantiality of the evidence supporting a quasi-legislative administrative decision" under Pub. Resources Code § 21168.5 (id. at p. 565), and extra-record evidence is "generally not admissible" to show the agency "has not proceeded in a manner required by law" in making a quasi-legislative decision (id. at pp. 565, 575–576).
- *Preserved for ministerial/informal actions:* "extra-record evidence is generally not admissible in traditional mandamus actions challenging quasi-legislative administrative decisions" — but "we will continue to allow admission of extra-record evidence in traditional mandamus actions challenging ministerial or informal administrative actions if the facts are in dispute" (id. at p. 576).
- *Narrow exception (quasi-legislative):* extra-record evidence "only in those rare instances in which (1) the evidence in question existed before the agency made its decision, and (2) it was not possible in the exercise of reasonable diligence to present this evidence to the agency before the decision was made" (id. at p. 578). The Court "do[es] not foreclose" admission "under unusual circumstances or for very limited purposes," e.g. background information or to ascertain whether the agency considered all relevant factors / fully explicated its grounds (id. at pp. 578–579, citing Asarco), but "extra-record evidence can never be admitted merely to contradict the evidence the administrative agency relied on in making a quasi-legislative decision or to raise a question regarding the wisdom of that decision" (id. at p. 579).
- *Application to § 679.10:* the denial is not a quasi-legislative rulemaking; it is an individual, informal determination with (on these facts) little or no formal administrative record. The petition's extra-record argument should therefore be framed as (a) this is a ministerial/informal action within the p. 576 preservation, *and* (b) in the alternative, the evidence (recording/transcript, blood result, dispatch log) existed before the denial and could not with reasonable diligence have been presented to the agency if the agency never gave the victim an opportunity to be heard / never disclosed its basis — a Saleeby-style procedural defect that itself justifies remand for consideration of the evidence. Do *not* cite Western States as if it generally *permits* extra-record evidence in traditional mandate; it does the opposite for quasi-legislative decisions, and the agency will quote pp. 565, 575–576 back.

## Q4 — Probable cause / DUI-arrest issues (certification-relevant only, not a suppression memo)

### (a) Vehicle Code § 40300.5 — the in-presence exceptions (leginfo, index)
"In addition to [Pen. Code § 836(a)(1)], a peace officer may, without a warrant, arrest a person when the officer has reasonable cause to believe that the person had been driving while under the influence … when any of the following exists: (a) The person is involved in a traffic crash. (b) The person is observed in or about a vehicle that is obstructing a roadway. (c) The person will not be apprehended unless immediately arrested. (d) The person may cause injury to themselves or damage property unless immediately arrested. (e) The person may destroy or conceal evidence of the crime unless immediately arrested." Veh. Code § 40300.5 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=40300.5.&lawCode=VEH). § 40300.6 requires § 40300.5 to be "liberally interpreted" to permit arrests "within a reasonable time and distance away from the scene of a traffic accident" (index). *Key:* § 40300.5 still requires "reasonable cause to believe [the person] had been driving while under the influence" — it excuses *presence*, not *probable cause*.

### (b) Mercer — "driving" = volitional movement (binding, Supreme Court; pincite index-verified, re-check before filing)
Mercer v. DMV (1991) 53 Cal.3d 753: § 23152 "requires proof of volitional movement of a vehicle"; "Because Penal Code section 836, subdivision 1, provides that a warrantless misdemeanor arrest is permissible only if a public offense occurs in the arresting officer's 'presence,' and because the officer in this case did not see Mercer's vehicle move, we conclude Mercer was not 'lawfully arrested' for a violation of section 23152(a)" for licence-revocation purposes (id. at pp. 769–770, as reproduced in search results; https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/mercer-v-department-motor-vehicles-31352). Mercer also states: "We do not hold that observed movement of a vehicle is necessary to support a conviction of drunk driving under 23152" (index) — i.e. *conviction* may rest on circumstantial proof of past volitional movement, while *in-presence arrest* under § 836(a)(1) requires observed movement. That distinction is the whole Q4 battleground: the agency conflates "could a jury find driving circumstantially?" with "was this a lawful in-presence arrest?" — they are different questions with different answers.

### (c) Factor-by-factor probable-cause analysis for the mission's facts
The standard is totality, judged objectively on facts known at arrest (Devenpeck, above; Espinoza, above). No single factor below is dispositive; the court will weigh them together, and the petitioner must win on *both* prongs: (1) no probable cause to believe DUI, *or* (2) probable cause but no § 40300.5 trigger (if the DUI is a misdemeanour and § 836(a)(1) presence is lacking).

| Fact | Helps petitioner (no PC / no lawful arrest) | Helps agency (PC / § 40300.5) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Found asleep in a stationary vehicle on a freeway shoulder, no observed movement | Mercer: no observed volitional movement → no § 836(a)(1) in-presence arrest. Sleep alone is not driving. | Being alone in the driver's seat of a vehicle on a freeway shoulder, engine/position suggesting recent arrival, supports an inference of recent driving (circumstantial-driving cases, index). | **Genuinely contested.** Sleep + stationary position defeats *presence* but does not by itself defeat *circumstantial* reasonable cause to believe the person *had been* driving. The fight is over § 40300.5, not over whether circumstantial driving proof exists at all. |
| Admission of earlier driving from one city toward another | — | An admission of driving is direct evidence of the driving element, and, combined with signs of intoxication, is classic probable-cause material. If the admission was made *before* arrest and is proved (recording/transcript authenticated), it is the agency's single strongest fact. | **Strong for agency on driving**, *if* the admission is authenticated and was pre-arrest. It does not by itself prove the person was *under the influence while driving* (as opposed to drinking after stopping) — timing matters, and the petitioner should press timing / sequence, not deny that admissions count. |
| Empty alcohol container in vehicle | An empty container proves at most past drinking, not intoxication while driving, and not driving at all. | Visible container + other signs supports an inference of drinking-and-driving and is routinely cited as PC material (index). | **Weak-to-moderate for agency, cumulative only.** Alone, insufficient; combined with admission + impairment signs, it adds weight. Do not argue it is legally irrelevant — argue it is not, without more, probable cause of *under-the-influence driving*. |
| Cup identified as urine | No verified California authority was found treating a cup of urine (or its identification as such) as evidence of DUI or of impairment. It does not show alcohol/drug influence, driving, or timing. | Agency may argue it shows the person was incapacitated / unable to care for self, supporting (d) (risk if not arrested) or general impairment — but that is speculation without a foundation linking urine in a cup to intoxication. | **Weak for both; do not over-invest.** Best petitioner use: it shows the arrest narrative was padded with non-probative observations, which bears on whether the *stated* PC basis was pretextual / inaccurate — but only if the denial actually relied on it. |
| Odor / speech observations *not communicated* to the arresting officer before arrest | PC is judged on "facts known to the arresting officer at the time of the arrest" (Devenpeck). Observations by another officer that were never communicated to the arresting officer, and were not the basis for a direction to arrest, cannot retroactively supply the arresting officer's PC. | Objective test + collective-knowledge / fellow-officer doctrine: if officers were acting as a team, or the observing officer communicated the observation (even briefly) before arrest, the agency will argue imputation. Also, the arresting officer's *own* odor/speech observations, if any, count in full. | **Turns entirely on the record: who observed what, when, and what was said to whom before arrest.** The petition must pin this down by declaration / dispatch / body-cam timeline; a bare assertion that observations were "uncommunicated" will not survive a contrary declaration. Do not cite a settled California "no imputation without communication" rule — none was verified in this research; frame it as Devenpeck's timing/knowledge requirement and force the agency to prove communication. |

Secondary commentary argues odour alone cannot be PC for arrest (index, San Diego DUI commentary) — but that is commentary, not a holding, and must not be cited as law. | Odour + slurred speech + admission + container, in totality, is routinely held to be PC (cf. Espinoza: odour + FST refusal reasonably interpreted as consciousness of guilt, index). | **Odour is a building block, not a conclusion.** The petitioner wins on odour only by showing it was the *only* impairment evidence *and* no other PC facts existed — a hard showing if admission + container are also present. |

**Freeway-shoulder / § 40300.5(b) point — flagged as unverified:** no California case found in this research holds that a vehicle lawfully stopped on a freeway *shoulder* is, or is not, "obstructing a roadway" under § 40300.5(b). "Roadway" is a defined term (Veh. Code § 530, not verified here) and a shoulder is arguably not part of the travelled roadway. The petition should plead (b) as *not established on this record* and force the agency to prove obstruction (position, lane encroachment, hazard), rather than asserting as law that a shoulder can never be obstruction. The agency's better § 40300.5 arguments are (e) (alcohol dissipates with time — see People v. Schofield (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 968, cited in search results, index: § 40300.5(e) creates an exception "because evidence could be destroyed by the simple passage of time") and, if the person was asleep at the wheel in a position to resume driving, (d). (c) ("will not be apprehended unless immediately arrested") is weak if the person was identified, stationary, and asleep — but the agency will argue flight risk once awakened. Each trigger must be proved with pre-arrest facts, not post hoc rationalisation.

### (d) Later-acquired evidence — Sanders, blood, warrant, dispatch parole information
**People v. Sanders (2003) 31 Cal.4th 318 (binding, Supreme Court; holding verified via later-case quotations, index):** "an otherwise unlawful search of the residence of an adult parolee may not be justified by the circumstance that the suspect was subject to a search condition of which the law enforcement officers were unaware when the search was conducted" (id. at p. 335); "such a search cannot be justified as a parole search, because the officer is not acting pursuant to the conditions of parole" (id. at p. 333). *Scope limit — state it openly:* Sanders is a *search* case about an unknown parole/probation search condition, not an *arrest* case about probable cause. Its value here is *analogical + timing*: justification is judged by what the officer knew and acted pursuant to *at the time*, not by facts discovered later. It does not, by itself, hold that a later blood result or a later warrant can never be *evidence* of what happened earlier — only that later facts cannot retroactively *supply the legal justification* that was missing at the time.

Applied factor by factor:
- **Post-arrest blood result:** cannot create pre-arrest probable cause (Devenpeck timing). It *can* be circumstantial evidence that the person was in fact intoxicated around the time of arrest, *if* timing is bridged by expert retrograde extrapolation (cf. Jackson, where the prosecution's expert opined on BAC at the earlier driving time from a later test, 189 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1465, 1468 — index/verified reproduction). A bare BAC number without timing, rising/falling analysis, or a foundation for the test's reliability proves little about PC at arrest and nothing about driving. Conversely, a *low / zero* later BAC does not by itself prove there was no PC — PC requires only a probability, judged on pre-arrest facts, and an officer may reasonably (if mistakenly) believe impairment existed.
- **Warrant obtained after arrest:** a warrant obtained after the arrest cannot retroactively make the warrantless arrest lawful; the arrest's lawfulness is judged at arrest. (General Fourth Amendment timing principle, Devenpeck; no California case on a post-arrest warrant curing a DUI arrest was verified — plead the principle, not a invented holding.)
- **Dispatch parole information learned after arrest:** Sanders analogy is strongest here — a parole/probation status of which the arresting officer was unaware at arrest cannot be invoked after the fact to justify the seizure/search. *Caveat:* if dispatch information was received *before* arrest (even minutes before) and communicated to the arresting officer, it counts; if it was received after, it does not. And parole status, without more, is not probable cause of DUI — it is, at most, a search-condition justification, which is a different legal basis that must have been known and acted on.

### (e) Voluntary PAS refusal — Vehicle Code § 23612(h)–(i) and People v. Jackson
**Statute (index, Justia reproduction):** § 23612(h) defines a PAS test as a field sobriety test that "may be used by an officer as a further investigative tool" to establish reasonable cause; § 23612(i) requires the officer to advise the person of the right to refuse the PAS test, and that the post-arrest chemical-test obligation is not satisfied by a PAS test (https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2012/veh/division-11.5/chapter-4/article-1/section-23612/).
**Jackson (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1461 (binding Court of Appeal; verified live reproduction), holding at pp. 1466–1469:** because § 23612 confers a statutory right to refuse the PAS test and requires the officer to advise of it, "it makes little sense to grant a right of refusal yet allow the prosecution to admit evidence of that refusal to establish consciousness of guilt" — admission at *trial* as consciousness of guilt was error (though harmless on Jackson's strong facts, including erratic driving, FST failures, and a 0.10% breath result). Jackson *distinguishes* standard field-sobriety-test refusal, which *is* routinely admitted as consciousness of guilt (Marvin v. DMV (1984) 161 Cal.App.3d 717, 719–720, cited in Jackson at p. 1467; Espinoza, above, repeats the distinction at length).
**Scope — the point the mission asks for, stated precisely:** Jackson is a *trial-evidence* holding. It does *not* hold that an officer may not *consider* a PAS refusal in the pre-arrest totality, nor that a PAS refusal is legally PC-neutral, nor that a PAS refusal can never contribute to reasonable cause. What Jackson *does* support, by its reasoning, is the narrower argument: (1) the Legislature created a right to refuse and required advisement of it, so treating the exercise of that right as inculpatory *defeats the right* — a court should be sceptical of a PC theory in which PAS refusal is the *central or sole* inculpatory fact; (2) PAS refusal must not be conflated with FST refusal (Marvin/Espinoza) — if the agency's PC narrative cites "refusal to cooperate with testing" without distinguishing PAS (protected) from FST/chemical-test refusal (not protected in the same way), the narrative is legally inaccurate; (3) if the officer never gave the § 23612(i) advisement, the refusal was not a knowing exercise of the statutory right at all, and its inculpatory weight is further diminished. The agency's response — that PC is an objective totality test and an officer may consider conduct (including refusal) as one factor, and that Jackson's harmless-error affirmance shows the refusal was not outcome-determinative even at trial — is credible where other PC facts (admission, odour, FST performance) exist. **Do not cite Jackson as a probable-cause bar; cite it as a reason the refusal cannot carry the PC burden alone and cannot be used to punish the exercise of a statutory right.**

### (f) Weight of a later blood-alcohol result and of §§ 849.5 / 851.6 detention certificate
- **Later BAC:** see (d) above. It is evidence of intoxication at test time, not a retroactive PC finding, not proof of driving, and not proof of lawfulness of the arrest. Its weight depends entirely on timing + extrapolation + test foundation. A high BAC helps the agency on the *factual* question "was this person in fact under the influence?" but does not answer the *legal* question "did the officer have probable cause *and* a § 40300.5 trigger *before* arrest?" — and for the § 236/§ 146 predicate, the legal question is the one that matters.
- **Detention certificate (Pen. Code §§ 849.5, 851.6) — index, secondary summaries:** when a person is arrested and released without an accusatory pleading being filed, the event "shall not be deemed an arrest, but a detention only" (§ 849.5), and the agency must issue a certificate describing the action as a detention (§ 851.6) and remove arrest references from its records. (Index: https://legalclarity.org/what-is-a-detention-certificate-in-california/; verify exact text on leginfo before filing.) **What it does and does not prove:** it proves *no charges were filed* and that the record must be treated as a detention for disclosure/record purposes. It does *not* adjudicate probable cause, does *not* prove the arrest was unlawful, does *not* prove false imprisonment, and does *not* erase the fact of the detention itself. The petitioner may use it to show the DUI investigation ended without prosecution (consistent with § 679.10(k)(1) — prosecution is not required) and to rebut any agency claim that "charges prove a crime occurred"; the agency may use it to argue the opposite — that release without charges reflects prosecutorial discretion / insufficient proof for *charging*, not absence of PC for *arrest*. Neither inference is compelled. Do not plead the certificate as a finding of unlawful arrest.

## Q5 — Federal U-visa framework

### Statute and regulation (verified live for the regulation; statute via regulation cross-reference)
- **8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(U)** (INA § 101(a)(15)(U)): creates U nonimmigrant status for victims of qualifying criminal activity (listed at § 1101(a)(15)(U)(iii), including "any similar activity") who suffered substantial physical or mental abuse, possess information about the crime, and have been / are being / are likely to be helpful to law enforcement in investigation or prosecution. The petition must contain a certification under 8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(1) (INA § 214(p)(1)).
- **8 C.F.R. § 214.14** (verified live: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/214.14):
  - (a)(2) Certifying agency = federal/state/local law enforcement agency, prosecutor, judge, or other authority with responsibility for investigation or prosecution of a qualifying crime (including agencies with criminal investigative jurisdiction in their area of expertise).
  - (a)(9) Qualifying crime/activity = the enumerated list "or any similar activities in violation of Federal, State or local criminal law," including unlawful criminal restraint, false imprisonment, and attempt/conspiracy/solicitation; "'any similar activity' refers to criminal offenses in which the nature and elements of the offenses are substantially similar to the statutorily enumerated list."
  - (a)(12) Supplement B "confirms that the petitioner has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful in the investigation or prosecution of the qualifying criminal activity of which he or she is a victim."
  - (a)(14) Victim = "an alien who has suffered direct and proximate harm as a result of the commission of qualifying criminal activity" (with indirect-victim provisions for deceased/incompetent/incapacitated victims, and an exclusion for a person culpable for the qualifying activity, (a)(14)(iii)).
  - (b) Eligibility: (1) substantial physical/mental abuse as a result of being a victim; (2) possesses credible/reliable information / knowledge of details; (3) has been / is being / is likely to be helpful *and* since initiating cooperation "has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested"; (4) activity occurred in the U.S. / territories / etc. or violated U.S. law with extraterritorial jurisdiction.
  - (c)(1) "USCIS has sole jurisdiction over all petitions for U nonimmigrant status."
  - (c)(2)(i) The petition must include a Supplement B signed within the six months preceding filing, stating: the signer is the agency head / designated supervisor / judge; the agency has responsibility for detection, investigation, prosecution, conviction, or sentencing of qualifying activity; **"the applicant has been a victim of qualifying criminal activity that the certifying official's agency is investigating or prosecuting"**; the petitioner possesses information about it; the petitioner has been / is being / is likely to be helpful; and the activity violated U.S. law or occurred in the U.S./territories/etc.

### Does certification require a conviction, prosecution, or ongoing investigation?
**No — under either federal or California law.** Federal text requires only that the agency *is investigating or prosecuting* the activity in the Supplement B attestation, and California § 679.10(k)(1) expressly provides that a current investigation, charges, apprehension, closing, prosecution, or conviction are *not* required. USCIS guidance is to the same effect: certification may issue at various stages, including before charges. The agency's contrary argument must therefore be framed not as "no prosecution = no certification" (which (k)(1) forecloses) but as "no investigation *of the qualifying activity* by *this* agency = no truthful Supplement B attestation" — see the structural problem below.

### Does the certifying agency determine qualifying criminal activity, or merely helpfulness?
**Both — but USCIS decides ultimate eligibility.** The Supplement B attests to *both* victimhood ("has been a victim of qualifying criminal activity that the agency is investigating or prosecuting") *and* helpfulness. USCIS guidance states: "USCIS recognizes that certifying agencies and officials may be in the best position to determine if a qualifying crime has taken place. If, in the normal course of duties, a certifying official or agency has determined that a qualifying criminal activity has taken place, the victim possessed information …, and the victim has been helpful, law enforcement may sign" and "Whether a certifying agency or official signs the certification is under the authority of the agency or official" (index: USCIS U Visa Law Enforcement Resource Guide, https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/guides/U_Visa_Law_Enforcement_Resource_Guide.pdf; same language in the U and T Visa Resource Guide, index). USCIS then "determines, in its sole discretion, the credibility of and weight given to all of the evidence, including the Supplement B" (8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(4); 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(4) — index via AAO decisions). So: the agency's determination is *necessary* (no Supplement B, no petition) but not *sufficient* (USCIS may still deny), and USCIS may disagree with the agency's characterisation on other evidence. A state writ court that orders certification is therefore not ordering USCIS to grant status — a point the petition should state expressly to defuse pre-emption / separation-of-powers objections.

### "Victim" and "substantially similar" — federal meaning
Victim = direct and proximate harm from the qualifying activity (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(14), verified live); California § 679.10(o)(2) is to the same effect for direct victims, plus indirect and bystander/witness categories (above). "Substantially similar" = nature *and* elements substantially similar to an enumerated crime (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(9), verified live; § 679.10(d) identical in substance). For § 146(a): the comparison is — unlawful restraint/detention by a person acting under color of authority without lawful authority (nature: deprivation of liberty by state actor; elements: officer status + color + arrest/detention + absence of lawful authority) vs. false imprisonment (nature: unlawful deprivation of liberty; elements: intentional unlawful restraint + compelled stay/go). The overlap is real but not identical: § 146 adds officer/color elements and drops the "against will / compelled" phrasing in favour of "against his or her will" in (a) itself. Plead the element-by-element comparison; do not assert similarity by label.

### May a state court determine qualifying activity for certification purposes?
**In two distinct senses — yes for mandate review, no (or not verified) for granting status:**
1. *Mandate review:* a superior court hearing a § 1085 petition necessarily determines, as a predicate to deciding whether the agency proceeded as required by law, whether the agency's qualifying-activity determination was legally erroneous, unsupported, or based on prohibited grounds. That is ordinary judicial review, not an immigration adjudication, and it does not bind USCIS.
2. *Judge as certifier:* a judge *is* a certifying entity/official under both 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(2)–(3) and Pen. Code § 679.10(a)(3), (b)(3). In theory, a judge who has determined victimhood/helpfulness in a proceeding could sign a Supplement B. **But** the Supplement B attestation requires that the qualifying activity be one "the certifying official's agency is investigating or prosecuting" (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(2)(i)) — a writ judge did not investigate or prosecute the officer's conduct, and a judge-signed certification in a writ case where the judge is also the adjudicator raises impartiality / attestation problems that no authority found addresses. The petition should therefore **not** ask the writ judge to sign the Supplement B as an alternative remedy without briefing this attestation problem; the cleaner alternative prayer is an order directing the *agency's designated official* to sign, if (iv) is met.
3. *Structural problem specific to officer-as-perpetrator cases:* the agency investigated a *DUI by the victim*, not a *false imprisonment by its own officer*. A Supplement B signed by that same agency would attest that the agency is investigating/prosecuting *false imprisonment* — which, unless the agency has opened (or the court orders it to open, which the court cannot do — charging/investigative discretion) such an investigation, is factually awkward. The petitioner's best answer: (k)(1) (no current investigation required) + the agency *did* investigate the underlying incident (the stop/arrest) in the normal course, and the qualifying activity *arises from* that incident; the Supplement B's "investigating or prosecuting" language in the federal form is satisfied by the agency's investigation of the incident, with the crime characterised correctly. The agency's best answer is that this reads "investigating or prosecuting [the qualifying activity]" out of the form. **This is an unresolved tension in the authorities found; flag it as the case's central vulnerability and do not present either reading as settled.**

## Q7 — Remedy drafting

### (a) Direct certification — minimum findings (all required; absence of any one defeats direct relief)
1. **Standing / duty / no adequate remedy:** petitioner is the victim (or authorised requester under (g)(1)), made a proper request to the correct certifying entity/official, and has no plain, speedy, adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law (CCP § 1086) — e.g. no other certifying entity with responsibility for the incident, and USCIS cannot act without a Supplement B.
2. **Qualifying criminal activity — found, not alleged:** on facts found by the court after a focused hearing or on undisputed facts, the elements of false imprisonment (§ 236) *or* of an offence substantially similar to it / to unlawful criminal restraint (§ 679.10(d)), including § 146(a) if pleaded on that basis, are made out — specifically: intentional restraint/detention against will, *and* absence of lawful authority (no probable cause *or* no § 836 presence + no § 40300.5 trigger, each analysed separately).
3. **Victimhood:** petitioner suffered direct and proximate harm from that activity (§ 679.10(o)(2); 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(14)) — not merely harm from the DUI investigation or from being a DUI suspect.
4. **Helpfulness:** petitioner has been / is being / is likely to be helpful to detection/investigation/prosecution of *that* activity, with the (h) presumption applied and any claimed rebuttal (specific reasonable request + refusal/failure, with knowledge) found not proved.
5. **No prohibited ground:** the denial did not, and the new decision will not, rest on any § 679.10(k)(3) ground, on a requirement of prosecution/conviction/current investigation (k)(1)–(2), or on a belief about USCIS approval (k)(3)(D).
6. **Only-one-reasonable-choice:** on the found facts, *no* reasonable official applying the correct (g)/(h)/(k) standards could deny — the CCSO p. 827 test — *and* the agency has already had a reasonable opportunity to exercise its discretion (Common Cause p. 446, Reinecke branch).
7. **Truthful attestation possible:** the designated official can truthfully sign the Supplement B as the form requires, including that the agency investigated the incident / qualifying activity (address the Q5 attestation problem expressly; if it cannot be truthfully signed by the agency, direct relief against the agency is not available and the court should not order a false attestation).
8. **Scope limit:** the order directs *only* completion, signing, and forwarding of the Supplement B (with (i)(1) specific details) within the (j) period or a date certain; it does *not* direct USCIS to grant status, does not decide substantial abuse, admissibility, or any other USCIS eligibility element, and does not bind future certifications.

### (b) Vacatur + controlled remand — minimum findings (each finding supports vacatur; the remand order must track them)
1. **The denial is reviewable under § 1085** (not § 1094.5 — no statutorily required hearing) and petitioner is beneficially interested with no adequate alternative remedy.
2. **Legal error identified with particularity:** e.g. denial applied a "certification is wholly discretionary / requires prosecution or ongoing investigation" standard contrary to (g)/(h)/(k)(1); or treated a (k)(3) ground as a reason; or required the victim to produce a crime report contrary to (k)(3)(G); or failed to apply the (h) presumption / treated absence of a request as non-helpfulness.
3. **Factual / evidentiary defect identified with particularity:** denial rests on a materially inaccurate fact on which it depends (specify fact, record cite, and why outcome depends on it); *or* denial ignores considerable relevant evidence that was before the agency (specify evidence, Bishop Area principle); *or* denial contains no findings sufficient to show its mode of analysis (Saleeby/Topanga).
4. **Procedural defect, if any:** denial lacks (i)(2) specifics where non-cooperation was invoked; or victim was given no opportunity to be heard / to respond to the agency's proposed basis (Saleeby reasonable-opportunity principle); or (f) report / (j) deadline was breached.
5. **Remand directions (law, issues, findings — not outcome):** vacate the denial; direct reconsideration (i) under the correct (g)/(h)/(k) standards as stated in the judgment, (ii) on the specified issues (qualifying activity incl. substantially-similar analysis; victimhood; helpfulness with presumption; PC / § 40300.5 analysis if the qualifying-activity theory depends on it), (iii) with written findings sufficient to apprise the parties and the court of the bases, including (i)(2) specifics if denial is again on non-cooperation, and (iv) within a date certain (apply (j) periods by analogy).
6. **Compliance mechanism:** verified statement of compliance filed/served by the date certain (new decision + signed Supplement B or new written denial + record relied on); jurisdiction retained *solely* to determine compliance / enforce the writ; express statement that the court expresses no view on the remand outcome except as stated in the legal directions (Saleeby p. 568 model: "We express no view on the merits").

### Overreach flags — findings / orders to avoid
- **Directing a future discretionary outcome** beyond the (iv) showing (e.g. "on remand you must certify" while also claiming the agency retains discretion) — internally contradictory and reversible under Common Cause p. 446 / § 1094.5(f) proviso.
- **Deciding immigration status** — granting, or directing USCIS to grant, U status, or deciding substantial abuse, admissibility, waiver, or cap/waiting-list placement. USCIS has sole jurisdiction over petitions (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(1)).
- **Deciding dismissed / unpleaded claims** — e.g. adjudicating the officer's criminal guilt, civil false-arrest liability, or § 1983 liability as part of the writ judgment. The writ decides only whether the *certification denial* was lawful; criminal/civil liability for the arrest is a different proceeding with different burdens and parties.
- **Ordering an investigation or prosecution** of the officer — charging and investigative discretion is not a § 679.10 duty; (k)(1) says no investigation is required, it does not create a duty to investigate.
- **Ordering a false Supplement B attestation** — if the agency cannot truthfully attest it investigated/prosecuted the qualifying activity, the remedy is remand for a lawful characterisation / consideration, not an order to sign a false form.
- **Binding future certifications** for other victims, other incidents, or other officials — the writ binds the parties / respondent on this request.
- **Retaining jurisdiction to re-decide the remand outcome de novo** — retention should be for compliance only; a second merits bite invites the "controlling discretion" objection on the remand itself.

### California case ordering, or refusing to order, a U-visa certification directly — explicit answer
**None was found in this research.** Searches for California cases ordering an agency to sign a Supplement B under § 679.10, or holding a court could not do so, returned the statute, the Commission on State Mandates test-claim decision (which addresses *state-mandate reimbursement* for processing certifications, not writ relief), USCIS guidance, and federal discretion cases — but **no published California superior-court, Court of Appeal, or Supreme Court decision granting or denying direct certification relief under § 679.10**. The petition must therefore present direct certification as a *first-impression* application of Common Cause / CCSO principles to § 679.10, not as established California U-visa law, and must not imply such a case exists. The closest California analogues are Saleeby (remand for reconsideration + findings, *expressly* no view on merits, p. 568) and Common Cause (reversal of a particular-remedy order where discretion remained, p. 446) — both of which point *away* from direct relief unless the (iv) test is met, and *toward* controlled remand as the default.
