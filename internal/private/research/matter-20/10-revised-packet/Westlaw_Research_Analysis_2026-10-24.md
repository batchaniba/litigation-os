## 1. Executive summary — top takeaways

1. **The statute is the strongest authority, and it directly contradicts the denial.** Penal Code § 679.10, subd. (c)(19), lists **false imprisonment** as "qualifying criminal activity." Captain Serrato's April 20, 2026 letter said false imprisonment was "not qualifying criminal activity." That is a misreading of the governing statute on its face — a textbook "proper interpretation of the applicable law" error under *Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors* (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442. (Statute: READ RELEVANT PASSAGES through subd. (o)(1); no flag.)

2. **Subdivision (g)(1) is mandatory once its two predicates are met.** A certifying official "shall certify victim helpfulness" on Form I-918 Supplement B on request when the requester was a victim of qualifying criminal activity and "has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful." Subd. (h) adds a rebuttable presumption of helpfulness; subd. (i)(2) requires a written explanation of any denial; subd. (j) sets a 30-day deadline (7 days if in removal proceedings); subd. (k)(3) lists impermissible reasons for denial, including a belief that USCIS will not approve the petition.

3. **No California appellate decision construes § 679.10 certification or reviews a denial by writ.** Targeted Westlaw searches — "Immigrant Victims of Crime Equity Act" (0), "Penal Code section 679.10" (0), `"U visa" & mandamus & certif!` (0), `679.10 & certif!` (2, neither usable) — found nothing. The only California case citing the statute on certification (*People v. Agustin*, 2025 WL 3564125) is unpublished and red-flagged. **[Researcher's read]** Count 1 presents a question of first impression; the brief should say so candidly and build from the statute plus general mandamus law.

4. **The mandamus framework is solid and current.** Core: *Common Cause*, 49 Cal.3d at 442, and *Cal. Correctional Supervisors Org. v. Dept. of Corrections* (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 824, 827 ("Where only one choice can be a reasonable exercise of discretion, a court may compel an official to make that choice"; no negative treatment). Recent reaffirmations: *Crestwood Behavioral Health v. Baass* (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1, 15–16; *Alameda Health System v. ACERA* (2024) 100 Cal.App.5th 1159, 1177; *CV Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista* (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 279–280; *Water Audit California v. Merced Irrigation Dist.* (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1147, *1181.

5. **Remedy authority is better than the packet currently uses.** *CV Amalgamated* models both remedies in one opinion: a direct order to rescind and process (at 283) and an order to re-exercise discretion under the correct standard (at 286). *Saleeby v. State Bar* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547, 565–568, supports requiring findings sufficient for review and a remand "for such further consideration as may be appropriate."

6. **Qualifying-crime support is good on the law, thinner on fabricated evidence.** *Hagberg v. California Federal Bank* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 350, 372 fn. 7 & 372–373 (false arrest is one way to commit false imprisonment; tort and crime defined the same way); *Asgari v. City of Los Angeles* (1997) 15 Cal.4th 744, 752–753 (officers not immune for false arrest/imprisonment); Gov. Code § 820.4; Pen. Code § 847(b); *Schmidlin v. City of Palo Alto* (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 728, 777 (arrest without probable cause is unlawful). **No California case located expressly holds that an officer's arrest on fabricated evidence is false imprisonment.** Rely on § 679.10(c)(19), these authorities, and Judge Krishnan's June 29, 2026 screening order (persuasive only).

7. **Federal authority cuts both ways but does not control.** *Perez Perez v. Wolf*, 943 F.3d 853 (9th Cir. 2019) (majority READ IN FULL) holds the U-visa statutory framework supplies "meaningful standards" for review, including whether a crime is "qualifying criminal activity." *Chairez v. Mayorkas*, 168 F.4th 1227 (9th Cir. 2026) narrows review of discretionary judgment calls under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(2)(B)(ii) but distinguishes statutory threshold requirements and does not cite *Perez Perez*. **[Researcher's read]** Limits on *federal* courts reviewing *USCIS* do not govern *state* mandamus over a *state* officer's duty under a *state* statute — but expect CHP to cite *Ordonez Orosco*, *Mercado-Guillen*, *Trevino*, and *Chairez*.

8. **Remove or avoid:** *Vichy Springs* (the useful passages are in unpublished parts), *Gillan* (red flag), *Assn. of Deputy DAs v. Gascon* (red flag), *People v. Agustin* (unpublished, red flag), *Feah, LLC v. City of El Monte* (unpublished), and the *Sanders* (330–332) and *Mercer* (768–769) pinpoints.

9. **Candor points.** *CCSO* affirmed the *denial* of a writ; the *Crestwood* petitioner lost on the facts; *Alameda Health* applies deferential quasi-legislative review. Cite them for the standards, not for their results.

|---|---|---|

| (g)(1) | Certifying official "shall certify victim helpfulness" on Form I-918 Supp. B, on request, when the victim was a victim of qualifying criminal activity and "has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful." | The mandatory-duty hook. | (h) | Rebuttable presumption of helpfulness. | Shifts burden: the April 20 letter did not rely on lack of helpfulness. |

| (k)(3) | Impermissible reasons for denial include a belief that USCIS will not approve the petition. | Constrains any "discretion" CHP claims; useful against post-hoc rationalizations. |
| (k)(1) | (Per packet, earlier-verified) certification does not depend on charges being filed. | Answers "no one was charged" arguments. |

### 2.2 Analysis

**Mandatory duty.** The verb is "shall," and the duty attaches "when" the two predicates exist. **[Researcher's read]** Any discretion CHP retains is discretion to *decide the predicates under the correct statutory definitions* — not discretion to refuse once they are met, and not discretion to redefine "qualifying criminal activity." That framing fits *Common Cause*, 49 Cal.3d at 442 (mandamus may compel an official "to exercise it under a proper interpretation of the applicable law") and *CCSO*, 96 Cal.App.4th at 827 (relief where the official acted "in derogation of the applicable legal standards"; compel the only reasonable choice).

**The denial's stated ground.** The April 20 letter said false imprisonment is "not qualifying criminal activity." Subdivision (c)(19) says the opposite. **[Researcher's read]** On that ground alone the denial cannot stand: it rests on a misreading of the statute, which is reviewable de novo as legal error (cf. *Amalgamated Transit Union Local 276 v. San Joaquin RTD* (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 1, citing *CCSO* for de novo statutory interpretation — weak, use as "see" only). The remaining question is remedy: direct certification (only one lawful choice) or vacate and remand (lawful range remains).

**Where CHP will push back.** (1) The incident was a lawful DUI arrest, so no "victim" of false imprisonment exists — this is a factual dispute about the first predicate, not a defense of the letter's legal ground. (2) Federal regulation 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(2) and federal case law treat certification as discretionary. **[Researcher's read]** California's "shall certify ... when" displaces that for state officers; federal discretion cases address federal-court power over federal agencies.

**No case law construing § 679.10.** See Takeaway 3. Secondary sources located (snippets only; NOT read): 53 Cal. W. L. Rev. 149 (2017) (on SB 674); 22 San Joaquin Agric. L. Rev. 141 (2012–13) (titled in part "U-Visa Certification Requirement Is Blocking Congressional Intent Creating the Need for a Writ of Mandate…"). Do not quote either until read.
