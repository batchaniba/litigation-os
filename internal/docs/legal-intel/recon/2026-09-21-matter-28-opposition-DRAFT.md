## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendants ask this Court to dismiss a federal civil-rights complaint on two theories, and neither
survives their own exhibits. **First**, they say a July 15, 2026 state demurrer ruling bars everything
under res judicata. But the document they attach as Exhibit C is an *order sustaining a demurrer* — not
a judgment of dismissal — signed on a form defense counsel drafted. Under California law a ruling on a
demurrer is not preclusive until a judgment is entered, and none has been. Res judicata also fails
because the individual defendant here, Shannon Smith, **was never a party** to the state case, and
because the state proceeding could not, and did not, adjudicate the federal § 1983 and RICO claims — as
to which state administrative exhaustion is not even an element.

**Second**, defendants say the complaint pleads no viable claim. That argument depends on ignoring the
controlling authority: a private contractor who performs a function the State is obligated to provide
to persons in its custody or on its supervision is a state actor. *West v. Atkins*, 487 U.S. 42 (1988).
HOPE is the sole CASOMB-certified provider in the Stockton region; California law assigns *to HOPE's
certified professional* the determination of how long a parolee stays in treatment as a condition of
parole; and HOPE feeds that determination to CDCR on the Form 3043 pipeline that directly governs
plaintiff's liberty. Taking the complaint's allegations as true and construing this pro se pleading
liberally, plaintiff states claims. At minimum, any defect is curable, and leave to amend must be
granted.

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests only legal sufficiency. The Court "accept[s] as true" the complaint's
allegations and "construe[s] them in the light most favorable" to plaintiff. *Hishon v. King &
Spalding*, 467 U.S. 69, 73 (1984) (cited by defendants). A claim survives if it pleads "enough facts to
state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 570
(2007); *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009).

Two settled rules govern a pro se pleading and frame this entire motion:

- **Liberal construction.** A pro se complaint "must be held to less stringent standards than formal
  pleadings drafted by lawyers" and liberally construed. *Erickson v. Pardus*, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007);
  *Lopez v. Smith*, 203 F.3d 1122, 1130–31 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc).
- **Leave to amend is the default.** A pro se litigant must be given leave to amend "unless it is
  absolutely clear that the deficiencies of the complaint could not be cured by amendment." *Lopez*,
  203 F.3d at 1130–31; Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2); *United States v. Corinthian Colls.*, 655 F.3d 984,
  995 (9th Cir. 2011). Defendants seek dismissal **without leave to amend** — they carry the burden of
  showing amendment would be futile as to *each* count, and they do not carry it.

**Scope note (limits the RJN).** On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion the Court's review is confined to the
complaint, documents incorporated by reference, and matters judicially noticeable. *Khoja v. Orexigen
Therapeutics, Inc.*, 899 F.3d 988 (9th Cir. 2018). Plaintiff does not dispute that the Court may
notice the *existence and legal effect* of the state filings in Exhibits A–C; plaintiff **does** object
to any use of those documents for the *truth* of their factual assertions. *Id.* at 999.

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. Res Judicata Does Not Bar This Action

Defendants correctly state the elements: a preclusive prior action requires (1) an identical claim or
issue, (2) **a final judgment on the merits**, and (3) the same parties or their privies. *Boeken v.
Philip Morris USA, Inc.*, 48 Cal. 4th 788, 797 (2010); *Brodheim v. Cry*, 584 F.3d 1262, 1268 (9th Cir.
2009). Three independent defects defeat preclusion here. Any one is fatal.

**1. There is no final judgment.** Defendants' own Exhibit C is an "[Proposed] Order … [that] sustained
[the demurrer] without leave to amend." It is not a judgment of dismissal, and the packet contains
none. In California, "[u]ntil the entry of judgment, a ruling on a demurrer is not res judicata."
*Valvo v. Univ. of S. Cal.*, 67 Cal. App. 3d 887 (1977); accord *Berri v. Superior Court*, 43 Cal. 2d
856, 860 (1955) (an order sustaining a demurrer without leave "is not appealable as it is not the final
judgment"). A judgment of dismissal must be *separately entered*. Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 581d. Defendants
attach an order, not a § 581d judgment; the second preclusion element is unmet on the face of their
papers. *(Pre-filing: re-pull the San Joaquin register; if a § 581d judgment has since been entered,
this defect falls away but defects 2 and 3 are judgment-independent.)*

**2. Smith was never a party, and privity is not established.** The state defendants were HOPE
Psychotherapy, Inc., Katie Palacios, and Does — **not Shannon Smith**. The third element requires that
"the party against whom [preclusion] is being asserted was a party or in privity with a party." *Boeken*,
48 Cal. 4th at 797. Nonparty preclusion is "disfavored" and confined to narrow exceptions. *Taylor v.
Sturgell*, 553 U.S. 880, 892–95 (2008). Defendants invoke employer-employee privity, but the state
action adjudicated no facts about **Smith's personal direction** of the challenged statewide policy —
the gravamen of the individual-capacity § 1983 claim. Privity requires a shared, adequately-represented
interest on the very matter now asserted. *DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber*, 61 Cal. 4th 813, 824–26 (2015).

**3. The dismissal was not "on the merits," and it cannot reach the federal claims.** Even a general
demurrer "may not be on the merits, for the defects set up may be technical or formal, and the plaintiff
may in such case by a different pleading … allege facts constituting a good cause of action … [and] the
prior judgment on demurrer will not be a bar." *Goddard v. Sec. Title Ins. & Guar. Co.*, 14 Cal. 2d 47,
52–53 (1939). A dismissal resting on **prematurity** — a failure to satisfy a precondition to suit — is
not a merits adjudication and precludes nothing. *Perez v. City of San Bruno*, 27 Cal. 3d 875, 882–83
(1980); *Heath v. Cleary*, 708 F.2d 1376, 1380 & n.4 (9th Cir. 1983).

Two points make this decisive. **(a)** Defendants' own demurrer (Ex. B) argued the FAC failed "to allege
exhaustion" of administrative remedies — a curable, non-merits, prematurity defect that a repleading
cures. **(b)** In all events, exhaustion of state administrative remedies **is not a prerequisite to a
§ 1983 action**. *Patsy v. Bd. of Regents*, 457 U.S. 496, 516 (1982). The PLRA's exhaustion carve-out
does not apply, because a parolee at liberty is not a "prisoner": the statute reaches only a person
"incarcerated or detained in any facility." 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a), (h); *Talamantes v. Leyva*, 575 F.3d
1021, 1023–24 (9th Cir. 2009) (status measured at filing); *Page v. Torrey*, 201 F.3d 1136, 1139–40
(9th Cir. 2000); accord *Kerr v. Puckett*, 138 F.3d 321, 323 (7th Cir. 1998). Plaintiff filed as a
parolee subject to GPS and curfew — supervision conditions, not detention in a facility.

Defendants' "could-have-been-litigated" argument (*Clark v. Yosemite*) presupposes a **valid final
judgment on the merits**. Because the state ruling is neither a judgment nor a merits adjudication, and
because state administrative exhaustion is not an element of the federal claims, nothing the state court
did precludes the § 1983 and RICO claims here.

**Alternative ask.** If the Court has any doubt about the state record, it should **defer preclusion to
summary judgment**, where the register and the basis of the ruling can be established — as this Court
already contemplated in leaving preclusion for defendants to raise on a developed record (Dkt. 11).

### B. The Complaint States a § 1983 Claim

**1. Defendants are state actors.** Defendants brief four state-action tests but never engage the
controlling case: a private party who carries out a function the State is constitutionally or
statutorily obligated to provide to those in its custody or under its supervision acts under color of
state law. *West v. Atkins*, 487 U.S. 42, 54–57 (1988). The Ninth Circuit applies *West* to private
providers integrated into a public function. *Jensen v. Lane County*, 222 F.3d 570 (9th Cir. 2000);
*Rawson v. Recovery Innovations, Inc.*, 975 F.3d 742, 747–48 (9th Cir. 2020), cert. denied (Oct. 4,
2021).

The gravamen is not "a vendor with a contract." It is **participation in the parole-supervision
machinery itself**:

- California *statute* — not merely a contract — assigns to HOPE's "certified sex offender management
  professional" the determination of how long plaintiff must remain in mandatory treatment "as a
  condition of parole." Cal. Penal Code § 3008(d)(2). That is a **state-conferred power over liberty**.
- HOPE reports its determination to CDCR on the **Form 3043** Containment-Team pipeline, and CDCR relies
  on it to continue supervision. That is the joint action / close-nexus the tests require. *Jensen*,
  222 F.3d 570; *Franklin v. Fox*, 312 F.3d 423 (9th Cir. 2002).
- Defendants' cases are distinguishable: *Rendell-Baker* and *Blum* involved **discretionary** private
  decisions the State did not command, not a statutory delegation of the liberty-length determination.
  Their out-of-circuit district cases (*Reinhardt*, *Colliton*) addressed only the public-function test
  on an *admission/enrollment* theory; here the theory is close-nexus and joint action in ongoing
  supervisory reporting.²

> **² Footnote (distinguish, do not hide).** *Gross v. Samudio*, 630 F. App'x 772 (10th Cir. 2015)
> (unpublished; persuasive only) rejected state action where a provider's only act was refusing to
> re-enroll a parolee and the plaintiff argued the public-function test alone. This case is different in
> kind: ongoing Form 3043 reporting that feeds parole sanctions, a statewide blanket-retention policy,
> and a statutory delegation of the completion determination — pleaded first as close-nexus/joint action
> under published Ninth Circuit law (*Jensen*, *Rawson*), with public function only in the alternative.

**2. First Amendment retaliation.** Defendants recast the claim as "mere speech in response to speech."
That mischaracterizes it. The adverse action is not Palacios's words alone; it is the **retaliatory
official reporting** — logged no-shows for sessions plaintiff attended and adverse Form 3043 status
reports — that prolonged plaintiff's supervision. The elements are (1) protected activity, (2) action
that would "chill a person of ordinary firmness," and (3) a substantial-motivating-factor nexus.
*Ariz. Students' Ass'n v. Ariz. Bd. of Regents*, 824 F.3d 858, 867 (9th Cir. 2016) — the very case
defendants cite, which **reversed** a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and directed leave to amend. False
reports that extend custodial supervision are a paradigmatic adverse action, not de minimis criticism.

**3. Due process.** Defendants' own authority establishes the liberty interest: the "sex offender"
label coupled with mandatory treatment whose completion gates parole "create[s] the kind of
deprivations of liberty that require procedural protections." *Neal v. Shimoda*, 131 F.3d 818, 830 (9th
Cir. 1997); see *Wilkinson v. Austin*, 545 U.S. 209, 221 (2005) (liberty interest may arise from state
law or policy). Section 3008(d)(2) and 15 C.C.R. § 3574 create the expectation that the completion
determination turns on the certified professional's individualized judgment — not a blanket rule. The
procedural injury here is that defendants **fabricated the inputs** (false no-shows, mischaracterized
homework) that corrupt the very CTM process. Defendants' "you had a grievance you didn't use" argument
is the exhaustion point recycled; it is not a merits defect, and it is not an element of § 1983. *Patsy*,
457 U.S. at 516. *Kildare v. Saenz*, 325 F.3d 1078 (9th Cir. 2003), on which defendants rely, is an
SSDI/SSI mandamus case and is not the liberty-interest line.

### C. The Bane Act Claim (Cal. Civ. Code § 52.1) Rises With § 1983

Defendants concede the analysis: if plaintiff pleads a constitutional violation, the Court proceeds to
whether interference was by "threat, intimidation, or coercion." *Campbell v. Feld Ent., Inc.*, 75 F.
Supp. 3d 1193, 1211 (N.D. Cal. 2014). The First and Fourteenth Amendment violations pleaded above supply
the predicate. The coercion is HOPE's leverage as the **sole CASOMB-certified gatekeeper**, wielding a
non-completion determination it knew would keep plaintiff under GPS, curfew, and the threat of a parole
violation. The claim rises or falls with the § 1983 counts and should not be dismissed while they stand.
*(Do not plead the § 52.1(b) $25,000 civil penalty as a private award — it is public-enforcement only.)*

### D. Fraud / Negligent Misrepresentation — At Minimum, Leave to Amend

This is defendants' strongest ground, and plaintiff meets it head-on rather than conceding a single
count. Rule 9(b) requires the "who, what, when, where, and how." *Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.*, 567 F.3d
1120, 1124 (9th Cir. 2009); *Edwards v. Marin Park, Inc.*, 356 F.3d 1058, 1066 (9th Cir. 2004) (both
cited by defendants). Plaintiff can — and in an amended pleading will — supply that grid: for each
misrepresentation, the **speaker**, the **document/communication**, the **date**, the **recipient**, and
**why it was false**, including specific Bates-numbered reports (e.g., HOPE_000142, HOPE_000194 — a
no-show logged despite an agent-confirmed technical error) attributed to a named individual, **including
Smith personally** where the record supports it.

Defendants' reliance argument — that the misrepresentations went to CDCR, whose decision it was — fails
because California recognizes **third-party reliance**: a defendant who makes a misrepresentation
intending it to be acted upon by a third party (CDCR) is liable to the foreseeably injured plaintiff.
*Randi W. v. Muroc Joint Unified Sch. Dist.*, 14 Cal. 4th 1066 (1997).³ The complaint also alleges
misrepresentations made **directly to plaintiff** (an "individualized path to completion"), on which he
relied by continuing to submit homework and structuring his CDL employment around HOPE's schedule.

To the extent the operative pleading lacks any particular, the remedy is leave to amend, not dismissal
with prejudice: the deficiency is a pleading-specificity gap, not an incurable one. *Corinthian Colls.*,
655 F.3d at 995.

> **³ Pin/verify before filing:** *Randi W.* (third-party reliance) and the *Zetz v. Boston Scientific
> Corp.*, 398 F. Supp. 3d 700 (E.D. Cal. 2019) particularity standard (defendants' lead fraud cite —
> meet it; do not group-plead "HOPE and Smith").

### E. RICO — Distinctness Is Pleaded; Any Defect Is Curable

Defendants argue only enterprise distinctness and Rule 9(b) particularity. Distinctness is satisfied:
Smith, a natural person, is legally distinct from HOPE, the enterprise. *Cedric Kushner Promotions, Ltd.
v. King*, 533 U.S. 158, 163 (2001). The predicate acts (mail/wire fraud) can be pleaded with the same
who/what/when grid supplied for Count IV; any shortfall is curable by amendment, not fatal. Notably,
defendants' own particularity authority, *Schreiber Distrib. Co. v. Serv-Well Furniture Co.*, 806 F.2d
1393, 1400–01 (9th Cir. **1986**) (defendants miscite it as "2004"), **reversed** a RICO dismissal and
remanded **with leave to amend** — it supports plaintiff, not dismissal without leave.⁴

> **⁴** Plaintiff keeps RICO lean. If defendants press proximate cause under *Anza v. Ideal Steel Supply
> Corp.*, 547 U.S. 451 (2006) on reply, plaintiff will address direct causation then; the motion does
> not raise it. (Do **not** over-argue *Medical Marijuana, Inc. v. Horn*, 604 U.S. 593 (2025) — use it
> only if defendants run an antecedent-personal-injury bar.)

### F. In the Alternative, Leave to Amend Must Be Granted on Every Count

Defendants ask for dismissal **without leave to amend**. That is proper only where amendment "could not
possibly cure" the defect. *Corinthian Colls.*, 655 F.3d at 995; *Lopez*, 203 F.3d at 1130–31. Plaintiff
has identified, count by count, curative facts already in his possession (the Form 3043 pipeline detail
for state action; the retaliatory-reporting nexus; the Rule 9(b) grid with Bates-numbered reports for
fraud and RICO). At an absolute minimum, any dismissal must be **with leave to amend**.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For these reasons, plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court **deny** the motion to dismiss in its
entirety, or, in the alternative, grant dismissal only **with leave to amend**.
