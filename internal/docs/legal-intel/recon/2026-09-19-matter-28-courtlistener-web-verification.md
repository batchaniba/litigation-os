# Matter 28 — CourtListener + web verification / substantive gap fill
## *Newanforbi v. Shannon Smith, LCSW, CEO, et al.*, N.D. Cal. No. 3:26-cv-07504-LJC

**Compiled 2026-09-19.** Additive research for the anticipated Rule 12(b)(6) opposition. Nothing here is filed or served.

This pass verified load-bearing authorities against CourtListener / primary-web sources and searched for authorities missing from the September 12–19 Shannon corpus.

## Executive changes

1. **State action has a closer adverse line than Merrill:** *Wilson v. Azinkhan* (C.D. Cal. 2017) and *Lovelace v. Oregon* (D. Or. 2009). Both involved parole-mandated sex-offender treatment and found treatment/reporting alone insufficient state action.
2. **Those cases sharpen the § 3574 theory.** California now embeds the contracted clinician in a formal CTM/retention process; retention requires written good cause and a Form 3043 signed by both provider and parole agent.
3. **Current § 3574(a)(3) makes Aftercare voluntary.** That is in direct tension with HOPE_000294's stated full-supervision-term Aftercare rationale.
4. ***Wicklund* is verified**, but unpublished. It is a strong factual retaliation analogue, not the sole clearly-established-law anchor.
5. ***Estate of Esche* remains reasoning-only on immunity.** Its actual holding is collateral-order jurisdiction, and its discussion is double-edged because it recognizes the *Filarsky* contractor line.
6. **Fraud needs stricter reliance analysis.** *Gawara* and fresh N.D. Cal. authority *Asmus v. United Airlines* undermine a generic “HOPE lied to CDCR, CDCR relied, Plaintiff was harmed” economic-damages theory.
7. **RICO has a better causation architecture in *Harmoni*.** General billing is the attenuated *Anza* side; plaintiff-targeted false official reporting designed to trigger a compulsory governmental consequence is the stronger side.
8. **PR #881's Bane statement is too broad.** The Bane Act itself has no state-action requirement per se, but the underlying right must be one the private defendant can violate or interfere with.

## II. New adverse state-action line: Wilson + Lovelace

### Wilson v. Azinkhan — closest California analogue located

*Geoffrey Fitzgerald Wilson v. Tannaz H. Azinkhan, Ph.D. / Sharper Future*, C.D. Cal. No. 2:16-cv-08092-JVS-JC.

Wilson alleged that Sharper Future contracted with California, parole required him to attend treatment, and the therapist exercised authority within that setting. The district court nevertheless held that a state contract and compulsory attendance, without facts tying the **specific challenged conduct** to the State, were insufficient under § 1983.

This is closer to Matter 28 than *Merrill* and should be addressed affirmatively.

Sources: C.D. Cal. Dkts. 7, 11, 13; Ninth Circuit No. 18-55243. The Ninth Circuit terminal appeal was procedural and did not create a merits state-action holding.

### Lovelace v. State of Oregon

*Lovelace v. State of Oregon*, D. Or. No. 1:08-cv-03107-PA, Dkt. 91 (Aug. 10, 2009).

Lovelace was a parolee required to complete sex-offender treatment. The private therapist worked within the parole regime and reported to supervision authorities. The court granted summary judgment to the therapist, finding that treatment/reporting, without more, did not establish state action.

### Why Matter 28 is different

Do **not** distinguish these cases merely by saying HOPE has a CDCR contract or treatment is compulsory.

The distinction is the **current California decision machinery**:

1. § 3574(a)(4) defines a CTM whose normal membership includes the parole agent, unit supervisor, supervised person, and **contracted clinician**.
2. Even a minimally constituted CTM requires the contracted clinician, parole agent, supervisor, and supervised person.
3. § 3574(d) expressly permits completion before parole discharge.
4. § 3574(f) requires retention to be supported by **good cause**, stated in writing, and **signed by the treatment provider and parole agent on Form 3043**.
5. § 3574(f) identifies substantive risk/conduct considerations for retention.

That is materially different from “private therapist treats parolee and reports progress.” The challenged conduct should be framed as **participation in a joint, state-prescribed retention decision**.

### Briefing hierarchy

State action should run:

1. *Kirtley / Caviness* — identify the specific challenged conduct.
2. § 3008(d)(2) + § 3574 — statutory/regulatory assignment of treatment-duration and retention functions.
3. *Rawson* — State involvement in the decisionmaking at issue.
4. *West / Jensen* — supporting contractor / mental-health analogues.
5. *Wilson / Lovelace / Merrill / Gross* — distinguish directly.

Do not lead with public function; joint action/nexus is stronger.

## III. § 3574 verification produces a new high-value policy fact

Current 15 C.C.R. § 3574 provides:

- **Aftercare is voluntary** after successful completion of Curriculum and Maintenance, or after discharge from parole.
- CTMs formally evaluate readiness for SOMP completion.
- Supervised persons **may complete SOMP before discharge from parole**.
- Retention requires written **good cause**, provider + parole-agent signatures on Form 3043, and delivery within 30 days.

### Operative date correction

Regulatory history:

- new § 3574 filed as emergency **Oct. 14, 2024; operative Oct. 14, 2024**;
- emergency refiled Mar. 24, 2025;
- certificate of compliance filed Apr. 17, 2025.

Thus PR #880's date caution is needed only for conduct before **10/14/2024** (subject to checking whether a particular subsection changed in the permanent filing).

### HOPE_000294 tension

Portfolio evidence quotes HOPE_000294:

> “The Aftercare Phase of treatment is designed to comply with state law requiring registered 290 offenders to maintain their connection with treatment for the duration of their term on community supervision.”

Current § 3574(a)(3), by contrast, defines Aftercare as **voluntary** for people who successfully completed Curriculum and Maintenance (or discharged from parole).

For post-10/14/2024 conduct, this is a stronger Monell/custom fact than the generalized “blanket retention” description: HOPE's stated institutional rationale appears to treat a voluntary post-completion phase as compulsory full-term treatment.

Before filing, pin the manual's version/effective date.

Primary sources: CDCR emergency approval (10/14/2024), NCR 25-01, current 15 C.C.R. § 3574.

## IV. Retaliation: Wicklund is verified

*Wicklund v. Idaho Department of Correction*, 623 F. App'x 338 (9th Cir. 2015), No. 12-35294, decided Nov. 23, 2015.

The Ninth Circuit applied the five-element retaliation test to a probationer and held that evidence of a threat of jail because he pursued litigation/speech could support retaliation. A threat alone can be adverse action, and threatened incarceration would chill a person of ordinary firmness.

**Use:** factual analogue alongside *Watison, Rhodes,* and *Brodheim*.

**Limit:** unpublished / nonprecedential. Do not make it the sole clearly-established-law anchor.

This corrects PR #880's “UNVERIFIED” flag.

## V. QI: Estate of Esche audit confirmed

*Estate of Esche v. Bunuel-Jordana*, Ninth Cir. No. 24-6244, published Sept. 29, 2025.

The actual holding is jurisdictional: denial of the private defendants' good-faith defense was **not immediately appealable** under the collateral-order doctrine.

The immunity discussion is useful reasoning, not a holding that private medical defendants are categorically ineligible for QI. It surveys *Richardson, Jensen, Ace Beverage,* and *Halvorsen*, while recognizing *Filarsky* for private persons hired by government to assist in public work.

So:

- the Matter 28 safe-cite audit is correct;
- do not cite *Esche* as a QI-threshold holding;
- it is **double-edged for HOPE**, which actually is a government contractor;
- the argument must explain why a standing institutional vendor resembles *Richardson/Jensen/Halvorsen*, not a temporary *Filarsky* adjunct.

## VI. Fraud: downgrade the intermediary-reliance theory

### Gawara

*Gawara v. United States Brass Corp.*, 63 Cal. App. 4th 1341 (1998), reads *Mirkin* as requiring actual reliance for ordinary economic-loss fraud. Indirect communication is permitted, but the necessary reliance chain still must exist.

Gawara distinguishes *Randi W.* because *Randi W.* involved foreseeable **physical injury** and Restatement §§ 310/311; economic-loss plaintiffs remain under a more restrictive reliance rule.

### Fresh N.D. Cal. analogue: Asmus

*Asmus v. United Airlines, Inc.*, N.D. Cal. No. 5:26-cv-00962-BLF, Dkt. 21 (July 2, 2026).

This is fresh N.D. Cal. Rule 12 authority involving a pro se plaintiff and alleged false statements to a government agency that then acted against the plaintiff. It reinforces that *Randi W.* is not a generic way around the reliance element in an economic-damage fraud claim.

### Matter 28 consequence

Do not treat:

> HOPE lied to CDCR → CDCR relied → Plaintiff was harmed

as sufficient standing alone for Count IV's economic damages.

Build the Rule 15 cure grid around:
- direct statements/omissions to Plaintiff;
- what Plaintiff understood;
- what action or forbearance Plaintiff took because of them;
- concealment facts showing how disclosure would have changed Plaintiff's conduct;
- any properly applicable intermediary-reliance theory.

PR #881's *DZ Reserve* point does not resolve the threshold question of **whose reliance** Count IV alleges.

## VII. RICO: Harmoni separates the strong and weak causation theories

*Harmoni International Spice, Inc. v. Hume*, 914 F.3d 648 (9th Cir. 2019), is unusually useful because it analyzes two fraud-to-government schemes differently.

**Too attenuated:** fraudulent Customs submissions enabled competitors to evade duties and undercut prices, indirectly reducing Harmoni's sales. That failed under *Anza*.

**Direct enough:** sham Commerce filings that defendants allegedly knew would trigger a legally required administrative review. Harmoni's response costs flowed directly from the filings; the government process did not independently break the chain.

### Matter 28 translation

Weaker theory:

> fraudulent billing to CDCR → CDCR pays HOPE → financial incentive to retain clients → continued treatment/supervision → employment loss.

That is the *Anza / Harmoni scheme-one* side.

Stronger theory:

> deliberately false official treatment/retention reporting about Plaintiff → transmitted into the parole decision process to produce a compulsory consequence → direct retention/supervision consequence → identifiable economic cost.

That is closer to *Harmoni* scheme two.

*Dual Diagnosis Treatment Center v. Centene*, Ninth Cir. No. 21-55962 (Aug. 15, 2022) (unpublished), is useful corroboration that a government/law-enforcement intermediary does not automatically sever RICO causation when the plaintiff is the direct target and the *Mendoza* factors support directness. Use only as nonprecedential support; *Harmoni* carries the section.

**Drafting instruction:** billing should be motive/pattern context, not the principal proximate-cause engine.

## VIII. Bane Act correction to PR #881

PR #881's statement that Bane “rises or falls with state action” is too broad.

Civil Code § 52.1 applies to a person **“whether or not acting under color of law.”** *Jones v. Kmart Corp.*, 17 Cal. 4th 329 (1998), supplies the nuance:

- Bane itself has no state-action requirement per se;
- the plaintiff still must identify an underlying legal right;
- if the underlying constitutional right can only be violated by the State or its agents, a purely private actor cannot directly violate that right merely because § 52.1 says “whether or not acting under color of law”;
- private actors can be liable for coercive interference with rights that run against private actors and, in some circumstances, for coercively interfering with the plaintiff's exercise of a right vis-à-vis the State.

Matter 28 therefore should not say either “Bane survives regardless of state action” or “Bane automatically dies if § 1983 state action fails.” Analyze the underlying right.

Keep *Cornell / Reese / Sandoval* on specific intent.
