I. INTRODUCTION
Plaintiff’s Motion for Class Certification (“Motion”) should be denied. Plaintiff 
brings claims against Defendant The Vons Companies, Inc. (“Vons” or “Defendant”) for 
alleged classwide violations of (1) the California Penal Code (“Megan’s Law”) and (2) the 
California Fair Chance Act (“FCA”). Plaintiff fails to meet his burden under Rule 23 to 
demonstrate these claims are suitable for class treatment. 
Initially, Plaintiff, a former schoolteacher, is demonstrably unfit to serve as a class 
representative (i.e., fiduciary). His federal conviction for possessing child-exploitation 
material, his repeated violations of supervised release, his acknowledged inability to 
exercise sound judgment while under the influence, and his ongoing struggles with 
compliance and sobriety leave no doubt about his unfitness. No class action can proceed 
with a representative whose own conduct, credibility, and continuing criminal issues 
overwhelm the litigation. 
Plaintiff is also atypical. He disclosed his conviction to Vons, and the undisputed 
evidence shows Vons would have taken the same employment action regardless of his 
appearance on the Megan’s Law Website (“MLW”). His circumstances—an existing 
employee hired during a staffing emergency, not an applicant covered by the FCA—bear 
no resemblance to the situations of other individuals he seeks to represent.
Nor can Plaintiff establish commonality or predominance. His theory ignores 
California law, which authorizes employers to rely on MLW information to protect a 
person at risk, including children—precisely the concern presented here. And the FCA
requires individualized assessments, which necessarily defeats any effort to adjudicate 
liability on a classwide basis. Determining whether Vons lawfully evaluated conviction 
history, whether a particular crime was job-related, whether mitigating evidence was 
provided, or whether MLW information was used for a statutorily permissible purpose all 
require individualized inquiries.
Finally, a class action is not a superior method of resolving these claims. The case 
turns on individualized assessments, sensitive personal histories, and fact-intensive

II. RELEVANT FACTUAL BACKGROUND
A. Vons Maintains Background Check Policies and Procedures That 
Require Individualized Assessment of Every Applicant. 
Vons is a family-friendly grocery retailer whose stores routinely serve and employ 
minors. (Declaration of Courtney Jones (“Jones Decl.”) ¶ 2, 6.) It therefore maintains and 
follows background-adjudication procedures tailored to California’s FCA. (Jones Decl., ¶ 
3; Ex. 1 (Standard Operating Procedures)1; Ex. 2 (North Depo.) at 161:4-14.) The 
procedures also are consistent with the Legislature’s safety directive that MLW 
information may be used “to protect a person at risk.” (Id.)2
Consistent with Government Code section 12952 and its regulations, Vons conducts 
background checks of all its retail job applicants at the post-offer stage of the hiring 
process. (Id.) During the relevant period, Toni North and Courtney Jones were responsible 
for adjudicating background checks for California. (Ex. 2 at 44:2-14, 51:8-12, 75:6-15, 
61:7-8; Jones Decl., ¶ 3.) As part of its process, Vons makes conditional offers first and 
then evaluates conviction history, with no pre-offer inquiry into criminal records and no 
consideration of arrests not resulting in conviction, diversion, or sealed/expunged matters.

IV. LEGAL STANDARD FOR CLASS CERTIFICATION
Class actions are “an exception to the usual rule that litigation is conducted by and 
on behalf of the individual named parties only.” Califano v. Yamasaki, 442 U.S. 682, 700-
701 (1979). Class actions for damages are considered the “most adventuresome” type of 
class action. Amchem Prods. Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 614-615 (1997); see also Fed. 
R. Civ. Proc. 23(b)(3)(D) (requiring consideration of “the likely difficulties in managing a 
class action”). Issues are “individualized” when members of a proposed class must “present 
evidence that varies from member to member.” Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S. 
442, 453 (2016).
To obtain class certification under Rule 23(a), Plaintiff bears the burden of 
demonstrating, by a preponderance of the evidence, each of the four threshold 
requirements: (1) numerosity; (2) common questions of law or fact; (3) typicality; and (4) 
adequacy of the class representatives and their counsel. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(a)(1)-(4); Olean 
Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v. Bumble Bee Foods LLC, 31 F.4th 651, 664 (9th Cir. 
2022). The Supreme Court has made clear: “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading 
standard.” Wal-Mart v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). Rather, “[a] party seeking class 
certification must affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule.” Id. 
Even if Plaintiff could satisfy Rule 23(a)—and he cannot—it remains his burden to 
show that the case also qualifies for class treatment under Rule 23(b). Dukes, 564 U.S. at 
345-46. Under Rule 23(b)(3), Plaintiff must establish both predominance and superiority. 
The predominance criterion is even more demanding than commonality under Rule 23(a).” 
Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 133 S. Ct. 1426, 1432 (2013). It is not enough for Plaintiff to 
simply point to questions he claims are common—the central question is whether those 
issues are sufficiently cohesive and capable of classwide resolution. Class certification
