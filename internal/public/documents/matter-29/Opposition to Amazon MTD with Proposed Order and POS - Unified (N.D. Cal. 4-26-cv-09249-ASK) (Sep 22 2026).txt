 2

 3            Pursuant to Civil Local Rule 7-4(a)(3), the issues presented are:

 4

 5            1. Whether the Fifth Claim states a claim under article I, section 1 of the California

 6            Constitution where it alleges the use of registry information to deny employment -- the

 7            specific use California law prohibits -- rather than the disclosure of secret information.

 8

 9            2. Whether, if the Court concludes under Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp. that it

10            lacks equitable jurisdiction over the Sixth Claim under Business and Professions Code

11            section 17200, the dismissal as against Amazon should be without prejudice.

12

13            3. Whether the unopposed dismissal of the Seventh Claim as against Amazon should

14            be without prejudice.

15

16            4. Whether the motion must be decided on the Complaint alone, without regard to the

17            factual assertions in the motion's first footnote, and without any ruling on Claims One

18            through Four.

20                                            II. INTRODUCTION

21

22            Amazon's motion is limited. Plaintiff pleads seven causes of action, but Amazon

23    moves only against the Fifth Claim for invasion of privacy, the Sixth Claim under California's

24    Unfair Competition Law (UCL), and the Seventh Claim for intentional infliction of emotional

25    distress (IIED). It does not move against the First Claim under Penal Code section

26    290.46(j)(2)(E), the Second Claim under the California Fair Chance Act, the Third Claim

27    under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), or the Fourth Claim under the Investigative

28    Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (ICRAA). The merits of Claims One through Four are

                                                      Page 5
     PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO AMAZON'S MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. AMAZON.COM SERVICES, LLC (N.D. Cal.)
 1    therefore not presented for decision. Accurate Background has answered the Complaint and

 2    has not joined the motion. ECF No. 21.

 3

 4            The motion should be denied as to the Fifth Claim. Amazon treats that claim as an

 5    assertion that public registry information is secret. Plaintiff alleges something narrower: a

 6    protected interest in freedom from the use of registry information to deny employment -- the

 7    specific use California expressly prohibits. Indeed, the statutory restriction exists only because

 8    the information is public; public availability therefore cannot be the end of the analysis.

 9    California's enactment supplies the legal and social norms that inform each element under Hill

10    v. National Collegiate Athletic Ass'n, 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994).

11

12            Plaintiff does not oppose dismissal of the Sixth Claim as against Amazon only,

13    provided the dismissal is without prejudice. Plaintiff does not defend lost wages or ordinary

14    employment benefits as UCL restitution. Any dismissal would rest on federal equitable

15    jurisdiction under Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., 971 F.3d 834 (9th Cir. 2020), not on the

16    merits of the statutory predicates Amazon has not challenged. Under Guzman v. Polaris

17    Industries Inc., 49 F.4th 1308 (9th Cir. 2022), such a dismissal should be without prejudice.

18    Plaintiff likewise does not oppose dismissal of the Seventh Claim as against Amazon only,

19    without prejudice, and without adopting Amazon's characterization of that claim.

20

21            Finally, Amazon's footnote about the status of the background report is unsupported

22    attorney argument. The motion must be decided on the Complaint, and it cannot yield a ruling

23    on the four claims Amazon did not challenge.

 2                                          IV. LEGAL STANDARD

 3

 4            A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests the sufficiency of the pleading, not the merits. A claim

 5    survives when the pleaded facts permit a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable.

 6    Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The Court accepts well-pleaded allegations as

 7    true and draws reasonable inferences in Plaintiff's favor. Starr v. Baca, 652 F.3d 1202,

 8    1216-17 (9th Cir. 2011). A self-represented litigant's pleading is construed liberally. Erickson

 9    v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007). A pro se plaintiff should be granted leave to amend unless

10    the pleading could not possibly be cured by additional facts. Lopez v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122,

11    1127, 1130 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc).

12

13                                               V. ARGUMENT

14

15    A. The Motion Must Be Decided on the Complaint, and It Does Not Reach Claims One

16    Through Four.

17

18            Review under Rule 12(b)(6) is limited to the Complaint, documents properly

19    incorporated by reference, and matters properly subject to judicial notice. Lee v. City of Los

20    Angeles, 250 F.3d 668, 688-90 (9th Cir. 2001), overruled on other grounds by Galbraith v.

21    County of Santa Clara, 307 F.3d 1119 (9th Cir. 2002). Even when a document is judicially

22    noticeable, disputed facts within it may not be accepted as true. Khoja v. Orexigen

23    Therapeutics, Inc., 899 F.3d 988, 999-1003 (9th Cir. 2018).

24

25            Amazon submits nothing outside the pleadings -- no background report, no

26    declaration, no status record, and no request for judicial notice. Its first footnote nonetheless

27    asserts that the report remains unfinished because certain California court records are difficult

28    to obtain, and that no adverse action has been taken. Mot. 1 n.1. That is unsupported attorney

                                                      Page 8
     PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO AMAZON'S MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. AMAZON.COM SERVICES, LLC (N.D. Cal.)
 1    argument, and it cannot displace the pleaded allegations. Accordingly, the motion must be

 2    decided on the Complaint and materials properly considered under Rule 12(b)(6).

 3

 4            That posture distinguishes Lerma v. Amazon.com Services, LLC,

 5    No. 8:22-cv-01727-FWS-JDE, 2023 WL 4681571 (C.D. Cal. May 26, 2023), which the

 6    Complaint describes. Compl. ¶¶ 31-33. As the Complaint alleges, the court in that case

 7    resolved the source of the disqualifying information on the background report itself, which

 8    was before the court and which showed court-record sourcing and no registry hit. Id. ¶ 32.

 9    Here, Amazon has placed no report in the record, and Plaintiff alleges that he has demanded it

10    and not received it. Id. ¶ 29. For purposes of this motion, the Court therefore takes the

11    Complaint's alternative allegations about the source of the registry information as true.

12

13            Accurate's Answer does not change that analysis. Accurate admits that Plaintiff's

14    "consumer report is pending" and denies reporting registry information. ECF No. 21 at 10:3-4,

15    12:14-15, 18:18-19. A co-defendant's pleaded denials are not evidence and cannot resolve

16    Amazon's Rule 12(b)(6) motion; the Complaint's well-pleaded allegations are accepted as true,

17    and neither defendant has placed a background report before the Court.

18

19            Nor does the motion reach Claims One through Four. Amazon moved against only the

20    Fifth, Sixth, and Seventh Claims, and its footnote comments on the remaining claims request

21    no relief. Those claims are not presented for decision, and the order on this motion should not

22    be read to address them.

 1    B. Plaintiff's Fifth Claim for Invasion of Privacy Is Adequately Pleaded.

 2

 3            A claim for invasion of the California constitutional right of privacy requires a legally

 4    protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy under the circumstances, and a

 5    serious invasion. Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 35-37; Cal. Const. art. I, § 1. Whether a reasonable

 6    expectation exists and whether an invasion is serious are mixed questions of law and fact; they

 7    may be decided as a matter of law only where the undisputed facts show no reasonable

 8    expectation or an insubstantial impact on privacy interests. Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 40. Amazon's

 9    arguments on each element answer a secrecy claim Plaintiff has not brought.

10

11    1. The asserted interest is freedom from the prohibited use of registry information, not

12    secrecy.

13

14            Amazon argues that Plaintiff has no privacy interest in public criminal records or

15    information displayed on a government website. Mot. 5. But the Complaint does not allege

16    that the registry listing is confidential. It alleges an interest in the information "insofar as that

17    information is used for purposes expressly prohibited by Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)."

18    Compl. ¶ 73. The alleged invasion is the use of registry information to deny employment. Id.

19    ¶¶ 74-75.

20

21            Hill defines informational privacy as the interest in precluding the "dissemination or

22    misuse" of sensitive and confidential information. 7 Cal. 4th at 35 (emphasis added). Plaintiff

23    does not contend that his registry listing is confidential; Amazon is correct that it is publicly

24    accessible. The claim does not depend on confidentiality. It depends on restricted use, context,

25    the scope of consent, and resulting harm -- the considerations that inform whether an

26    expectation of privacy is reasonable and an invasion serious. Id. at 35-37.

27

28            The Legislature has identified the misuse at issue here. Absent a statutory exception,

                                                     Page 10
     PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO AMAZON'S MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. AMAZON.COM SERVICES, LLC (N.D. Cal.)
 1    Penal Code section 290.46(j)(2)(E) prohibits the use of registry information disclosed under

 2    that section for purposes relating to employment -- including, as alleged here, to deny it -- and

 3    section 290.46(j)(4)(A) gives the person harmed a private civil remedy. That enactment bears

 4    on whether the asserted interest is legally protected and on the social norms governing the

 5    challenged use.

 6

 7            At minimum, the statutory prohibition and its express civil remedy supply concrete

 8    legal and social norms relevant to the contextual Hill inquiry; they foreclose dismissal based

 9    solely on the public availability of the underlying information.

10

11    2. Amazon's public-record authorities do not address a statutory use restriction.

12

13            Amazon relies on Brahmana v. Henard, 2010 WL 11488727, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 12,

14    2010), and Estate of Hearst, 67 Cal. App. 3d 777, 782 (1977). Mot. 5. As Amazon presents

15    them, those decisions establish that court records and information about criminal convictions

16    on a government website are publicly accessible. Plaintiff does not dispute that proposition.

17    Neither decision addresses a statute that restricts a particular use of otherwise-public

18    information, and neither involved a use that the Legislature has made independently

19    actionable.

20

21            The distinction is not a technicality. Section 290.46(j)(2) governs information

22    "disclosed pursuant to this section" -- information that is, by definition, public. The

23    Legislature chose to publish registry information for protective purposes while restricting its

24    use for employment and other enumerated purposes. Public availability is therefore relevant,

25    but it is not necessarily dispositive of the distinct theory pleaded here: that Defendants used

26    registry-derived information for a purpose California expressly prohibits. Section 290.46(j)

27    informs the objective expectations and social norms relevant under Hill, even though the

28    statute does not itself establish every constitutional element.

 2    3. General authorization for a background check is not consent to the alleged prohibited

 3    use.

 4

 5            Amazon next argues that because Plaintiff applied, completed onboarding paperwork,

 6    and received an offer conditioned on a background check, he consented to the check and

 7    cannot claim a reasonable expectation of privacy. Mot. 5. That argument equates awareness of

 8    a background check with consent to every possible source and use. The Complaint does not

 9    support that expansion. It alleges that the offer was conditioned on a background check and

10    that Plaintiff later opted into text-message communications from Accurate. Compl. ¶¶ 8, 20. It

11    does not allege that Plaintiff authorized Defendants to use registry information to deny

12    employment in violation of section 290.46(j)(2)(E).

13

14            Saleh v. Nike, Inc., 562 F. Supp. 3d 503, 524 (C.D. Cal. 2021), on which Amazon

15    relies, recites the general principle that a plaintiff cannot claim a reasonable expectation of

16    privacy in an intrusion to which he consented. It does not hold that consent to one activity

17    extends to a different use, and still less to a use that state law forbids. Amazon cites no

18    authority holding that authorization for a lawful employment screening constitutes consent to

19    a prohibited use of the results. Under Hill, the reasonableness of an expectation of privacy is

20    an objective question informed by widely accepted community norms and the circumstances,

21    including the opportunity to consent. 7 Cal. 4th at 36-37. Here the governing norm is codified,

22    and the scope of the consent alleged is narrower than the use alleged. At the pleading stage,

23    the Court should not enlarge that consent in Amazon's favor.

 1    4. The Complaint plausibly alleges a serious invasion, and the balancing Amazon seeks is

 2    premature.

 3

 4            Seriousness is evaluated through the invasion's nature, scope, and actual or potential

 5    impact. Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 37. Plaintiff alleges more than abstract access to a public listing. He

 6    alleges the use of registry information for the precise employment purpose California forbids,

 7    resulting in the loss of a specific, dated job without the required notices or any opportunity to

 8    respond. Compl. ¶¶ 22-25, 75-76. A use the Legislature has singled out for prohibition and

 9    made privately actionable is, at a minimum, plausibly an egregious breach of the social norms

10    underlying the privacy right.

11

12            Amazon's reliance on the "high bar" language of In re Yahoo Mail Litigation, 7 F.

13    Supp. 3d 1016, 1038 (N.D. Cal. 2014), does not change the analysis. Privacy claims can be

14    dismissed at the pleading stage, but dismissal is unwarranted where, as here, the pleaded

15    circumstances plausibly support seriousness and the relevant context remains fact-dependent.

16    See Hill, 7 Cal. 4th at 40. Any asserted countervailing interest likewise requires a fact-specific

17    justification analysis. Id. On the pleaded allegations, Amazon identifies no basis to resolve

18    that analysis against Plaintiff at the Rule 12 stage, and the Complaint alleges a use the

19    Legislature has already labeled wrongful. Seriousness is at least plausible.

20

21            The same alleged conduct underlies the First Claim under section 290.46(j)(2)(E).

22    Amazon's decision not to move against that claim is not a concession that a violation occurred,

23    but it places the statutory merits outside this motion. The question here is narrower: assuming

24    the pleaded prohibited use, do the allegations satisfy Hill? At the Rule 12 stage, they plausibly

25    do.

25            Amazon argues that the UCL claim must be dismissed because the UCL affords only

 2    defend lost wages or ordinary employment benefits as UCL restitution; those sums are sought,

 5            A Sonner disposition does not adjudicate the UCL predicates. The UCL count borrows

 9            Under Guzman, if the Court concludes that equitable jurisdiction is absent, dismissal

12    prejudice, without adjudicating the UCL claim's substantive merits.

15    of the screening and application, the prospective harms that damages cannot prevent, the

18    remains in "Pending" status, Compl. ¶ 23 -- a point Amazon's motion does not dispute, see

19    Mot. 1 n.1, and that Accurate's Answer admits, ECF No. 21 at 10:3-4. Plaintiff therefore

20    requests either dismissal without prejudice under Guzman or leave to amend under Rule

21    15(a)(2).
