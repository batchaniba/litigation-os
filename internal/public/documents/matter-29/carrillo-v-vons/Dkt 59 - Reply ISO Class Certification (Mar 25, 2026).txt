1                                             INTRODUCTION
2           Asurint’s Opposition does not identify a genuine Rule 23 defect.1 Instead, it largely
3    sets forth merits defenses and ad hominem attacks on Plaintiff as purported class-certification
4    arguments. But Rule 23 asks whether Plaintiff’s claim under the FCRA is cohesive enough
5    to be tried through common proof—not whether Asurint has assembled inflammatory facts it
6    hopes to use against Plaintiff. With respect to the FCRA Class certification should be readily
7    granted because: (i) Plaintiff is typical of the group he seeks to represent – those “persons in
8    California who applied for employment with Vons and on whom Asurint conducted a
9    background search and furnished a consumer report containing sex offender registry
10   information from the Megan’s Law Website that was used by Vons and/or Asurint for
11   employment decisions”; (ii) Plaintiff’s claim is common and predominates over any
12   individualized issues because Asurint sent the FCRA 613 notice, Pre-Adverse Action Notice,
13   and Adverse Action Notice on behalf of Vons, which show that Asurint furnished
14   background reports with SOR information to Vons, knowing that Vons was using them in
15   violation of the Megan’s Law. Vons has already identified the members of the FCRA Class,
16   and the only thing left is to determine whether or not Asurint violated the FCRA with respect
17   to the FCRA Class members by furnishing background reports with SOR information to
18   Vons knowing they would be used in violation of the Megan’s Law. Accordingly, Plaintiff
19   respectfully requests the Court grant class certification of the FCRA Class.

17 B.      The FCRA Class Is Adequately Defined And Ascertainable
18         Although not required by Rule 23, courts have required a showing that the class to be
19 certified is ascertainable. See Walker v. Life Ins. Co. of the Sw., No. CV 10-9198 JVS

20 RNBX, 2012 WL 7170602, at *18 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 9, 2012). “Ascertainability requires that

21 the class definition be ‘definite enough so that it is administratively feasible for the

22 court to ascertain whether an individual is a member’ before trial, and by reference to

23 ‘objective criteria.’” Ehretv. Uber Techs., Inc., 148 F. Supp. 3d 884, 889 (N.D. Cal. 2015)

24 (citation omitted). A class definition that relies on objective, verifiable criteria satisfies the

25 ascertainability requirement. See Keegan v. Am. Honda Motor Co., 284 F.R.D. 504, 521

26 (C.D. Cal. 2012). Notably, the Ninth Circuit has held that in the context of a consumer class

27 action, that plaintiffs not only do not have to identify class members as a prerequisite to

28 meeting their burden under Rule 23, but also do not have to present a feasible methodology

3 D.       Commonality And Predominance Is Satisfied
4          The FCRA Class presents a straightforward common question that predominates over
5 any individualized issue: whether Asurint conducted a background search and furnished a

6 consumer report containing SOR information from the Megan’s Law Website knowing that

7 the information would be unlawfully used by Vons for employment decisions.

8          This common question governs Plaintiff and FCRA Class members’ FCRA claim.
9 Asurint argues individualized issues arise in resolving Vons’ liability under the Megan’s
10 Law. But Vons has already conceded that it does not hire applicants with SOR information

11 in their background reports. Vons identified approximately 43 applicants who applied for

12 employment, had background reports with SOR information provided by Asurint, and were

13 denied employment as a result. Kim Decl. ¶ 15. Each of these 43 applicants received an

14 Adverse Action Notice from Asurint. Thus, commonality and predominance with respect to

15 the FCRA Class is easily satisfied.

16 E.      Plaintiff Is Typical Of The FCRA Class
17         “Typicality is satisfied when the representative’s claims are reasonably coextensive
18 with those of absent class members, even if they are not substantially identical.” DZ Rsrv. v.

19 Meta Platforms, Inc., 96 F.4th 1223, 1238 (9th Cir. 2024), cert. denied, 145 S. Ct. 1051

20 (2025). A unique defense defeats typicality only when there is a danger that the named

21 plaintiff will become preoccupied with that defense to the detriment of the class. Id. at 1238-

22 39. Asurint argues Plaintiff is atypical because he disclosed his conviction. Opp. at 19:19-21.

23 Asurint’s argument is irrelevant here. Whether or not Plaintiff disclosed his conviction does

24 not exculpate Vons from liability for violating the Megan’s Law or Asurint from violating

25 the FCRA knowing that Vons was using the background reports for unlawful purposes . The

26 Megan’s Law does not require the SOR information be the but-for cause of the employment

27 decision. Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) (prohibiting “use of any information that is

28 disclosed” in Megan’s Law Website). The plain language of the statute provides that the “use

22 G.      The FCRA Class Should Be Certified For Injunctive Relief Under Rule 23(b)(2).
23         Rule 23(b)(2) applies when the defendant has acted on grounds generally applicable to
24 the class, making final injunctive relief appropriate for the class as a whole. Parsons, 754

25 F.3d at 686-89. That standard is satisfied here. Plaintiff seeks uniform injunctive relief

26 requiring “Asurint cease from furnishing consumer reports that include information from the

27 Megan’s Law Website knowing that such information will be used in violation of the law.”.
