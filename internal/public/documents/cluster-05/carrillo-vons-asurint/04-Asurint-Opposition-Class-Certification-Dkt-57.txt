I. INTRODUCTION 
Plaintiff is a registered sex offender with a disturbing criminal history. In February 
2023, Asurint, a consumer reporting agency (“CRA”), was requested by Vons, an employer, 
to prepare a background report regarding Plaintiff. During the background check process, 
Plaintiff self-disclosed his conviction for receipt of an image of a minor engaged in 
sexually explicit conduct. Asurint later accurately reported to Vons that Plaintiff was a 
registered sex offender. Based on Plaintiff’s criminal self-disclosure and his status as a 
registered sex offender, Vons independently made the decision to terminate Plaintiff’s 
employment. 
The story should end there as to Asurint. Instead, Plaintiff asserts an entirely novel 
and unsupported theory of liability against Asurint under Section 1681b(b)(1) of the Fair 
Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”), which requires that CRAs, such as Asurint, obtain a 
certification from end-users of consumer reports, such as Vons, that “information from the 
consumer report will not be used in violation of any applicable Federal or State equal 
employment opportunity law or regulation.” 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii). 
Plaintiff does not dispute that Asurint obtained this specific certification from 
Vons—which alone dooms his claims against Asurint. Instead, Plaintiff contends that 
Asurint somehow should have “known” that Vons was going to disregard or disregarded 
the statement in the certification (it did not), that Vons’ decision not to hire Plaintiff and 
his fellow purported class of sex offenders was in violation of Megan’s Law (it was not), 
and that this alleged violation by Vons somehow negates the certification it provided to 
Asurint (it does not). On this spurious basis, Plaintiff purports to represent a “FCRA Class” 
of California sex offenders on whom Asurint prepared a background report that was then 
used by Vons to make an employment decision in alleged violation of Megan’s Law. 
As detailed below, apart from the obvious merits-based failures in Plaintiff’s claims 
against Asurint, Plaintiff cannot satisfy multiple requirements for class certification under 
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. In particular, the FCRA Class is entirely dependent on

II. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND 
A. Asurint’s Business and its Limited Role as a Consumer Reporting Agency. 
Asurint is a CRA, as that phrase is defined in the FCRA. Declaration of Asurint 
Representative Lindsey Rajan (“Asurint Decl.”) ¶ 6; see also 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(f). In that 
capacity, Asurint makes available various background screening products and services, 
including preparing consumer reports for its customers who potentially use them in making 
employment decisions. Asurint Decl. ¶¶ 6-8. 
Asurint’s customers, including Vons, determine which background searches to order 
and which categories of information they want to receive in a background report. Id. With 
respect to criminal background screening, customers may select from packages including 
jurisdiction-based criminal history searches and sex offender registry searches. Id. ¶ 8.

B. Asurint’s Relationship with Vons and Vons’ Certification to Asurint of 
Compliance With All Obligations Under Section 1681b(b)(1) of the FCRA. 
As a third party provider to Vons, Asurint provides background screening services 
to Vons pursuant to Vons’ written Services Agreement and addenda. Asurint Decl. ¶ 15;
see also Declaration of Jasmine J. Stanzick (“Stanzick Decl.”) Ex. 2 (“Vons Dep.”) 168:6–
19. As part of this relationship, Vons requested Asurint to conduct specific criminal record 
searches, including searches for sex offender registry information from California. Asurint 
Decl. ¶ 18; Vons Dep. 190:2–192:9. 
At Vons’ specific request, Asurint also provides certain administrative notice 
services, including the transmission of pre‑adverse action and adverse action notices.
Asurint Decl. ¶ 14. To initiate this service, Vons personnel must affirmatively click on a 
drop down screen in the online portal, identify the Vons-approved pre-adverse action notice 
it wants to be sent on its behalf, and select the email and physical address where the notice 
is to be sent to an individual on behalf of Vons. Id. Consistent with Vons’ request, Asurint 
then transmits Vons’ pre‑adverse action and adverse action notices on Vons’ behalf and at
Vons’ direction. Id. ¶¶ 13-14, 28, 31; Vons Dep. 99:25–100:1; 109:7–9. The adverse action

resolving factual disputes, even on a motion for class certification, district courts must

IV. ARGUMENT 
A. Overview of the Substantive FCRA Claim. 
In analyzing the requirements for class certification under Rule 23, a court must 
consider (but not decide) the elements of the underlying claim. Dukes, 564 U.S. at 350-51. 
Consideration of the elements of Plaintiff’s proposed Section 1681b(b)(1) class claim 
begins with the relevant statutory provision: 
A consumer reporting agency may furnish a consumer report for employment 
purposes only if – (A) the person who obtains such report from the agency 
certifies to the agency that – … (ii) information from the consumer report 
will not be used in violation of any applicable Federal or State equal 
employment opportunity law or regulation […].
15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii) (emphasis added). 
This section imposes a narrow obligation on CRAs: before furnishing a consumer 
report, the CRA must obtain a certification from the employer that the report will not be 
used in violation of applicable equal employment opportunity laws. In other words, Section 
1681b(b)(1) governs the interaction between the employer and the CRA at the time of 
furnishing—not the employer’s subsequent interaction with the applicant or its decision 
making. See Juster v. Workday, Inc., 617 F. Supp. 3d 1128, 1137 (N.D. Cal. 2022) (Section 
1681b(b)(1) “concerns the conditions under which a CRA may furnish a report for 
employment purposes”). 
In support of his claim against Asurint, Plaintiff can point to no authority even 
suggesting that Section 1681b(b)(1) requires a CRA to monitor, predict, or police an 
employer’s conduct after a report is furnished, much less monitor and know all of the 
underlying laws that may apply (and how they apply) to a specific employer and to a 
specific candidate for employment. In fact, California courts have repeatedly held that any 
statutory limitations on the “use” of sex-offender registry information apply to the entity 
that uses the information—not to a background screening company, such as Asurint, that 
republishes publicly available records at an employer’s request. See Mendoza v. ADP 
Screening & Selection Servs., Inc., 182 Cal. App. 4th 1644, 1648–55 (2010) (holding that

C. Plaintiff Has Not Established The Rule 23(b) Requirements. 
Even if Plaintiff could satisfy Rule 23(a) (which he cannot), certification under Rule 
23(b)(2) or Rule 23(b)(3) is still improper. 
1. Plaintiff Lacks Standing to Seek Injunctive Relief. 
Plaintiff seeks broad injunctive relief, requesting an order “prohibiting Asurint from 
generating and providing Vons with background reports containing SOR information with 
knowledge that Vons uses the background reports in violation of the Megan’s Law.” Mot. 
at PageID 820. This relief cannot be certified under Rule 23(b)(2) for several independent 
reasons. 
First, Plaintiff seeks to enjoin conduct expressly permitted under California law. 
The injunction Plaintiff proposes would bar Asurint from reporting SOR information to 
Vons under any circumstances. But California law expressly permits CRAs to furnish 
SOR‑derived conviction information, so long as the information is publicly available and
lawfully sourced. Courts applying California law have recognized that reporting SOR 
information is permissible. Mendoza, 182 Cal. App. 4th at 1648–55. Because Asurint’s 
inclusion of SOR information is lawful, Rule 23(b)(2) cannot be used to prohibit it. 
Second, the requested injunction is not tied to any violation of Section 1681b(b)(1). 
Section 1681b(b)(1) regulates how a consumer reporting agency furnishes a report—
requiring it to obtain employer certifications before doing so. It does not prohibit a CRA 
from reporting particular categories of public‑record information, and it does not require a
CRA to police an employer’s hiring decisions or predict whether an employer will 
allegedly use information unlawfully. Plaintiff identifies no Asurint practice that violates 
§ 1681b(b)(1). Because the requested injunction does not correspond to any cognizable
statutory violation, it cannot be certified under Rule 23(b)(2).

2. Plaintiff Has Not Shown that Individual Issues Will Not Predominate. 
Rule 23(b)(3) permits class certification if “the questions of law or fact common to 
class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that 
a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating 
the controversy.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This requirement is “far more demanding” than 
the commonality requirement of Rule 23(a) and asks whether a putative class is 
“sufficiently cohesive” to merit representative adjudication. Amchem Prods., Inc. v. 
Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997).

Plaintiff fails to show predominance for several independent reasons.
