# Penal Code § 290.46 Employment-Use Restriction — Exhaustive Case Survey

**Compiled 2026-09-12.** Doctrinal research memorandum; not legal advice; requester is pro se.
**Scope:** every discoverable decision, docket, settlement, or news report applying California
Penal Code § 290.46's employment-use restriction to an employment decision.

> **Read the two framing corrections in § 0 before using anything below.** One is a statutory
> misnumbering that runs through the request and through this portfolio's own earlier memo; the
> other is a three-year date error on the lead comparator case.

## 0. Framing corrections

### 0.1 The employment-use restriction is § 290.46(j)(2)(E), not § 290.46(l)

The research request, and this portfolio's earlier memo
(`2026-09-12-case-law-and-counsel-analytics.md`, Part II § 1), both frame the provision as
**§ 290.46(l)**, with the earlier memo stating it was "formerly numbered (j)(2) in earlier
versions." **That is backwards.** The evidence:

| Source | Date | Subdivision used |
|---|---|---|
| *Donaleski v. Wal-Mart* order (E.D. Cal.) | May 2009 | "§ 290.4(d) and 290.46**(l)**" |
| *Cross v. Cooper*, 197 Cal.App.4th 357 (6th Dist.) | July 2011 | § 290.46**(l)** |
| *Towner v. County of Ventura* (2d Dist.) | Apr. 28, 2021 | "§ 290.46, subd. **(l)**" |
| *Lerma v. Amazon* order, Dkt. 36 (C.D. Cal.) | May 26, 2023 | "*See* Cal. Pen. Code § 290.46**(j)(2)**" |
| Accurate Background MTD memo, *Lerma* Dkt. 23-1 | Dec. 15, 2022 | "§ 290.46**(j)(2)**… (E) Employment" |
| UPS Rule 12(b)(6) motion, Matter 26 Dkt. 7 | Aug. 26, 2026 | "§ 290.46**(j)(2)(E)**" ×12; "(j)(3)" for exceptions |
| Newanforbi complaint + Sept. 9, 2026 opposition | 2026 | "(j)(1)", "(j)(2)(E)", "(j)(3)", "(j)(4)(A)" |

So **(l) is the pre-renumbering designation** that was operative when *Donaleski* was decided in
2009. The current structure, as used by every party and the court in the two modern cases, is:

- **(j)(1)** — authorization to use website information *to protect a person at risk*
- **(j)(2)(E)** — use "for purposes relating to … (E) Employment" is prohibited
- **(j)(3)** — carve-out list of entities otherwise authorized to access criminal history
- **(j)(4)(A)** — remedies: actual damages; an additional amount set by the trier of fact not
  exceeding three times actual damages and not less than $250; attorney's fees; and exemplary
  damages or a civil penalty not exceeding $25,000

**Practical consequence:** a brief filed today citing "§ 290.46(l)" cites a subdivision that no
longer carries the employment restriction. Every filed and draft document in this portfolio that
uses "(l)" should be conformed to "(j)(2)(E)" before filing.

**The timeline is now tight.** A published Court of Appeal decision used **(l)** as late as
**April 28, 2021** (*Towner*), and the *Lerma* order used **(j)(2)** by **May 26, 2023**. Search
results indicate SB 384 (2017) gave § 290.46 one version effective Jan. 1, 2018 and a successor
**operative Jan. 1, 2022** — which falls exactly in that window. So the (l) → (j) shift is
attributable to the Jan. 1, 2022 operative version.

**Still flagged as unverified:** the amendment text itself. `leginfo.legislature.ca.gov` is blocked
by this session's network egress policy (see § 6), so the renumbering is inferred from how four
courts across 2009–2023 spelled the subdivision, not read off the statute. Confirm against leginfo
or Westlaw before relying on the renumbering *history* in a filing. The *current* lettering is
solid independently — it rests on a 2023 federal order and 2026 filings by both sides.

## 1. Bottom line

**Six decisions have addressed a § 290.46 claim. Three are published. Not one holds an employer
liable for an employment decision — but the field is materially less hostile than this portfolio
has been assuming.**

| | Employment? | Ruled on a § 290.46 claim? | Published |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Donaleski v. Wal-Mart* (E.D. Cal. 2009) | **Yes** — termination | **Yes** — SJ for employer, no decisionmaker "use" | No |
| *Pastrana v. Local 9509, CWA* (S.D. Cal. 2008) | **Yes** — discharge | **Split.** First cause of action **survived** SJ; second and third dismissed. **Which one was the § 290.46 count is unknown.** | No |
| *McGroarty v. Am. Background Info. Servs.* (C.D. Cal. 2009) | Screener defendant | **Yes** — Rule 12(c); access/distribution ≠ "use" | No |
| *Mendoza v. ADP* (Cal. Ct. App. 2010) | Screener defendant | **Yes** — anti-SLAPP prong two; republication ≠ "use" | **Yes** |
| *Cross v. Cooper* (Cal. Ct. App. 2011) | No — real estate | **Yes** — "person at risk" authorization litigated | **Yes** |
| *Towner v. County of Ventura* (Cal. Ct. App. 2021) | **Yes** — public employee discipline | **No** — held only that § 290.46 is civil, so no *Flatley* exception | **Yes** |

**Four things changed in this pass.**

1. **The statute is § 290.46(j)(2)(E), not (l)** (§ 0.1). "(l)" was correct through 2021 and is wrong
   now. A 2026 brief citing (l) cites a subdivision that no longer carries the restriction.
2. ***Pastrana* is not a defense win** (§ 2.2). The portfolio's core framing — "the only merits
   decisions are two defense summary-judgment wins" — rests on a mischaracterization. The docket
   shows **summary judgment was denied on Pastrana's first cause of action**, and he litigated
   nearly three more years. Whether that surviving claim was the § 290.46 count is **the most
   valuable unanswered question in this research**, because if it was, *Pastrana* is the best
   plaintiff-side § 290.46 authority that exists.
3. **Two published cases were missing entirely** — *Towner* (§ 2.6), an employment case asserting
   § 290.46(l), which also supplies the only published appellate characterization of the statute's
   remedies; and *Cross v. Cooper* (§ 2.5), which litigated the **"person at risk"** authorization
   the first pass said no court had ever addressed.
4. **A live employment case exists that nobody had found** — *Sandoval Carrillo v. The Vons
   Companies* (§ 2.8), C.D. Cal. 8:25-cv-00191, filed January 2025, **still open with 64 docket
   entries running into May 2026**, complaint pleading § 290.46(j)(2)(E), brought by **the same
   lawyer who brought *Lerma***.

**What has not changed:** no decision has ever held an employer liable under § 290.46 for an
employment decision, and every loss turned on the **"use"** element rather than the statute's scope
(§ 3.3). The strongest corroboration remains adversarial: **UPS's Rule 12(b)(6) motion in Matter 26
cites no § 290.46 case law at all** (§ 2.9) — 22 authorities, none of them on this statute.

## 2. The cases

### 2.1 *Donaleski v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.* — the clearest employer-side merits decision

| | |
|---|---|
| **Caption** | *Donaleski v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.* (plaintiff **James Donaleski**; "and DOES 1 through 20") |
| **Docket** | **2:08-cv-00219-JAM-JFM** (E.D. Cal.) |
| **Judge** | **John A. Mendez**; Magistrate Judge John F. Moulds |
| **Filed / terminated** | **Jan. 29, 2008** / **May 8, 2009** |
| **Order** | **Dkt. 46**, May 8, 2009 — "ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT" |
| **Posture** | Defendant's motion for summary judgment |
| **Disposition** | **GRANTED** — "Defendant's motion is GRANTED." |
| **Status** | **Unpublished.** Free full text on govinfo: `USCOURTS-caed-2_08-cv-00219-1` |
| **Subdivisions pleaded** | § 290.4(d) **and** § 290.46(l) |

**What the plaintiff alleged.** Donaleski, a Wal-Mart assistant manager, told Wal-Mart of his sex
offense conviction and incarceration around Nov. 27, 1995; managers permitted a leave of absence
for the incarceration and he returned to work. On or about Dec. 18, 2006, Yelena Lopushkova, an
hourly sales-floor associate at the Rancho Cordova store, told a coworker that her sister — a
non-employee — had recognized Donaleski from a photograph on a sex-offender registry website.
Lopushkova then did her own internet research and found him listed on the California Megan's Law
Website. The listing reached Store Manager George Rodriguez, who informed Market Human Resource
Manager Linda Fermin. A committee made the termination decision. Donaleski sought a civil penalty
plus statutory, general, economic and special damages exceeding $75,000; his **second cause of
action** was negligence premised on the termination.

**Why it failed.** The court found **Wal-Mart's committee did not terminate Donaleski because he
was listed on the Website** — the causation/"use" element. The negligence count fell with the
statutory claim.

> **⚠ Quotation caveat — read before quoting this case.** The prior portfolio memo attributes to
> *Donaleski* the sentence: "The statutory language of the CPC does not prevent an employer from
> engaging in an independent investigation and obtaining personal information from the ex-offender
> even if the employer is aware that the ex-offender's information is displayed on the Website."
> **I could not confirm that sentence appears in the opinion.** govinfo is egress-blocked in this
> session, so the opinion could only be reached through search-engine extraction, and targeted
> queries for that exact language did not return it. The *substance* of the ruling
> (decisionmaker-causation) **is** confirmed. **Do not quote the "independent investigation"
> sentence until someone opens the govinfo PDF and reads it.** It is the single most load-bearing
> quotation in the portfolio's § 290.46 analysis and it is currently unverified.

### 2.2 *Pastrana v. Local 9509, CWA, AFL-CIO* — verified, and it is NOT a defense win

| | |
|---|---|
| **Full caption** | *Pastrana v. Local 9509, Communications Workers of America, AFL-CIO* (plaintiff **Alberto Pastrana**; co-defendant **PacBell** / AT&T Services Co.) |
| **Docket** | **3:06-cv-01779-BTM-MDD** (S.D. Cal.) — earlier captioned 06-CV-1779 W(AJB) |
| **Judges** | **Thomas J. Whelan** (through 2009), later **Barry Ted Moskowitz**; Magistrates Anthony J. Battaglia, then William V. Gallo |
| **Plaintiff's counsel** | Reza Keramati |
| **Filed / terminated** | **Sept. 1, 2006** / **Aug. 8, 2011** |

| **Citation as given** | 2008 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 94612 (S.D. Cal. **Nov. 19, 2008**) — matches the **signed** date exactly |
| **Status** | Unpublished; docket and order caption verified via CourtListener RECAP |

**The citation is real, and it is an employment case** — Pastrana was **discharged**, and he sued
both his union (Local 9509) and his employer (PacBell).

> **⚠ Correction, and it inverts the portfolio's position.** This portfolio has described *Pastrana*
> as a **defense summary-judgment win**, alongside *Donaleski*, as one of "two defense summary-judgment
> wins" that make up the whole § 290.46 field. **The docket says otherwise.** Dkt. 93 resolved
> **cross-motions** (Dkts. 71 and 74), granting and denying each in part. Quoting the docket entry
> verbatim:
>
> > "ORDER Granting In Part and Denying In Part 71 Motion for Summary Judgment; Granting In Part and
> > Denying In Part 74 Motion for Summary Judgment. Defendant PacBells Request for judicial notice is
> > granted. **Summary judgment is denied as to Pastranas first cause of action. Summary judgment is
> > granted as to Pastranas second and third causes of action.** Based on the **after-acquired
> > evidence doctrine**, Pastranas damage claims are limited to damages from the date he was
> > discharged to the date PacBell discovered Pastranas wrongful conduct, as set forth in section
> > III.D. Signed by Judge Thomas J. Whelan on 11/19/08."
>
> **Pastrana's first cause of action survived summary judgment.** He then litigated nearly three
> more years — reconsideration denied Dec. 22, 2008 (Dkt. 97), a mandatory settlement conference set
> for Jan. 11, 2011 (Dkt. 155), reconsideration denied again Jan. 3, 2011 (Dkt. 192) — before the
> case terminated in August 2011.

**THE OPEN QUESTION, and it is the single highest-value follow-up in this survey: was Pastrana's
surviving first cause of action the § 290.46 claim?**

- **If yes**, then *Pastrana* is the only case in which a § 290.46 claim has ever **survived
  summary judgment**, and the portfolio's framing of the field — "the only merits decisions are two
  defense summary-judgment wins" — is not merely imprecise but backwards. It would be the best
  plaintiff-side authority in existence on this statute.
- **If no**, the § 290.46 claim was among the second or third causes of action that were dismissed.
  There is a reason to think this is the likelier reading: *Donaleski* cited *Pastrana* six months
  later for the proposition that the statute does not bar an employer's independent investigation —
  language that would sit in a portion decided **for** the employer. But that is **inference from
  how a later court used the case, not knowledge of its holding.**

**Do not characterize *Pastrana* either way until Dkt. 93 is read.** Retrieve it from PACER (S.D.
Cal. 3:06-cv-01779, Dkt. 93) or Lexis (2008 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 94612) and identify the three causes of
action. Also note the **after-acquired evidence** damages limitation — a discharge-case doctrine
the portfolio has not accounted for anywhere, and one UPS cannot use against a failure-to-hire
claim, but which matters for how *Pastrana* reads as a comparator.

> **⚠ Posture correction.** The *Lerma* briefing's framing led the first pass of this survey to
> call *McGroarty* a **motion to dismiss**. The docket shows it was resolved on a **Rule 12(c)
> motion for judgment on the pleadings** — after the pleadings closed, not on a pre-answer motion.
> It was also a **putative class action**, which the briefing never mentions. Both matter: a 12(c)

### 2.4 *Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Services, Inc.* — published; the "use" holding

| | |
|---|---|
| **Caption** | *Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Services, Inc.* |
| **Citation** | 182 Cal.App.4th 1644 (2010) |
| **Court / case no.** | Cal. Ct. App., 2d Dist., Div. 8 — No. B214653 |
| **Date** | March 23, 2010 |
| **Below** | L.A. Superior Court, Judge Emilie H. Elias |
| **Posture** | **Appeal from an order granting a Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16 (anti-SLAPP) motion to strike** |
| **Disposition** | **Affirmed** |
| **Status** | **Published** — binding on California state-law issues |
| **Subdivisions pleaded** | §§ **290.4 and 290.46**, plus ICRAA, plus declaratory relief |

> **⚠ Two corrections.** First, the portfolio's earlier memo calls *Mendoza* "the only published
> California appellate decision construing § 290.46." It is not — *Cross v. Cooper* (§ 2.5) and
> *Towner* (§ 2.6) are both published and both construe the statute. *Mendoza* is the only
> published decision construing the **"use"** prohibition. Second, and more important: ***Mendoza* is an anti-SLAPP case.** The Court of Appeal
> affirmed the striking of the complaint. The "not 'use'" reasoning is how the court resolved
> **prong two** — that Mendoza failed to show a probability of prevailing — after holding at prong
> one that providing employment-screening reports is constitutionally protected activity. Citing
> it as a straight statutory-construction holding overstates it; citing it as *only* an anti-SLAPP
> ruling understates it. Describe it accurately: a published holding on the meaning of "use,"
> reached at the second anti-SLAPP prong.

**The two questions presented**, per the opinion: (1) whether an employment-screening business has
a constitutional free-speech right to republish information disclosed on the Megan's Law Website
to its clients notwithstanding the statutory use prohibitions; and (2) whether the trial court
properly granted the business's anti-SLAPP motion to strike an MLW-based damages complaint.

**Holding, as quoted in the *Lerma* briefing (182 Cal.App.4th at 1657):**

> "[T]he [Megan's Law] statute is not intended to create liability for damages on the part of
> employment-screening businesses that access, compile and republish information disclosed on the
> [Megan's Law Website]. … [T]he statutory liability created by the [Megan's Law] statute should
> be limited to employers who 'use' information disclosed on the [Megan's Law Website] as a basis
> for an employment decision."

**Verification status.** Caption, volume/page, case number, decision date, panel, trial judge, and
anti-SLAPP posture all verified by multiple independent sources (CourtListener opinion page,
FindLaw summary for B214653 dated 03/23/2010, California Anti-SLAPP Project caselaw entry, Law.com
*The Recorder*, Proskauer's 2010 client alert, *Metropolitan News-Enterprise* Mar. 25, 2010). The
quoted passage is verified as quoted in a federal filing; **the page-1657 text was not read in the
opinion itself** (no reachable full-text source).

**Limit.** Like *McGroarty*, **not an employment-decision case** — the defendant was the screener.
Its holding is dictum as applied to employers, favorable dictum though it is.

### 2.5 *Cross v. Cooper* — published; the "person at risk" authorization, outside employment

| | |
|---|---|
| **Caption** | *Cross v. Cooper* |
| **Citation** | **197 Cal.App.4th 357**, 127 Cal.Rptr.3d 903 (2011) |
| **Court / case no.** | Cal. Ct. App., **6th Dist.** — No. **H033164** |
| **Date** | **July 11, 2011** |
| **Posture** | Anti-SLAPP (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16) |
| **Status** | **Published** |
| **Subdivision** | § 290.46**(l)** |

**Not an employment case** — included because it is published, it construes the statute, and it
reaches the one provision the UPS defense is leaning on.

Sandra Cross leased a house to Stephen and Laura Cooper. As the lease neared expiry Cross put the
house up for sale and contracted with prospective buyers. The buyers backed out, and Cross sued the
Coopers for interfering with the sale, alleging they disclosed — or threatened to disclose — that a
registered sex offender lived nearby. Cross argued the disclosure was an **unauthorized "use"**
because there was no evidence it was made **"to protect a person at risk,"** and that its
illegality stripped the Coopers of anti-SLAPP protection.

**Why it matters to Matter 26.** The first pass of this survey stated that **no** decision
addresses "person at risk." That was too strong, and is corrected here: *Cross v. Cooper*
squarely litigated whether a disclosure fell within the "to protect a person at risk"
authorization. What remains accurate is narrower and still useful — **no decision construes
"person at risk" in the employment context**, and none holds that an employer's customers,
co-workers, or applicant pool qualify.

**Flagged as unverified:** the court's actual holding on the phrase. I could not retrieve the
opinion text (see § 6), so I know the issue was joined but not how the court resolved it. **Read
H033164 before citing or distinguishing it** — it cuts both ways depending on how broadly the Sixth
District read the authorization, and UPS will find it if it has not already.

### 2.6 *Towner v. County of Ventura* — published, employment, and missed by every prior pass

| | |
|---|---|
| **Caption** | *Towner v. County of Ventura* |
| **Court / case no.** | Cal. Ct. App., **2d Dist.** — No. **B306283** |
| **Date** | **April 28, 2021** |
| **Posture** | Appeal from an order granting an anti-SLAPP motion to strike |
| **Disposition** | **Reversed and remanded** |
| **Status** | **Published** |
| **Subdivision** | "§ 290.46, subd. **(l)**" |

**An employment case, published, asserting § 290.46 — and it appears in neither the request's
known-case list, nor the portfolio's earlier memo, nor the first pass of this survey.**

**Facts.** Tracy Towner was a Ventura County District Attorney investigative commander. A VCDA
investigator brought an administrative action alleging "fraud, favoritism, and other non-merit
based factors in the promotional process." Towner testified under subpoena at the Civil Service
Commission hearing. The VCDA concluded he had testified falsely and noticed its intent to terminate
him for dishonesty. The county then sought to disqualify the Commission on a conflict theory and
submitted notices of disciplinary action labeled "CONFIDENTIAL PERSONNEL DOCUMENT."

**How Towner won — not on § 290.46.** The court agreed that the County's willful disclosure of his
confidential personnel records, without the mandatory disclosure procedures, was punishable as a
**misdemeanor under Gov. Code § 1222**, so under *Flatley v. Mauro* it was illegal as a matter of
law and therefore not protected activity for anti-SLAPP purposes.

**What it held about § 290.46 — and this is the citable part.** Towner's § 290.46(l) allegations
**did not** carry the *Flatley* exception, because § 290.46 does **not** make a violation a
misdemeanor or felony. Instead the statute "**expressly prescribes civil remedies — including
actual and exemplary damages, and a civil penalty — for a violation of its provisions**."

**Why that is worth having.** It is a **published Court of Appeal characterization of § 290.46's
remedial structure**, confirming from the bench the remedy schedule this portfolio has been
quoting from the statute alone: actual damages, exemplary damages, and a civil penalty. Use it for
that. It is the only published appellate statement on § 290.46 remedies located in either pass.

**What it is NOT.** Under the request's own guardrail, ***Towner* is not a merits decision on a
§ 290.46 claim.** The court ruled on whether the § 290.46 allegations could support a *Flatley*
illegality argument — and held they could not, precisely because the statute is civil rather than
criminal. It did not decide whether the County violated § 290.46, and the reversal rested on
Gov. Code § 1222, not on Megan's Law. Do not describe *Towner* as a plaintiff win on a § 290.46
claim.

**A note on the numbering.** *Towner* uses "§ 290.46, subd. (l)" in **April 2021**, and the *Lerma*
court uses "(j)(2)" by **May 2023**. Those two published/filed dates are what pin the renumbering
window in § 0.1.

**Verification status.** Caption, case number, court, date, posture, and disposition verified
across four independent sources (Justia for B306283, FindLaw, the CELA Bulletin, CourtListener).
The quoted remedial language is reported consistently by those sources; **the opinion text was not
read directly** (see § 6).

**How the sweep missed it.** *Towner* did not surface in any of the eight CourtListener queries in
§ 6 — its opinion spells the provision "290.46, subd. (l)", which none of the subdivision-string
queries match. It was found instead by following an unrelated opinion-search hit. That is a
standing caution about the sweep's recall, recorded in § 6.

### 2.7 *Lerma v. Amazon.com Services, LLC* — fullest record; a real § 290.46 merits ruling

**The five FAC claims**, per the order at 3:

1. California's Megan's Law, Cal. Pen. Code § 290.46 *et seq.*
2. Fair Chance Act, Cal. Gov. Code § 12952

**Disposition (Dkt. 36, May 26, 2023), quoted verbatim:**

> "For the reasons set forth above, the court GRANTS the Motions and **DISMISSES WITHOUT

So: **three of five claims dismissed at 12(b)(6), without prejudice. Claims 2 (Fair Chance Act)
and 3 (ICRAA seven-year rule) were not dismissed and survived.** The request's "3 of 5" is right.

**Why Claim 1 (§ 290.46) died — sourcing, quoted verbatim:**

> "Defendants argue Plaintiff's claim fails because Plaintiff does not plausibly allege that the
> conviction information in the background report was obtained from the Megan's Law website rather
> than publicly available court records. … **The court agrees with Defendants.** … As stated above,
> the background report indicates that information about Plaintiff's criminal history was obtained
> from Orange County criminal records."

On the pleaded alternative theory:

> "Although Plaintiff argues the FAC asserts a 'competing theory' as to how Defendants obtained
> the conviction information in the background report and Plaintiff is 'entitled to discovery on
> this issue,' … the court finds **the FAC's allegations are contradicted by documents that are
> properly subject to judicial notice.** … In short, the court need not accept as true Plaintiff's
> allegations that Defendants improperly obtained conviction information from the Megan's Law
> website when those allegations are not supported by the background report itself."

> "Accordingly, the court finds the FAC does not sufficiently allege a claim for misuse of
> information from California's Megan's Law website against either Accurate or Amazon and GRANTS
> both Motions as to Claim One (violation of California's Megan's Law)."

The dispositive record fact: Accurate's report searched **twelve databases** — four counties,
three federal districts, the National Sex Offender Database and others — and **only one,
"County Criminal – Orange County, CA," was marked "record found."** Everything else read "no
record found," "completed," or "pending."

**The alternative ground the court expressly did NOT reach (footnote 1), quoted verbatim:**

> "Accurate argues in the alternative that even if the claim survived, Plaintiff's claim fails
> because Megan's Law only provides for liability against the user of the information (rather than
> the agency that generates the information) and Plaintiff does not sufficiently allege Accurate
> acted as an agent of Amazon.) … **Because the court finds the FAC does not adequately state a
> claim for violation of Megan's Law, the court declines to address Accurate's alternative grounds
> for dismissal.**"

This matters: *Lerma* is **not** authority on the *Mendoza* user-vs-vendor question. The court
ducked it.

**Why Claim 5 (UCL) died — no inadequate-legal-remedy allegation:** the court applied *Sonner v.

952 F.3d 1122, 1129 (9th Cir. 2020). Plaintiff had **conceded** the point and himself asked that

## 3. Cross-cutting angles from the request

### 3.1 The § 290.4 parallel provision — confirmed, but narrowed

**Every early § 290.46 case paired § 290.4 with it.** The RECAP sweep put this beyond doubt: a
dedicated `"290.4(d)"` query returned only seven dockets nationwide, and **two of the seven are
*Donaleski* and *McGroarty*** — i.e. the § 290.46 cases are a meaningful share of all § 290.4(d)
litigation that RECAP has text for.

- ***Donaleski***: pleaded "California Penal Code **§ 290.4(d)** and 290.46(l)."
- ***McGroarty***: surfaced on the **`"290.4(d)"`** query, so its filings plead the parallel
  provision too — a fact neither the *Lerma* briefing nor the earlier memo records.
- ***Mendoza***: pleaded "violations of Penal Code **§§ 290.4 and 290.46**," plus ICRAA and
  declaratory relief.

But the parallel is weaker than it looks. Per *McGroarty*, 2009 WL 980417, at *2 (as briefed in
*Lerma* Dkt. 23-1), **in 2004 the Legislature removed the § 290.4 provision** that had imposed
liability on "[a]ny person who copies, distributes, discloses or …" — the redisclosure penalty.
Accurate Background used that legislative history affirmatively to argue that redisclosure is no
longer actionable. **Anyone pleading § 290.4 alongside § 290.46 needs to confirm the current text
of § 290.4 first** — I could not (leginfo blocked).

### 3.2 ICRAA § 1786.18(a)(7) paired with § 290.46 — confirmed, and the ICRAA claim is the survivor

*Lerma*'s FAC pleaded exactly this pairing, and the result is instructive: the § 290.46 claim was
dismissed and **the ICRAA seven-year claim was not.** The FAC's Third Cause of Action invoked
Civ. Code § 1786.18(a)(7) — no reporting of "records of conviction … that, from the date of
disposition, release, or parole, antedate the report by more than seven years" — alleging the
March 2022 report carried a conviction antedating it by more than seven years. Claim 3 is absent
from Dkt. 36's disposition and survived to Amazon's answer.

**Takeaway:** the ICRAA seven-year theory is structurally more durable than the § 290.46 theory,
because it turns on the *age* of the reported record rather than on *where the data came from* —
and sourcing is precisely what killed the § 290.46 claim in *Lerma*. Where a report contains
history older than seven years, plead both.

### 3.3 The "use" element decides every case

Every § 290.46 loss turned on the same element — **that the decisionmaker actually used
website-sourced information** — and never on the statute's scope:

| Case | Element that failed | Stage (as corrected against the docket) |
|---|---|---|
| *Donaleski* | Committee did not decide because of the Website listing | Summary judgment (Dkt. 46) |
| *McGroarty* | CRA's access/distribution is not "use" | **Rule 12(c) judgment on the pleadings** (Dkt. 21) |
| *Mendoza* | Screener's republication is not "use" | Anti-SLAPP, prong two |
| *Lerma* | Report showed Orange County records, not the Website, as source | 12(b)(6) (Dkt. 36) |
| *Pastrana* | **Unknown** — Dkt. 93 granted in part and denied in part | Unread (§ 2.2) |

Still genuinely open after both passes: **"person at risk" as applied to employment** (*Cross v.
Cooper* litigated the phrase, but in a real-estate disclosure setting — § 2.5), the (j)(3)
carve-outs, and whether a customer base or co-worker pool can ever qualify. The (j)(4)(A) remedy
schedule is no longer wholly unconstrued — *Towner* characterizes it from the bench (§ 2.6).

## 6. The CourtListener sweep — method, results, and what it still misses

The first pass of this survey could not run a docket-level search, and recorded that as its largest
gap. **That gap is now closed.** The sweep ran in GitHub Actions rather than in the research session,
because `COURTLISTENER_TOKEN` is a **repository secret** — injected only into workflow runs, never
readable from outside one — and because `courtlistener.com` is denied outright by the session's
network egress policy. Script: `internal/scripts/courtlistener/sweep-290-46.mjs`. Workflow:
`.github/workflows/pc-290-46-sweep.yml`. Raw output: `290-46-recap-sweep.json` in this directory.

### 6.1 A bare "290.46" is a useless query

The first run searched `"290.46"` unquoted-by-subdivision and returned **196 opinion hits and 540
RECAP hits** — almost entirely noise. CourtListener tokenizes the numeral, so the query also matches
**the dollar amount $290.46**, and the RECAP results were dominated by bankruptcy claim schedules
(*9 Crosby LLC*, *Spirit Aviation Holdings*, *LeFever Mattson*, and two dozen more). Anyone
repeating this research should not read a large hit count as a large field.

**Subdivision strings are what work**, and both numberings must be searched, because filings before
the renumbering say (l) and filings after say (j) (§ 0.1):

| Query | Type | Hits | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `"290.46(j)"` | opinions | **0** | **No court opinion has yet cited the current numbering at all** |
| `"290.46(l)"` | opinions | 71 | Noise — the `(l)` is dropped in tokenizing, so this degenerates to the bare-number problem |
| `"Megan's Law" employment "290.46"` | opinions | **6** | Precise and complete: *Doe v. Brown*, *Doe v. Cal. DOJ*, *In re J.C.*, 2006 AG Opinion, ***Mendoza***, ***Cross v. Cooper*** |
| `"290.46(j)(2)(E)"` | RECAP | **2** | ***Carrillo*** and ***Lerma*** — nothing else in the country |
| `"290.46(j)"` | RECAP | 7 | Adds *Faith Wilson v. City of Pomona*; remainder unrelated |
| `"290.46(l)"` | RECAP | 5 | ***Pastrana***, ***Donaleski***, *Mitchell v. Brown*, *Adamski v. Martis*, one unrelated |
| `"Megan's Law website" employment` | RECAP | 1 | Unrelated (Roman Catholic Bishop of Oakland bankruptcy) |
| `"290.4(d)"` | RECAP | 7 | ***Donaleski***, ***McGroarty***; remainder unrelated (§ 3.1) |

**Unlike the opinion index, RECAP's full-text search honors the parenthetical.** That asymmetry is
why the same `"290.46(l)"` string is garbage against opinions and precise against filings.

### 6.3 What the sweep still misses — do not treat it as exhaustive

- ***Towner* did not appear in any of the eight queries** (§ 2.6). Its opinion writes
