 1                                     I.    INTRODUCTION
 2          Plaintiff is a registered sex offender with a disturbing criminal history. In February
 3    2023, Asurint, a consumer reporting agency (“CRA”), was requested by Vons, an employer,
 4    to prepare a background report regarding Plaintiff. During the background check process,
 5    Plaintiff self-disclosed his conviction for receipt of an image of a minor engaged in
 6    sexually explicit conduct. Asurint later accurately reported to Vons that Plaintiff was a
 7    registered sex offender. Based on Plaintiff’s criminal self-disclosure and his status as a
 8    registered sex offender, Vons independently made the decision to terminate Plaintiff’s
 9    employment.
10          The story should end there as to Asurint. Instead, Plaintiff asserts an entirely novel
11    and unsupported theory of liability against Asurint under Section 1681b(b)(1) of the Fair
12    Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”), which requires that CRAs, such as Asurint, obtain a
13    certification from end-users of consumer reports, such as Vons, that “information from the
14    consumer report will not be used in violation of any applicable Federal or State equal
15    employment opportunity law or regulation.” 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii).
16          Plaintiff does not dispute that Asurint obtained this specific certification from
17    Vons—which alone dooms his claims against Asurint. Instead, Plaintiff contends that
18    Asurint somehow should have “known” that Vons was going to disregard or disregarded
19    the statement in the certification (it did not), that Vons’ decision not to hire Plaintiff and
20    his fellow purported class of sex offenders was in violation of Megan’s Law (it was not),
21    and that this alleged violation by Vons somehow negates the certification it provided to
22    Asurint (it does not). On this spurious basis, Plaintiff purports to represent a “FCRA Class”
23    of California sex offenders on whom Asurint prepared a background report that was then
24    used by Vons to make an employment decision in alleged violation of Megan’s Law.
25          As detailed below, apart from the obvious merits-based failures in Plaintiff’s claims
26    against Asurint, Plaintiff cannot satisfy multiple requirements for class certification under
27    Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. In particular, the FCRA Class is entirely dependent on

18    A.    Asurint’s Business and its Limited Role as a Consumer Reporting Agency.
19          Asurint is a CRA, as that phrase is defined in the FCRA. Declaration of Asurint
20    Representative Lindsey Rajan (“Asurint Decl.”) ¶ 6; see also 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(f). In that
21    capacity, Asurint makes available various background screening products and services,
22    including preparing consumer reports for its customers who potentially use them in making
23    employment decisions. Asurint Decl. ¶¶ 6-8.
24          Asurint’s customers, including Vons, determine which background searches to order
25    and which categories of information they want to receive in a background report. Id. With
26    respect to criminal background screening, customers may select from packages including
27    jurisdiction-based criminal history searches and sex offender registry searches. Id. ¶ 8.

22                                   III.   LEGAL STANDARD
23          Class actions are the “exception to the usual rule that litigation is conducted by and
24    on behalf of the individual named parties only.” Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27,
25    33 (2013) (citation omitted). To justify departure from the usual rule, a party seeking to
26    maintain a class action “must affirmatively demonstrate” compliance with Rule 23. Wal–
27    Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere
28                                                 9
           ASURINT’S MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES IN OPPOSITION TO
                      PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR CLASS CERTIFICATION
     Case 8:25-cv-00191-SRM-KES       Document 57 Filed 02/18/26         Page 14 of 30 Page ID
                                            #:1362


 1    pleading standard[,] … a party must not only ‘be prepared to prove that there are in fact
 2    sufficiently numerous parties, common questions of law or fact,’ typicality of claims or
 3    defenses, and adequacy of representation, as required by Rule 23(a) [but] must also satisfy
 4    through evidentiary proof at least one of the provisions of Rule 23(b).” Comcast Corp.,
 5    569 U.S. at 33 (quoting Dukes, 564 U.S. at 350) (emphasis in original).
 6          Because “[c]lass certification is . . . not to be granted lightly[,] . . . Rule 23 mandates
 7    that district courts ‘rigorous[ly] analy[ze]’ whether a proposed class meets various
 8    requirements.” Black Lives Matter L.A. v. City of L.A., 113 F. 4th 1249, 1258 (9th Cir.
 9    2024) (quoting Comcast, 569 U.S. at 33). In many cases, “that ‘rigorous analysis’ will
10    entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” Dukes, 564 U.S. at
11    350-51. As the Ninth Circuit explained in Wang v. Chinese Daily News, Inc., “‘the merits
12    of the class members’ substantive claims are often highly relevant when determining
13    whether to certify a class,’ and ‘a district court must consider the merits’ if they overlap
14    with Rule 23(a)’s requirements.” 737 F.3d 538, 544 (9th Cir. 2013) (quoting Ellis v.
15    Costco Wholesale Corp., 657 F.3d 970, 981 (9th Cir. 2011)) (emphasis added).
16          While courts accept the allegations in the complaint as true, “the court need not
17    accept conclusory or generic allegations regarding the suitability of the litigation for
18    resolution through a class action.” Hanni v. Am. Airlines, Inc., No. C 08-00732 CW, 2010
19    WL 289297, *8 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 15, 2010) (citing Burkhalter Travel Agency v. MacFarms
20    Int’l, Inc., 141 F.R.D. 144, 152 (N.D. Cal. 1991)). “In addition, the court may consider
21    supplemental evidentiary submissions of the parties.” Hanni, 2010 WL 289297, *8 (citing
22    In re Methionine Antitrust Litig., 204 F.R.D. 161, 163 (N.D. Cal. 2001)). And when
23    resolving factual disputes, even on a motion for class certification, district courts must
24    consider “the persuasiveness of the evidence presented.” Ellis, 657 F.3d at 982.

 2    A.    Overview of the Substantive FCRA Claim.
 3          In analyzing the requirements for class certification under Rule 23, a court must
 4    consider (but not decide) the elements of the underlying claim. Dukes, 564 U.S. at 350-51.
 5    Consideration of the elements of Plaintiff’s proposed Section 1681b(b)(1) class claim
 6    begins with the relevant statutory provision:
 7          A consumer reporting agency may furnish a consumer report for employment
            purposes only if – (A) the person who obtains such report from the agency
 8          certifies to the agency that – … (ii) information from the consumer report
            will not be used in violation of any applicable Federal or State equal
 9          employment opportunity law or regulation […].
10    15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii) (emphasis added).
11          This section imposes a narrow obligation on CRAs: before furnishing a consumer
12    report, the CRA must obtain a certification from the employer that the report will not be
13    used in violation of applicable equal employment opportunity laws. In other words, Section
14    1681b(b)(1) governs the interaction between the employer and the CRA at the time of
15    furnishing—not the employer’s subsequent interaction with the applicant or its decision
16    making. See Juster v. Workday, Inc., 617 F. Supp. 3d 1128, 1137 (N.D. Cal. 2022) (Section
17    1681b(b)(1) “concerns the conditions under which a CRA may furnish a report for
18    employment purposes”).
19          In support of his claim against Asurint, Plaintiff can point to no authority even
20    suggesting that Section 1681b(b)(1) requires a CRA to monitor, predict, or police an
21    employer’s conduct after a report is furnished, much less monitor and know all of the
22    underlying laws that may apply (and how they apply) to a specific employer and to a
23    specific candidate for employment. In fact, California courts have repeatedly held that any
24    statutory limitations on the “use” of sex-offender registry information apply to the entity
25    that uses the information—not to a background screening company, such as Asurint, that
26    republishes publicly available records at an employer’s request. See Mendoza v. ADP
27    Screening & Selection Servs., Inc., 182 Cal. App. 4th 1644, 1648–55 (2010) (holding that

 7          1.     Plaintiff Lacks Standing to Seek Injunctive Relief.
 8          Plaintiff seeks broad injunctive relief, requesting an order “prohibiting Asurint from

12    reasons.
13          First, Plaintiff seeks to enjoin conduct expressly permitted under California law.
14    The injunction Plaintiff proposes would bar Asurint from reporting SOR information to
15    Vons under any circumstances. But California law expressly permits CRAs to furnish
16    SOR‑derived conviction information, so long as the information is publicly available and
17    lawfully sourced. Courts applying California law have recognized that reporting SOR
18    information is permissible. Mendoza, 182 Cal. App. 4th at 1648–55. Because Asurint’s
19    inclusion of SOR information is lawful, Rule 23(b)(2) cannot be used to prohibit it.
20          Second, the requested injunction is not tied to any violation of Section 1681b(b)(1).
21    Section 1681b(b)(1) regulates how a consumer reporting agency furnishes a report—
22    requiring it to obtain employer certifications before doing so. It does not prohibit a CRA
23    from reporting particular categories of public‑record information, and it does not require a
24    CRA to police an employer’s hiring decisions or predict whether an employer will
25    allegedly use information unlawfully. Plaintiff identifies no Asurint practice that violates
26    § 1681b(b)(1). Because the requested injunction does not correspond to any cognizable
27    statutory violation, it cannot be certified under Rule 23(b)(2).

20          2.     Plaintiff Has Not Shown that Individual Issues Will Not Predominate.
21          Rule 23(b)(3) permits class certification if “the questions of law or fact common to
22    class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that
23    a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating
24    the controversy.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This requirement is “far more demanding” than
25    the commonality requirement of Rule 23(a) and asks whether a putative class is
26    “sufficiently cohesive” to merit representative adjudication. Amchem Prods., Inc. v.
27    Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997).
