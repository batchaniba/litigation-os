24                                          II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

25

26            A motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) tests the legal sufficiency of the complaint.

27   Navarro v. Block, 250 F.3d 729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001). To survive, a complaint must contain

28   "sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to 'state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.'"

                                                           Page 3
                 OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The court must "accept all factual allegations in the

 2   complaint as true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party."

 3   Knievel v. ESPN, 393 F.3d 1068, 1072 (9th Cir. 2005). Where competing inferences may be

 4   drawn from the same set of facts, the complaint survives-"even if it strikes a savvy judge that

 5   actual proof of the facts alleged is improbable." Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 556

 6   (2007).

 7

 8             Pro se pleadings are held "to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted by

 9   lawyers" and must be construed liberally. Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007); Hebbe v.

10   Pliler, 627 F.3d 338, 342 (9th Cir. 2010). Dismissal with prejudice is warranted only when it is

11   "absolutely clear that the deficiencies of the complaint could not be cured by amendment." Lopez

12   v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1137 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (quoting Noll v. Carlson, 809 F.2d 1446,

13   1448 (9th Cir. 1987)). "[T]he rule favoring liberality in amendments to pleadings is particularly

14   important for the pro se litigant." Id. at 1131 (quoting Noll, 809 F.2d at 1448).

18   A. Defendant's Rule 12(f) Motions to Strike Are Improper Under Whittlestone.

19

20             Defendant moves under Rule 12(f) to strike Plaintiff's fourth cause of action and prayers

21   for declaratory relief, injunctive relief, attorney fees, and punitive damages. (MTD at 12-14.)

22   Each motion fails at the threshold.

23

24             In Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co., 618 F.3d 970 (9th Cir. 2010), the Ninth Circuit

25   held that Rule 12(f) "does not authorize a district court to strike a claim for damages on the

26   ground that such damages are precluded as a matter of law." Id. at 971, 974-75. Rule 12(f)

27   authorizes striking only "redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous" matter. Id. at 973

28   (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(f)). Challenges that test the legal sufficiency of a damages prayer "are

                                                    Page 4
                  OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   better suited for a Rule 12(b)(6) motion or a Rule 56 motion, not a Rule 12(f) motion." Id. at 974.

 2   Defendant's reliance on Bureerong v. Uvawas, 922 F. Supp. 1450 (C.D. Cal. 1996), and

 3   Wilkerson v. Butler, 229 F.R.D. 166 (E.D. Cal. 2005), is misplaced. Both predate Whittlestone

 4   and are no longer good law on this point in the Ninth Circuit.

 5

 6            To the extent the Court construes these arguments as part of the Rule 12(b)(6) motion,

 7   they fail on the merits as well.

 8

 9            Punitive damages. The FAC alleges Defendant (a) denied certification despite possessing

10   the dashcam recording and Detention Certificate proving fabricated evidence, (b) issued a

11   boilerplate denial engaging with no evidence, and (c) did so amid a concrete institutional

12   incentive to deny while supervising the accused officers. (FAC ¶¶ 37, 49.) These allegations

13   plausibly state "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." Smith

14   v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). That the FAC disclaims relitigating the officers' underlying tort

15   liability does not erase Serrato's own conduct: refusing to engage with evidence in his own

16   custody when deciding a certification request under section 679.10.

17

18            Injunctive relief. The FAC alleges an ongoing deprivation of access to the U-visa process,

19   continuing immigration uncertainty, inability to obtain employment authorization and removal

20   protections, and dignitary injury from the unresolved denial. (FAC ¶¶ 39, 44, 51, Prayer ¶¶ E-F.)

21   Those allegations speak directly to Winter's irreparable-harm and balance-of-equities inquiries.

22   Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008). Whether Plaintiff ultimately

23   obtains an injunction is a merits question; it is not a basis to strike the prayer at the pleading

24   stage.

25

26            Attorney fees. Plaintiff does not contend he may recover fees for his own pro se work

27   under Kay v. Ehrler, 499 U.S. 432, 438 (1991). To the extent counsel is later retained or

28   appointed-including through the Federal Pro Bono Project referral (Dkt. No. 5 at 5)-the prayer

                                                    Page 5
                 OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)

 3   B. The First Amendment Retaliation Claim Is Well-Pleaded.

 4

 5           Defendant's second ground in the Notice is that retaliation fails "because Plaintiff was not

 6   entitled to U-visa certification as a matter of law." (MTD at 2.) That gets the doctrine

 7   backwards-and ignores this Court's screening holding that Serrato's certification discretion does

 8   not include discretion to retaliate. (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.) "[E]ven though a person has no 'right' to a

 9   valuable governmental benefit and even though the government may deny him the benefit for

10   any number of reasons, there are some reasons upon which the government may not rely. It may

11   not deny a benefit to a person on a basis that infringes his constitutionally protected

12   interests-especially, his interest in freedom of speech." Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593, 597

13   (1972); see also Bd. of Cnty. Comm'rs v. Umbehr, 518 U.S. 668, 674 (1996). Discretionary

14   benefits are precisely where retaliation doctrine operates. Whether Plaintiff had a freestanding

15   "right" to certification is irrelevant to whether Defendant denied it in retaliation for protected

16   activity.

17

18           To state a First Amendment retaliation claim, Plaintiff must allege that he engaged in

19   protected activity, that Defendant took adverse action that would chill a person of ordinary

20   firmness, and that Defendant's desire to chill that speech was a but-for cause of the adverse

21   action. Ford v. City of Yakima, 706 F.3d 1188, 1193 (9th Cir. 2013). At the pleading stage,

22   Plaintiff need only allege facts making retaliatory but-for causation plausible-not prove it, and

23   not disprove every innocent explanation. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009); Capp v.

24   Cnty. of San Diego, 940 F.3d 1046, 1055 (9th Cir. 2019). The Mt. Healthy burden-shift-under

25   which the defendant may show it would have taken the same action anyway-is an affirmative

26   defense for summary judgment or trial, not a pleading barrier. Mt. Healthy City Sch. Dist. Bd. of

27   Educ. v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274, 287 (1977).

28

                 OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1           The FAC more than satisfies Ford's but-for standard through five mutually reinforcing

 2   allegations:

 3

 4           Documented knowledge. Defendant's own March 26, 2026 letter expressly acknowledges

 5   Plaintiff's citizen complaint and assigns an investigator. (FAC ¶ 48.) This is a contemporaneous

 6   written admission-not an inference. Defendant's response-that assigning an investigator is

 7   proper-misses the point. The letter proves knowledge of protected activity before the denial.

 8   Knowledge plus adverse action in close proximity is classic retaliation circumstantial evidence.

 9

10           Tight temporal proximity. POST forwarded the complaint on March 23. Serrato assigned

11   an investigator on March 26. Plaintiff submitted the certification request on April 9. Serrato

12   denied it on April 20-eleven days later-without substantive analysis. (FAC ¶ 49(b).) Pratt v.

13   Rowland, 65 F.3d 802, 808 (9th Cir. 1995). Defendant's "prompt action reflects responsiveness"

14   theory is a competing inference. Competing inferences must be resolved in Plaintiff's favor at

15   this stage. Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556.

16

18   Serrato to attest under penalty of perjury that his subordinate officers committed qualifying

19   criminal activity-while CHP was defending those same officers in two pending civil lawsuits.

20   (FAC ¶ 49(c).) That incentive is not "Plaintiff's decision to sue"; it is Serrato's institutional

21   interest in avoiding an official admission that would undermine those defenses.

27   for the boilerplate refusal appears. Capp, 940 F.3d at 1055; Nieves v. Bartlett, 587 U.S. 391, 406

28   (2019). (FAC ¶ 49(d).)

 7   the underlying records, and that agency's commander was structurally compromised as

15   C. The Procedural Due Process Claim Is Well-Pleaded.

16

17          Even accepting Serrato's substantive discretion to deny certification, the manner of denial

18   must still satisfy the process section 679.10 requires. Defendant argues the denial letter "lays out

19   the reason behind the denial" and that "is all the procedural due process the law requires." (MTD

20   at 9.) The FAC alleges otherwise. Procedural due process first requires a protected liberty or

21   property interest. Bd. of Regents v. Roth, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972). Plaintiff does not claim a

22   protected interest in the ultimate certification result. The interest alleged is narrower: section

23   679.10's mandatory criteria and denial procedures materially constrain official discretion and

24   create a legitimate entitlement to a non-retaliatory, statutorily compliant decisional

25   process-including a meaningful written explanation-even if not to a compelled Form B signature.

26   (FAC ¶¶ 40-44.)

27

28          Section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2), requires "a written explanation for the denial." Where

                                                    Page 8
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   the denial rests on non-cooperation, the explanation must include "specific details of any

 2   reasonable requests for cooperation and a detailed description of how the victim refused to

 3   cooperate." The first sentence is a stand-alone command applicable to every denial; the second

 4   supplements it for cooperation-based denials. To read the second as the only requirement would

 5   mean an agency could deny certification on the most common ground-a finding the applicant was

 6   not a crime victim-with no meaningful explanation at all. That is what AB 1261 was enacted to

 7   prevent. The Legislative Counsel's Digest describes the written-explanation duty without

 8   qualification: a certifying entity that does not certify "shall provide a written explanation for the

 9   denial of the certification." (Stats. 2023, ch. 679.)

10

11           The FAC specifically alleges the five-sentence boilerplate was legally deficient because

12   it: (a) did not address the dashcam evidence; (b) did not address the section 851.6 Detention

13   Certificate; (c) did not identify which statutory element was unsatisfied; (d) did not acknowledge

14   the rebuttable presumption of helpfulness; and (e) did not provide specific reasons as required by

15   statute. (FAC ¶¶ 31, 37, 43.) A conclusory assertion that the incident "does not meet the

16   standard"-without identifying the standard applied, the element found wanting, or any

17   engagement with the agency's own exculpatory records-is not a "written explanation" in any

18   meaningful sense. It is the form of denial AB 1261 was written to abolish.

19

20           If that interest is protected, Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976), confirms the

21   process alleged here was inadequate: the private interest in access to the U-visa gateway is

22   substantial; the risk of erroneous deprivation from a five-sentence boilerplate engaging with no

23   evidence is extreme; and the governmental interest in withholding a reasoned explanation is

24   minimal because the Supplement B is a factual certification, not a visa approval. (FAC ¶ 38.)

25   Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S. 254, 262 (1970). These allegations state a plausible procedural due

26   process claim.

 1   D. The Substantive Due Process Claim Is Well-Pleaded.

 2

 3          Plaintiff does not assert a federal right to U-visa certification. Nor does he argue that

 4   section 679.10 eliminates all discretion. To the extent FAC paragraph 35 can be read as alleging

 5   that section 679.10 eliminates all substantive certification discretion or that the official "must

 6   sign" whenever statutory elements are met, Plaintiff does not rely on that proposition here. The

 7   surviving constitutional theory concerns retaliatory motive and mandatory procedural constraints.

 8   He accepts this Court's holding that Serrato had discretion to certify for purposes of the

 9   compel-signature theory Defendant attributes to the FAC. (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.) That acceptance does

10   not waive Plaintiff's separate state-mandate arguments under section 679.10. The claim here is

11   narrower: where California has enacted a statute that (a) uses mandatory language ("shall"), (b)

12   creates a rebuttable presumption of helpfulness, (c) requires specific written reasons for denial,

13   (d) mandates processing within 30 days, and (e) expressly provides that investigation, charges,

14   prosecution, or conviction are not required for certification, a certifying official who ignores

15   those constraints and issues a boilerplate denial without engaging with the evidence in his own

16   custody has acted arbitrarily.

17

18          Under federal law, the decision to sign a certification is discretionary. Perez Perez v. Wolf

19   , 943 F.3d 853, 863 (9th Cir. 2019). This Court quoted that principle at screening and still held

20   the complaint sufficient. (Dkt. No. 5 at 3-4.) Federal discretion does not erase California's

21   subsequent statutory constraints. Defendant's reliance on Catholic Charities CYO v. Chertoff,

22   622 F. Supp. 2d 865 (N.D. Cal. 2008), is misplaced: that case addressed the federal statute-which

23   lacked mandatory language-and was decided seven years before section 679.10 and sixteen years

24   before AB 1261. The secondary substantive due process theory is preserved, but it is not the lead

25   claim and does not depend on forcing a certification signature.

26

27          Defendant's "civil tort" label rests on the absence of criminal charges. Section 679.10,

28   subdivision (k)(1), expressly provides that investigation, charges, prosecution, or conviction "are

                                                   Page 10
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   not required" for certification. The FAC identifies enumerated qualifying criminal activity under

 2   8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(U)(iii) and section 679.10(c)-false imprisonment, unlawful criminal

 3   restraint, felonious assault, perjury, and obstruction of justice. (FAC ¶ 21.) This Court already

 4   accepted those allegations as supporting a plausible inference of false imprisonment based on

 5   fabricated evidence. (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.) And Defendant's attack on the helpfulness presumption

 6   inverts subdivision (h): if no assistance was requested, the victim has not "refused or failed to

 7   provide" it. The FAC further alleges affirmative cooperation through complaints and production

 8   of the agency's own records. (FAC ¶¶ 23, 47.)

10   E. Declaratory and Injunctive Relief Are Proper.

11

12          Plaintiff does not oppose treating the fourth cause of action as a prayer for remedy rather

13   than a standalone cause of action. Declaratory relief is properly characterized as a form of relief.

14   The relief sought, however, remains proper and should not be stricken. Plaintiff does not seek an

21   26CV191136 concerning section 679.10's enforceable processing and explanation duties.

22

23          The FAC seeks prospective relief against Defendant in his official capacity: a declaration

24   that the April 20, 2026 denial violated constitutional standards and that Plaintiff is entitled to a

25   new certification decision applying the correct standards to the evidence in CHP's possession,

26   together with corresponding injunctive relief. (FAC ¶¶ 53-54, Prayer ¶¶ E-F.) To the extent any

27   prayer language could be read more broadly, Plaintiff clarifies that the operative request on this

28   motion is process correction-vacatur and redo-not a directed Form B signature. That is

                                                   Page 11
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   prospective relief authorized by Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). It is not duplicative of the

 2   damages claims against Defendant in his individual capacity.

 3

 4          Defendant's authorities involve plaintiffs seeking declarations about completed

 5   foreclosures or other purely past wrongs with no forward-looking component. (MTD at 12.) Here

 6   an actual controversy remains live: Plaintiff's certification remains denied; he still needs a lawful

 7   certification decision to pursue U nonimmigrant status; and the parties dispute whether the April

 8   20 denial complied with constitutional and statutory standards. A new decision on a correct legal

 9   standard is forward-looking relief, not a retrospective damages substitute.

11   F. The Related-Case Narrative Does Not Defeat the FAC.

12

13          Defendant argues that Judge Fickes's denial of Plaintiff's judicial U-visa certification

14   motion in Case No. 26CV168913, Plaintiff's voluntary dismissals of earlier actions, and an

15   Eastern District report and recommendation show that retaliation is implausible and that leave to

16   amend would be futile. (MTD at 11, 16-17.) Plaintiff accepts those procedural facts for purposes

17   of this opposition. They still do not warrant dismissal.

18

19   1. Extrinsic Materials Are Improper on a Bare Rule 12(b)(6) Record.

20

21          On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the court generally may not consider materials outside the

22   pleadings. Lee v. City of Los Angeles, 250 F.3d 668, 688-89 (9th Cir. 2001). Judicial notice may

23   reach an order's existence but not the truth of disputed matters recited within it, and may not be

24   used to supply missing elements or to resolve competing inferences against the plaintiff. Khoja v.

25   Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc., 899 F.3d 988, 998-1003 (9th Cir. 2018). Defendant submits no

26   request for judicial notice, no declaration, and no exhibits. Even if the Court takes notice that

27   Judge Fickes denied a certification motion and that other cases were dismissed, those facts do not

28   establish-much less conclusively establish-that Serrato's April 20 denial was lawful or

                                                  Page 12
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   non-retaliatory. What Defendant actually asks is that the Court draw adverse inferences about

 2   Plaintiff's credibility and litigation motives from materials the Court cannot test on this record.

 3   That is the functional equivalent of a credibility determination-exactly what Rule 12(b)(6)

 4   forbids.

 5

 6   2. Judge Fickes's Denial Does Not Resolve This Case.

 7

 8           Accepting that Judge Fickes denied Plaintiff's motion for judicial U-visa certification on

 9   June 18, 2026, that denial does not defeat the FAC for at least four independent reasons.

10

11           First, different certifying official. Section 679.10 and 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(2) authorize

12   multiple categories of certifying officials, including judges and law-enforcement supervisors.

13   Judge Fickes's decision concerned whether a superior-court judge should certify Form I-918

14   Supplement B in a stayed civil tort action. This case concerns whether Captain

15   Serrato-Commander of the CHP Dublin Area, supervisor of the accused officers, and the official

16   who signed the April 20, 2026 denial-violated Plaintiff's constitutional rights in performing that

17   agency certification function. One certifying official's denial does not immunize another's

18   allegedly retaliatory or arbitrary denial.

19

20           Second, different legal question. The Fickes motion asked a state judge to issue an

21   affirmative certification. This action asks whether Serrato's existing denial was

22   unconstitutional-because it was motivated by retaliation for protected petitioning and because the

23   denial process ignored statutory explanation and evidence-engagement requirements. A "no" on

24   judicial certification is not a "yes" on the lawfulness of Serrato's motive or process. Even if Judge

25   Fickes's denial was substantively correct, that would not immunize Serrato's allegedly retaliatory

26   or procedurally defective denial. The constitutional violation is in the how, not just the what.

27

28           Third, different record posture and conflict. Serrato decided the request while personally

                                                   Page 13
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   supervising the accused officers, after acknowledging Plaintiff's POST/citizen complaint, and

 2   while those officers were defendants in pending civil suits. (FAC ¶¶ 48-49.) Judge Fickes did not

 3   occupy that structural conflict. The retaliation and bias allegations that this Court found plausible

 4   at screening are specific to Serrato.

 5

 6             Fourth, no preclusion showing. Defendant has not pleaded or briefed claim preclusion,

 7   issue preclusion, or Rooker-Feldman. He offers the Fickes denial as atmosphere for a futility /

 1   G. Qualified Immunity Does Not Apply.

 2

 3          As a threshold matter, qualified immunity does not reach Plaintiff's official-capacity

 4   claims for prospective injunctive and declaratory relief. Defendant's own notice limits qualified

 5   immunity to his individual capacity. (MTD at 2, 14.) Qualified immunity provides no defense to

 6   those official-capacity prospective claims.

 7

 8          As to the individual-capacity claims, qualified immunity protects officials "from liability

 9   for civil damages insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or

10   constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Pearson v. Callahan, 555

11   U.S. 223, 231 (2009). Dismissal on qualified-immunity grounds at the 12(b)(6) stage is

12   inappropriate "unless the court can determine from the complaint alone that qualified immunity

13   applies." Keates v. Koile, 883 F.3d 1228, 1235 (9th Cir. 2018). It cannot do so here.

14

15   1. Defendant Violated Constitutional Rights.

16

17          As set forth above, the FAC adequately alleges violations of substantive and procedural

18   due process and First Amendment retaliation. This Court has already found these allegations

19   sufficient: the complaint "supports the plausible inferences that CHP officers falsely imprisoned

20   him in November 2024 based on fabricated evidence" and that "Defendant Serrato retaliated

21   against him by wrongfully denying his certification request." (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.)

23   2. The Unlawfulness Was Clearly Established.

24

25          The clearly established law is clearest on retaliation-and that is enough to defeat qualified

26   immunity on the lead claim.

27

28          First, it was clearly established by April 2026 that a government official may not deny a

                                                   Page 15
               OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   discretionary benefit in retaliation for protected petitioning. Perry v. Sindermann, 408 U.S. 593,

 2   597 (1972); Bd. of Cnty. Comm'rs v. Umbehr, 518 U.S. 668, 674 (1996); Borough of Duryea v.

 3   Guarnieri, 564 U.S. 379, 387 (2011); Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250, 256 (2006). The

 4   analogue here is concrete: denying a statutory certification benefit because the applicant filed

 5   complaints and litigation against officers under the official's supervision. Defendant's own

 6   authority confirms the elements: Ford v. City of Yakima, 706 F.3d at 1193. Capp, 940 F.3d at

 7   1055, reversed dismissal of a retaliation claim on analogous pleading of retaliatory motive.

 8   Wesby's caution against defining clearly established law at too high a level of generality, 583

 9   U.S. at 64, does not require a prior case about U-visa certifications; it requires that the

10   unlawfulness follow from established principles. Retaliatory denial of a discretionary benefit for

11   protected petitioning is not a close call-especially after this Court stated that Serrato "did not

12   have discretion to retaliate." (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.)

14           Second, as to the secondary due-process theories, section 679.10's specific

15   written-explanation and non-dispositive-absence-of-charges requirements gave Defendant fair

16   notice that a boilerplate denial ignoring the agency's own evidence would violate those

17   constraints. Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002). Those theories are preserved; they are not

18   the lead clearly-established path.

20   H. If Any Claim Is Dismissed, Leave to Amend Must Be Granted.

21

22           Defendant argues leave to amend should be denied because Plaintiff has "substantial

23   experience with the legal process," has already amended once, and has pursued related litigation.

24   (MTD at 16-17.) The standard does not turn on litigation history or amendment count; it turns on

25   whether amendment would be futile. "[T]he rule favoring liberality in amendments to pleadings

26   is particularly important for the pro se litigant." Lopez v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1131 (9th Cir.

27   2000) (en banc). Leave should be granted unless it is "absolutely clear that the deficiencies of the

28   complaint could not be cured by amendment." Id. at 1137 (quoting Noll v. Carlson, 809 F.2d

                                                    Page 16
                OPPOSITION TO MTD / MOTION TO STRIKE -- NEWANFORBI v. SERRATO (N.D. Cal.)
 1   1446, 1448 (9th Cir. 1987)). This Court granted leave to file the First Amended Complaint. A

 2   single prior amendment does not establish futility; the question is whether the deficiencies could

 3   be cured, not how many times the complaint has been amended. Defendant has not demonstrated

 4   futility.

 5

 6               Even accepting Defendant's related-case narrative in full, it does not show futility. The

 7   Eastern District report and recommendation addressed different claims against different

 8   defendants and never became a final judgment-the action was dismissed without prejudice. Judge

10   liability for a retaliatory or arbitrary agency denial. The pending state writ petition seeks

11   mandamus against CHP under state law, not Section 1983 damages against Serrato. And this

12   Court has already held the operative theory here plausible. (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.)

13

15   cross-references for the enumerated federal qualifying crimes (FAC ¶ 21), any such pleading gap

16   is curable by amendment and is not a basis for dismissal with prejudice. The federal

17   categories-false imprisonment, unlawful criminal restraint, felonious assault, perjury, and

18   obstruction of justice-are pleaded under 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(U)(iii) and were accepted as

19   plausible at screening. (Dkt. No. 5 at 4.) Denial of leave to amend based on characterizations of

20   other litigation-particularly where this Court has already screened these claims as

21   sufficient-would be an abuse of discretion.
