17    I.     THE ALLEGATIONS WITHIN THE COMPLAINT SHOW THAT PROBABLE
             CAUSE EXISTED AT THE TIME OF PLAINTIFF’S SEARCH AND ARREST.
18

19           Although Plaintiff claims, without any factual support, that the initial welfare
20    check on Plaintiff was “pretextual,” the Opposition concedes that the alleged
21    seizure of Plaintiff did not begin until one officer stated that he believes there was
22    booze in a cup in Plaintiff’s vehicle and ordered Plaintiff out of his vehicle for field
23    sobriety tests. (Opposition p.3:26-p.4:11.)
24           Taking all well-pleaded factual allegations within the Complaint as true, no
25    constitutional violation exists. Although the Opposition argues that Defendants
26    Gonzalez and Melano’s (collectively “Individual Defendants”) “warrantless entry
27    [into Plaintiff’s car] and their “search [of the car] lacking probable cause[,] id. at
28    p.4:6, violated Plaintiff’s Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights because “a cup

 1    in a parked car is not contraband, and speculation about its contents is not a lawful

 3    facts supporting the existence of probable cause justifying both the search of

15    II.   THE CONCLUSIONS OF LAW AND CONCLUSORY ALLEGATIONS WITHIN
            THE COMPLAINT ARE NOT ENTITLED TO THE PRESUMPTION OF TRUTH
16          ON A MOTION TO DISMISS.

17          Plaintiff argues that Defendants’ Motion to Dismiss relies on facts that are
18    contradicted by the Complaint, which is improper at the Motion to Dismiss stage.
19    Opposition p.11:3-11. Specifically, the Opposition states that “[In the Complaint],
20    Plaintiff alleges that the officers’ suspicions were based on nothing more than a
21    speculative remark about a cup. that the cup turned out not to contain alcohol, that
22    any odor of alcohol is a fabrication, and that an empty can was found only as a
23    result of the unlawful search (not in plain view) ...[.]” Id. at 11:11-15. A review of
24    the Complaint shows that none of these allegations actually appear in the Complaint
25    and the Opposition provides no citations showing otherwise. In fact, the Complaint
26    appears to directly contradict the Opposition’s assertion that “an empty can was
27    found only as a result of the unlawful search (not in plain view) …” as Paragraph
28    11 of the Complaint alleges that, “upon observing a upon observing [sic] a cup in

 1    the center console, [an officer] stated, ‘I think it's booze,’ to which the other officer
 2    replied, ‘Yeah?’” Complaint ¶ 11. Furthermore, even had Plaintiff alleged in his
 3    Complaint that “any odor of alcohol is a fabrication” or that “the officers’
 4    suspicions were based on nothing more than a speculative remark about a cup,”
 5    such allegations would not be entitled to the presumption of truth for purposes of a
 6    Motion to Dismiss as they are “mere conclusions” unsupported by specific facts.
 7    Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 663 (2009). For example, the Opposition’s
 8    assertion that “the officers’ suspicions were based on nothing more than a
 9    speculative remark about a cup” is demonstratively false, as the Complaint also
10    alleges that Individual Defendants first encountered Plaintiff while he was “resting”
11    in his vehicle on the side of the freeway after midnight. Complaint ¶ 10.
12         Plaintiff’s allegation within the Complaint that “[n]o evidence of intoxication
13    or illegal activity was found” is also not subject to the presumption of truth for the
14    same reason. Complaint ¶ 16. To satisfy the standard set in Iqbal for well-pleaded
15    allegations, Plaintiff must provide specific facts showing that no evidence of
16    intoxication was found. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 664. (“While legal conclusions can
17    provide the complaint's framework, they must be supported by factual allegations.”)
18    Notably, the Opposition also fails to respond to Defendants’ question within the
19    moving papers of just how Plaintiff knew that he did not display any signs of
20    intoxication as the Complaint is bereft of any specifics regarding Plaintiff’s
21    performance on the field sobriety tests or his interaction with Individual
22    Defendants.
23         DEFENDANTS ARE ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY AS
          PLAINTIFF FAILED TO SHOW THAT DEFENDANTS VIOLATED
24                    “CLEARLY ESTABLISHED” LAW.
25         As stated in the moving papers, even had there been a constitutional violation,
26    Individual Defendants would still be entitled to qualified immunity because they
27    did not violate any clearly established law. The Opposition fails to cite to any case
28    law that would have put Individual Defendants on notice that their actions in this

 1    situation were unconstitutionally “beyond debate.” District of Columbia v. Wesby,
 2    583 U.S. 48, 64 (2018). Instead, the Opposition states that the law on “arrests
 3    without probable cause has been clearly established for over fifty years” and that
 4    what Individual Defendants did here was “so obviously illegal” that prior case law
 5    with identical facts is not required. Opposition at 9:7-10;18-20. Plaintiff
 6    misunderstands the clearly established prong of qualified immunity. “Clearly
 7    established” law means precedent existing at the time of the conduct that “‘squarely
 8    governs the specific facts’ at issue,” Kisela v. Hughes, 584 U.S.100, 104 (2018).
 9    ‘“[C]ourts must not define clearly established law at a high level of generality[.]’”
10    Wesby, 583 U.S. at 63.
11         Here, stripped of Plaintiff’s improper allegations that the search of his vehicle
12    and his arrest were conducted without probable cause, the facts alleged in the
13    Complaint show Plaintiff was arrested for DUI following field sobriety tests and
14    questioning by Individual Defendants. This is not a case involving obvious illegal
15    conduct by Defendants. As stated in the moving papers, the fact that a prosecutor
16    declined to press charges against Plaintiff has no bearing on the qualified immunity
17    analysis. Plaintiff has cited no authority to the contrary. Although Plaintiff claims
18    that the Individual Defendants conducted a “warrantless, non-consensual search of
19    a vehicle without probable cause, after the only suspicion (the cup) was dispelled”
20    this is not the case. Opposition p.9:26-28. As shown by the allegations within the
21    Complaint, Individual Defendants searched the vehicle after conducting field
22    sobriety tests on Plaintiff and had more than the presence of the cup to establish
23    probable cause. Moreover, the Opposition alleges that the cup (or can) was “not in
24    plain view” and found only after the search. Opposition 11:14-15. Under these
25    circumstances, even if Individual Defendants were mistaken as to the existence of
26    probable cause to search Plaintiff’s vehicle or arrest Plaintiff for DUI, they are still
27    entitled to qualified immunity as they have not violated any clearly established law.
28    The question of qualified immunity is appropriately decided on a Motion to
