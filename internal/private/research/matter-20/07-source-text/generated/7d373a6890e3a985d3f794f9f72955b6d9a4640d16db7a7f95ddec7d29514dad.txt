 2                                         I. INTRODUCTION

 3          Captain Serrato denied Petitioner's request for certification on one ground: that the

 4   November 1, 2024 incident involved no "qualifying criminal activity" under Penal Code section

 5   679.10. (Pet. Ex. 1.) He did not find that Petitioner was unhelpful, and he relied on no other

 6   ground. The denial therefore stands or falls on a single premise: that Petitioner's arrest and

 7   four-day custody were lawful. If they were not, the custody was false imprisonment, an offense

 8   the statute lists by name, and subdivision (g)(1) required certification.

 9          This Court has identified how that premise is tested. If the Court credits Petitioner's

10   showing that the officers' reported observations were untrue, "it could reasonably find probable

11   cause for arrest was lacking, which could support a finding that the CHP abused its discretion."

12   (Order, Aug. 29, 2026, p. 4.) The record now contains what the Court did not have at demurrer:

13   CHP's verified Answer, CHP's own production of its arrest report, warrant affidavit and

14   laboratory results, and the lodged recording.

15          That record does not support every claim in Petitioner's earlier papers, and this motion

16   does not repeat them. It does show that CHP's report misstates the field sobriety testing on

17   which the arrest decision expressly relied; that no officer mentioned the reported odor of alcohol

18   even while examining the vehicle's contents by smell; and that the arresting officer told

19   Petitioner, seconds before handcuffing him, that whether to arrest remained within his

20   discretion. Petitioner asks the Court to decide whether the arrest was supported by probable

21   cause and to issue the writ that follows. If that question cannot be decided on the papers,

22   Petitioner asks for a hearing limited to the facts identified in Part V.E.

14                                    IV. STANDARD OF REVIEW

15          Traditional mandate lies to compel performance of a duty the law specially enjoins and

16   to correct an abuse of discretion. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1085, subd. (a), 1086.) The Court asks

17   whether the agency's decision was "arbitrary, capricious, entirely lacking in evidentiary support,

18   or unlawfully or procedurally unfair" (Western States Petroleum Assn. v. Superior Court (1995)

19   9 Cal.4th 559, 574), and whether the agency adequately considered all relevant factors (

20   American Coatings Assn. v. South Coast Air Quality Management Dist. (2012) 54 Cal.4th 446,

21   [pin cite]). Because CHP acted informally and the facts are disputed, the Court may receive

22   evidence beyond the materials CHP considered. (Western States, at p. 576.) Mandate does not

23   ordinarily dictate how discretion is exercised, but it compels a particular act when only one

24   lawful choice remains. (Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442.)

25   Where an essential fact is disputed, the Court may try that issue. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1090.)

26   Petitioner bears the burden of proof.

 1                                             V. ARGUMENT

 2   A. The denial rests on one reviewable premise: that the custody was lawful.

 3             Section 679.10 lists "[u]nlawful criminal restraint" and "[f]alse imprisonment" as

 4   qualifying criminal activity and reaches offenses whose nature and elements are substantially

 5   similar. (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subds. (c)(18)-(19), (d).) It contains no exception for restraint

 6   imposed by peace officers. False imprisonment is "the unlawful violation of the personal liberty

 7   of another." (Pen. Code, § 236.) A public officer who, under color of authority but without

 8   lawful authority, arrests or detains a person against his will commits a misdemeanor (Pen. Code,

 9   § 146, subd. (a)), an offense substantially similar to false imprisonment. A warrantless arrest is

10   lawful only if supported by probable cause. (Pen. Code, § 836; Veh. Code, § 40300.5.)

11   Certification requires no charge, prosecution or conviction. (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (k)(1).)

12             The restraint is admitted: Petitioner was handcuffed, transported, and jailed for four

13   days. (Answer ¶¶ 39-41.) The only contested element is lawful authority, which turns on

14   whether, at 1:12 a.m., the facts known to the officers "would persuade someone of 'reasonable

15   caution'" that Petitioner had driven under the influence. (People v. Thompson (2006) 38 Cal.4th

16   811, 818.) CHP's denial necessarily answered that question yes. Reviewing the denial requires

17   the Court to answer it on the evidence.

18   B. On the full record, the probable-cause showing is materially weaker than CHP's report

19   states.

20             1. The report misstates the field sobriety tests on which the arrest expressly relied.

21   Of the four tests the report lists, two were never performed. The report attributes the

22   walk-and-turn to Petitioner's choice and his statement that it was "difficult." (AGO 000031.)

23   The recording shows the opposite: Petitioner said he thought he could do it, and Officer Melano

24   said he "wouldn't do that one" on the uneven shoulder. (Pet. Ex. 5 at 01:10:23-01:10:30.) The

25   report then states that the tests were "explained and demonstrated" and not performed, and lists

26   "poor performance on the FST's" as a basis for arrest (AGO 000032); the affidavit made the

27   same representation to the magistrate (AGO 000049). As to the walk-and-turn, that account is

28   not accurate. The point is narrow, but it concerns the category of evidence the report itself

 1   identifies as a reason for the arrest, and it shows the report cannot be accepted without

 2   comparison to the recording.

 3          2. The odor entries are absent where the recording would capture them. The report

 4   states that the officer smelled "the distinct odor of an alcoholic beverage emitting from within

 5   the vehicle" when the door was opened. (AGO 000032.) Within two minutes, Officer Melano

 6   held the open cup from inside that vehicle, smelled it, and identified the odor aloud as urine.

 7   (Pet. Ex. 5 at 00:58:49-00:59:04.) In roughly fifteen minutes of recorded pre-arrest questioning,

 8   no officer mentions alcohol odor, and CHP admits as much. (Answer ¶ 44.) Petitioner does not

 9   contend that an unspoken observation is necessarily false. But the officers' recorded attention to

10   the smell of the vehicle's contents, without mention of the "strong" odor later reported, is

11   evidence the Court may weigh in deciding whether that entry is reliable.

12          3. The recorded speech and orientation can be assessed directly. The report describes

13   slurred speech and confusion. (AGO 000030, 000032.) The recording captures Petitioner

14   identifying his location, reading signs, spelling his surname, and giving an extended, organized

15   explanation of why he stopped to sleep. (Pet. Ex. 5 at 00:59:57, 01:00:41-01:00:46,

16   01:01:21-01:01:56; Answer ¶¶ 37, 46.) The officers found him asleep (AGO 000031);

17   disorientation on being awakened near 1:00 a.m. is equally consistent with the fatigue he

18   described. The Court can listen to the lodged recording and decide.

19          4. The arresting officer treated the arrest decision as open until the voluntary test

20   was declined. Before Petitioner answered, the officer told him that if he declined the PAS test,

21   "it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not." Handcuffing began

22   one second after he declined. (Pet. Ex. 5 at 01:11:03-01:11:16.) Probable cause is measured

23   objectively, and the officer's state of mind does not control. (Devenpeck v. Alford (2004) 543

24   U.S. 146, 153.) But his contemporaneous words are evidence of how strong the observable

25   signs were at the time. In his own judgment, the decision was still open until Petitioner

26   exercised a choice the officer had just told him he was free to make.

27          5. CHP's remaining evidence does not end the inquiry. CHP will rely on the HGN

28   and Romberg results, red and watery eyes, a wristband, admitted driving from a party, the

28   concerned the investigation of him, not the investigation of the officers. CHP's denial did not
