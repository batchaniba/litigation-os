 2            California requires parolees convicted of certain sex offenses to complete, as a condition of
 3   parole, a sex offender management program. (Compl.), ECF No. 1 ¶ 14; see Cal. Penal Code
 4   § 3008(d)(2). Plaintiff, who is one of these parolees, was referred to Hope for enrollment in sex
 5   offender group therapy in accordance with this requirement. Id. ¶¶ 11, 13. Disgruntled over his
 6   inability to complete the program, Plaintiff has filed multiple lawsuits against Hope and/or its
 7   employees in the past year, each challenging this condition of his parole. One of his lawsuits, filed
 8   in the San Joaquin County Superior Court, was recently resolved in Hope’s favor when the court
 9   sustained Hope’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s first amended compliant without leave to amend for failure
10   to allege sufficient facts to state a cause of action. 1 Undeterred, Plaintiff filed the instant action less
11   than one week after the adverse state court decision. Although Plaintiff now asserts violation of his
12   federal rights—the San Joaquin Superior Court action advanced only state law claims—the complaint
13   filed in this case contains essentially the same factual allegations as the one Plaintiff filed in state
14   court.
15            The San Joaquin Superior Court correctly held that plaintiff’s claims were subject to dismissal
16   for failure to state a claim. Because the dismissal of the state court action is entitled to full faith and
17   credit, Plaintiff’s claims in this action are barred under the doctrine of res judicata. Alternatively,
18   Plaintiff’s complaint should be dismissed because it fails to include sufficient factual allegations to
19   state a claim for relief. For these reasons, Defendants’ motion to dismiss should be granted.

 3                                    II. LEGAL STANDARDS
 4          Pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (“Rules”), a defendant may
 5   move to dismiss a complaint for “failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.” Fed. R.
 6   Civ. P. 12(b)(6). A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests the legal sufficiency of the complaint. N. Star Int’l v.
 7   Ariz. Corp. Comm’n, 720 F.2d 578, 581 (9th Cir. 1983). “Dismissal can be based on the lack of a
 8   cognizable legal theory or the absence of sufficient facts alleged under a cognizable legal theory.”
 9   Balistreri v. Pacifica Police Dep’t, 901 F.2d 696, 699 (9th Cir. 1990). A complaint must allege
10   “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550
11   U.S. 544, 570 (2007). “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that
12   allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
13   alleged.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). “Where a complaint pleads facts that are
14   merely consistent with a defendant’s liability, it stops short of the line between possibility and
15   plausibility of entitlement to relief.” Id. (citations and quotation marks omitted).
16          When presented with a motion to dismiss pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6), the court accepts as true
17   the allegations in the complaint and construes them in the light most favorable to the plaintiff. Hishon
18   v. King & Spalding, 467 U.S. 69, 73 (1984); Love v. United States, 915 F.2d 1242, 1245 (9th Cir.
19   1989). The court, however, need not assume the truth of legal conclusions cast in the form of factual
20   allegations. United States ex rel. Chunie v. Ringrose, 788 F.2d 638, 643 n.2 (9th Cir. 1986). Although
21   Rule 8(a) does not require detailed factual allegations, “it demands more than an unadorned, the-
22   defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.
23          Thus, a complaint is insufficient if it offers mere “labels and conclusions” or “a formulaic
24   recitation of the elements of a cause of action.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555; see also Iqbal, 556 U.S.
25   at 676 (“Threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory
26   statements, do not suffice.”). “Moreover, it is inappropriate to assume the plaintiff ‘can prove facts
27   that it has not alleged or that the defendants have violated the . . . laws in ways that have not been
28   alleged.” Arroyo v. LL Folsom, L.P., 630 F. Supp. 3d 1346, 1349 (E.D. Cal. 2022) (quoting

10   A.      Claim Preclusion
11           “[F]ederal courts are required to give state court judgments the preclusive effects they would
12   be given by another court of that state.” Brodheim v. Cry, 584 F.3d 1262, 1268 (9th Cir. 2009). This
13   requires federal courts to apply the relevant state preclusion rules to assess the preclusive effect of
14   the state court’s decision. Miofsky v. Super. Ct. of Cal., 703 F.2d 332, 336 (9th Cir. 1983). In
15   California, res judicata “has a double aspect,” encompassing both claim preclusion and collateral
16   estoppel. People v. Barragan, 32 Cal. 4th 236, 252 (2004). Claim preclusion bars a second lawsuit
17   between the same parties on the same cause of action, while collateral estoppel, also referred to as
18   issue preclusion, bars relitigation of an issue that was actually litigated and determined in the first
19   action. Id. at 252-53. The elements for both claim and issue preclusion “are the same: (1) A claim
20   or issue raised in the present action is identical to a claim or issue litigated in a prior proceeding; (2)
21   the prior proceeding resulted in a final judgment on the merits; and (3) the party against whom the
22   doctrine is being asserted was a party or in privity with a party to the prior proceeding.” Boeken v.
23   Philip Morris USA, Inc., 48 Cal. 4th 788, 797 (2010) (quoting Barragan, 32 Cal. 4th at 252-53).
24           Here, each of these elements is satisfied. With respect to the first element—that the present
25   case is identical to a claim or issue litigated in a prior proceeding—“California courts have
26   ‘consistently applied the ‘primary rights’ theory.’” Id. (quoting Slater v. Blackwood, 15 Cal. 3d 791,
27   795 (1975)). Under California’s “primary rights” theory, “a cause of action is (1) a primary right
28   possessed by the plaintiff, (2) a corresponding primary duty devolving upon the defendant, and (3) a

10          1.      42 U.S.C. § 1983
11          Plaintiff’s first and second claims allege violations of his First and Fourteenth Amendment
12   rights under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Both claims are deficient in multiple respects. First, the complaint’s
13   allegations fail to demonstrate that Defendants are state actors. Second, Plaintiff’s allegations do not
14   establish a violation of his First and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
15                  a.      State Actor
16          A plaintiff does not have a cause of action directly under the United States Constitution.
17   Livadas v. Bradshaw, 512 U.S. 107, 132 (1994) (affirming 42 U.S.C. § 1983 provides federal cause
18   of action for the deprivation of rights secured by the United States Constitution). Instead, 42 U.S.C.
19   § 1983 provides the “mechanism for vindicating federal or constitutional rights.” Stilwell v. City of
20   Williams, 831 F.3d 1234, 1240 (9th Cir. 2016). To succeed on a section 1983 claim, a plaintiff must
21   establish “the violation of a right secured by the Constitution and laws of the United States, and . . .
22   that the . . . deprivation was committed by a person acting under color of state law.” West v. Atkins,
23   487 U.S. 42, 48 (1988); see Leer v. Murphy, 844 F.2d 628, 632-33 (9th Cir. 1988) (“Section 1983
24   imposes two essential proof requirements upon a claimant: (1) that a person acting under color of
25   state law committed the conduct at issue, and (2) that the conduct deprived the claimant of some right,
26   privilege, or immunity protected by the Constitution or laws of the United States.”).

 1          “When addressing whether a private party acted under color of law, [courts] start with the
 2   presumption that private conduct does not constitute governmental action.” Sutton v. Providence St.
 3   Joseph Med. Ctr., 192 F.3d 826, 835 (9th Cir. 1999); see also Am. Mfrs. Mut. Ins. Co. v. Sullivan,
 4   526 U.S. 40, 50 (1999) (“[T]he under-color-of-state-law element of § 1983 excludes from its reach
 5   merely private conduct, no matter how discriminatory or wrongful.”) (quotations omitted). That
 6   presumption can be rebutted “if, though only if, there is such a ‘close nexus between the State and
 7   the [private entity’s] challenged action’ that seemingly private behavior ‘may be fairly treated as that
 8   of the State itself.’” Brentwood Acad. v. Tennessee Secondary Sch. Athletic Ass’n, 531 U.S. 288, 295
 9   (2001) (quoting Jackson v. Metro. Edison Co., 419 U.S. 345, 351 (1974)).
10          Evaluating whether private conduct may be fairly attributable to the State is a fact-intensive
11   inquiry. Lee v. Katz, 276 F.3d 550, 554 (9th Cir. 2002). “The Supreme Court has developed four
12   different tests that aid [courts] in identifying state action: (1) public function; (2) joint action; (3)
13   governmental compulsion or coercion; and (4) governmental nexus.” Pasadena Republican Club v.
14   Western Just. Ctr., 985 F.3d 1161, 1167 (9th Cir. 2021) (quotations omitted). “‘Satisfaction of any
15   one test is sufficient to find state action, so long as no countervailing factor exists.’” Rawson v.
16   Recovery Innovations, Inc., 975 F.3d 742, 747 (9th Cir. 2020) (quoting Kirtley v. Rainey, 326 F.3d
17   1088, 1092 (9th Cir. 2003)). Importantly, Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing that Defendants
18   are state actors. See Florer v. Congregation Pidyon Shevuyim, N.A., 639 F.3d 916, 922 (9th Cir.
19   2011) (citing Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149, 156 (1978)). “Only in rare circumstances
20   can a private party be viewed as a ‘state actor’ for § 1983 purposes.” Harvey v. Harvey, 949 F.2d
21   1127, 1130 (11th Cir. 1992).

24   test. See ECF No. 1 ¶ 18-19. “Under the public function test, when private individuals or groups are

26   instrumentalities of the State and subject to its constitutional limitations.” Lee, 276 F.3d at 555
27   (quotations omitted). “The public function test is satisfied only on a showing that the function at
28   issue is both traditionally and exclusively governmental.” Rawson, 975 F.3d at 747 (quotations

13           2.      Bane Act Claim
14           Plaintiff’s third claim alleges violation of California’s Bane Act. ECF No. 1 at 17-18. Section
15   52.1 of that Act provides a private right of action for damages against any person, “whether or not
16   acting under color of law,” who “interferes” or “attempts to interfere by threat, intimidation, or
17   coercion, with the exercise or enjoyment by any individual or individuals of rights secured by the
18   Constitution or laws of the United States, or of the rights secured by the Constitution or laws [of

20   specified improper means (i.e., ‘threats, intimidation or coercion’), tried to or did prevent the plaintiff
21   from doing something he or she had the right to do under the law or to force the plaintiff to do
22   something that he or she was not required to do under the law.” Austin B. v. Escondido Union School

27   1193, 1211 (N.D. Cal. 2014). If the plaintiff sufficiently alleges that the defendants violated his
28   constitutional or statutory right, only then will “the Court consider whether such interference was the

 8          3.      Fraud and Negligent Misrepresentation Claim
 9          Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is styled as “Fraud and Negligent Misrepresentation.”
10   Notwithstanding his inclusion of the word “negligent,” Plaintiff appears to be alleging a claim for
11   either fraudulent concealment or fraudulent misrepresentation. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants
12   misrepresented to Plaintiff and CDCR that he “had an individualized path to treatment completion,
13   and that his non-completion status reflected individualized clinical assessment, and that he had only
14   ‘partially completed’ homework packets.’” ECF No. 1 ¶ 65. Plaintiff further alleges that Defendants
15   “concealed: (a) the blanket retention policy; (b) the October 18, 2023 Tassone Treatment Completion
16   Plan, which was placed in Plaintiff’s file and sent to CDCR but never shown to Plaintiff; and (c) that
17   HOPE’s internal policy, not Plaintiff's performance, precluded completion.” Id.
18          Plaintiff’s claim, whether construed as one for fraudulent misrepresentation or fraudulent
19   concealment claim, fails because Plaintiff does not allege facts showing that Defendants intended to
20   defraud him or that he justifiably relied on any misrepresentation or omission. To state a claim for
21   either fraudulent concealment or fraudulent misrepresentation under California law, a plaintiff is
22   required to allege “‘(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b)
23   knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e. to induce reliance; (d) justifiable
24   reliance; and (e) resulting damage.’” DZ Reserve v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 96 F.4th 1223, 1233 (9th
25   Cir. 2024) (quoting Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc., 15 Cal. 4th 951, 974 (1997)).
26          For claims based on fraud, a plaintiff must also satisfy the heightened pleading requirements
27   imposed by Rule 9(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Zetz v. Boston Scientific Corp., 398
28   F. Supp. 3d 700, 713 (E.D. Cal. 2019). That rule provides that “[i]n alleging fraud or mistake, a party

 5          4.      Civil RICO Claim
 6          Plaintiff’s last claim alleges violation of the Racketeering Influenced and Corrupt
 7   Organization Act (“RICO”), 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c). To state a civil RICO claim, plaintiff is required
 8   to allege: “a plaintiff must allege (1) conduct (2) of an enterprise (3) through a pattern (4) of
 9   racketeering activity.” Sanford v. MemberWorks, Inc., 625 F.3d 550, 557 (9th Cir. 2010). The
10   alleged enterprise must exist “separate and apart from that inherent in the perpetration of the alleged
11   [activity].” Chang v. Chen, 80 F.3d 1293, 1300-01 (9th Cir. 1996).
12          A “pattern of racketeering activity” requires at least two criminal acts proscribed by Title 18
13   of the United States Code, including, among others, mail fraud, wire fraud, and obstruction of justice.
14   Id. These so-called predicate acts, if based on fraudulent conduct, must be alleged with specificity
15   in accordance with Rule(9) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Schreiber Distrib. Co. v. Serv-
16   Well Furniture Co., 806 F.2d 1393, 1400-01 (9th Cir. 2004). “To avoid dismissal for inadequacy
17   under Rule 9(b),” a plaintiff must “state the time, place, and specific content of the false
18   representations as well as the identities of the parties to the misrepresentation.” Edwards v. Marin
19   Park, Inc., 356 F.3d 1058, 1066 (9th Cir. 2004). “A party alleging fraud must ‘set forth more than
20   the neutral facts necessary to identify the transaction.’” Kearns v. Ford Motor Co., 567 F.3d 1120,
21   1124 (9th Cir. 2009) (citing In re GlenFed, Inc. Sec. Litig., 42 F.3d 1541, 1548 (9th Cir. 1994).
22          Plaintiff alleges that Defendants engaged in three predicate acts: mail fraud, wire fraud, and
23   obstruction. ECF No. 1 at 20. With respect to the alleged mail and wire fraud, Plaintiff simply states
24   that from 2021 to present, Defendants transmitted, both electronically and through the United States
