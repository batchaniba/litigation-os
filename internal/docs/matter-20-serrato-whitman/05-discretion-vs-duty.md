# Discretion versus duty

## Ing's best discretion argument

CHP did not have a ministerial duty to certify merely because Petitioner submitted a request. The certifying official had to decide whether the record showed that Petitioner was a victim of qualifying criminal activity and met the other certification requirements. That evaluative judgment is discretionary. Traditional mandate cannot compel a particular exercise of discretion, and the court cannot replace CHP's statutory judgment with its own.

On this record, CHP can say the one-page denial identified the dispositive ground: the incident did not meet the standard for qualifying criminal activity or a substantially similar activity. The report supplied facts supporting an impaired-driving investigation; the recording confirms a parked driver, an alcohol container, incomplete field testing, refusal of a voluntary PAS, and later refusal of chemical testing. A § 851.6 detention certificate states that no criminal complaint would issue; it does not adjudicate that officers committed a crime. The report-versus-video disputes also do not establish the oath, intent, material falsity, and nexus needed for perjury or obstruction, and the blood draw does not establish felony assault. Because the denial was not based on noncooperation, the helpfulness presumption and detailed noncooperation explanation did not decide the request. Pins: AG MPA; CHP demurrer reply; August 29 order; operative petition Exs. 1, 5–7.

## Remand theory that survives that argument

Discretion is reviewable even when the court may not dictate the result. *Saleeby v. State Bar* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547, 562 holds that traditional mandate cannot compel a particular method of exercising discretion but can correct an abuse of discretion or action beyond legal power. *Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors* (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442–446 likewise separates an order to act lawfully from an order selecting the discretionary outcome.

Whitman's August 29 order already fixes the procedural lane: Count 1 survives because, if the pleaded absence of alcohol odor and impairment, performance on the recorded tests, and alleged false reporting are proved, a factfinder could find probable cause lacking and CHP's refusal abusive. The merits presentation therefore should ask whether CHP actually considered the full record and applied the correct qualifying-activity standard—not ask Whitman to sign or command certification.

If the court finds the denial arbitrary, based on an incomplete record, or infected by a legal-standard error, it may vacate the denial and remand for a new decision that considers the pinned materials and exercises CHP's own discretion under the correct law. That remedy leaves the ultimate certification decision with CHP and remains available even if the court refuses to order certification outright.
