# MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

## I. INTRODUCTION AND ISSUE PRESENTED

This motion asks the Court to enforce the California Legislature's conditional command that a certifying official **shall certify** when the requesting person was the victim of qualifying criminal activity and has been, is being, or is likely to be helpful in its detection or investigation. (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (g)(1).) The claim is neither that an applicant's mere accusation compels certification nor that every Fourth Amendment violation is itself a qualifying offense. It is that CHP rejected a documented claim of **false imprisonment and unlawful criminal restraint** without a lawful basis for maintaining that rejection on the proven statutory and incident record.

The August 29, 2026 order overruled the demurrer to Count One, recognizing that credible proof undermining the factual basis for the November 2024 detention could support a finding that CHP abused its discretion in refusing certification. That ruling preserved an issue for decision; it did not itself establish the truth of the competing accounts. Petitioner asks for that merits determination now, and offers a sharply delimited factual comparison so the Court need not decide collateral claims or speculative officer motives.

Three matters deserve dispositive attention: the precise point at which an initially permissible highway welfare contact escalated to a search and compulsory detention; CHP's own contemporaneous records, including material statements about the field sobriety process; and whether the April 20 certification decision applied the correct crime-and-helpfulness criteria to information submitted on April 9. The relief sought is a final statutory remedy, with the narrow alternative of vacatur and lawful reconsideration if genuine discretion remains.

## II. UNDISPUTED DECISION AND MATERIAL RECORD

On November 1, 2024, officers Meza-Gonzalez and Melano encountered Petitioner in a stationary rental Tesla Model Y on the right shoulder of eastbound Interstate 580. The officers did not personally observe the Tesla being driven. The contact began as a welfare check. CHP Form 202 records a DUI investigation and an arrest at approximately 1:12 a.m., later transport, a warrant-authorized blood draw at 2:38 a.m., and jail booking. (Verified Pet., Ex. 6, CHP Form 202; October 23 production, AGO 000029–000033.) Petitioner disputes the existence of lawful cause for the involuntary restraint, not the fact that the arrest occurred.

On April 9, 2026, Petitioner requested certification on Form I-918, Supplement B and submitted a supporting memorandum and incident materials, including a timestamped recording transcript, the CHP report, and the Penal Code section 851.6 certificate. CHP acknowledged receipt April 10. Captain Serrato refused certification by letter of April 20, stating that the incident did not satisfy the standard for qualifying criminal activity. (Verified Pet., Exs. 1–3.) Petitioner does not contend that CHP missed the 30-day processing period, that the denial's brevity alone proves abuse, or that charges must have been filed.

## III. THE ORIGINAL CONTACT AND EACH EXPANSION OF POLICE AUTHORITY REQUIRE DISTINCT EVALUATION

The incident did not begin with a vehicle-code offense witnessed by the officers. Looking through an automobile window with a flashlight is not, standing alone, the same act as opening a door, reaching inside, directing the occupant outside, or applying handcuffs. The distinction is demonstrated by the submitted transcript of CHP's own recording:

At approximately 00:56:09, before speaking with Petitioner, Melano said, “I think it's booze,” apparently about a visible cup. The officers knocked on the passenger window. The knocking awakened Petitioner. At **00:56:40**, Melano instructed, “Open the window.” At **00:56:43**, the transcript describes **Melano opening the front passenger door**, before Petitioner had an opportunity to lower the glass. Only thereafter did the officers ask if he was okay and receive a response. (Verified Pet., Ex. 5, transcript at pp. 2–3, 00:56:07–00:56:49; Nforbi Decl. ¶¶ 3–5, once signed.) Petitioner did not consent to opening the door. This chronology is offered as evidence of what occurred, not as a categorical claim that officers may never check the welfare of a motorist asleep on a freeway shoulder.

The distinction matters because an opening and physical entry into the occupied passenger compartment require an objectively adequate justification separate from the mere ability to observe items through glass. (*United States v. Ngumezi* (9th Cir. 2020) 980 F.3d 1285, 1289–1291.) There may be circumstances supporting immediate aid to an unresponsive motorist; the Court must consider the facts existing **before** the door was opened, including the knock, the instruction to lower the glass, the actual opportunity to respond, any observable medical emergency, and roadway safety. Compare *Mardis v. Superior Court* (1963) 218 Cal.App.2d 70, 73–75 (officer possessed a prior report of apparently impaired driving by a matching vehicle). The present claim does not hinge on an officer's subjective suspicion alone.

The transcript then describes the cup being examined. Melano identified the liquid as urine at **00:58:51–00:59:04**. At **00:59:12**, Meza-Gonzalez directed or requested that Petitioner step out. At **00:59:46**, the transcript describes Melano opening the rear passenger door and searching while Petitioner was with Meza-Gonzalez. At **01:00:06–01:00:09**, Melano produced an empty Mike's Hard Lemonade can, which Petitioner denied consuming. (Pet., Ex. 5, transcript pp. 3–5.) The cup and can are distinct evidence. The empty can must be considered as part of the case; a discovery made in a later search cannot, merely because it was found, retrospectively establish the justification for an earlier physical intrusion.

The record also requires care concerning any parole-search theory. Petitioner affirmatively states that the officers had no earlier knowledge of his parole status. The available transcript first records dispatch communicating that status at **01:11:42**, after the initial entry and the 00:59:46 search; much later Melano refers to searching for weapons due to parole status. (Pet., Ex. 5, transcript pp. 12–14.) Knowledge of an applicable search condition acquired only afterward cannot retroactively authorize a search. (*People v. Sanders* (2003) 31 Cal.4th 318, 330.) Petitioner requests that CHP's contemporaneous dispatch and database timestamps be assessed so this factual chronology is resolved from the agency's own records rather than assumption. The Court need not resolve a standalone federal search claim to determine whether the ensuing **restraint of Petitioner's person** was without lawful authority.

## IV. CHP'S RECORDED EVIDENCE MATERIALLY UNDERCUTS THE ASSERTED GROUNDS FOR RESTRAINT

The central issue under the pleaded false-imprisonment theory is the absence of lawful authority to detain, handcuff, and imprison—not simply that the officers searched a vehicle. Penal Code section 236 defines false imprisonment as the unlawful violation of personal liberty. Penal Code section 146, subdivision (a), separately proscribes a public officer's detention under color of authority without lawful authority. Section 679.10(c)(18)–(19) expressly identifies **unlawful criminal restraint** and **false imprisonment** as qualifying activity. The certification question does not require this Court to try an officer criminally or to apply proof beyond a reasonable doubt merely because the alleged perpetrator was a peace officer.

CHP's narrative attributes strong alcohol odor, slurred speech, confusion, impaired performance and unwillingness to perform roadside tests to Petitioner. Those are material allegations, and their truth cannot be resolved by pretending they were never made. But the recording and CHP's own response place the reliability and meaning of several critical assertions in dispute. The October 12 Answer reportedly admits that neither officer verbally announced an alcohol odor on the recording and that Petitioner correctly identified Livermore and read road signs; it does not concede the absence of an actual odor or coherence at every moment. (Compare actual Answer ¶¶ 37, 44–45 before filing.) A court can evaluate Petitioner's audible speech and objective test instructions itself without confusing lack of narration with absence of sensation.

One affirmative discrepancy is particularly concrete. CHP Form 202 states Petitioner chose not to complete the walk-and-turn test because it was difficult. Yet the transcript records Petitioner saying at **01:10:23** that he believed he could do that test, and Melano intervening that the freeway shoulder was “uneven ground” and that he “wouldn't do that one.” Meza-Gonzalez agreed and moved on. (Pet., Ex. 5, transcript p. 11, 01:10:14–01:10:30; Ex. 6, CHP Form 202, AGO 000031.) If the original recording confirms that exchange, the Court should not count the resulting nonperformance as Petitioner refusing to attempt a test the officer himself deemed unsafe. This does not dispose of the reported HGN results, the Modified Romberg estimate, or other potential impairment indicators; it identifies a specific problem in CHP's explanation of one claimed arrest factor.

The sequence surrounding the voluntary preliminary alcohol screening (“PAS”) also warrants accurate treatment. Meza-Gonzalez told Petitioner the PAS was not required at **01:10:54**, Petitioner declined at **01:11:15**, and Meza-Gonzalez ordered him to turn around at **01:11:16**, with handcuffing described around **01:11:33**. (Pet., Ex. 5, transcript p. 12.) Refusal of a voluntary screening test is not itself a chemical-analysis result or a stand-alone demonstration that the person drove impaired. The Court should evaluate whether the preexisting observations supplied independent lawful cause for the arrest, rather than substituting the refusal for that analysis.

CHP will point to the occupied freeway shoulder, Petitioner's acknowledgment he previously drove from Oakland, the empty can, its reported odor and eye observations, HGN and Romberg observations, and the later blood result. Petitioner asks the Court to weigh **that actual totality**, including the contradictions and the limits of each piece of proof. The blood drawn at **02:38** measured approximately **0.073 ± 0.004 g% ethanol**, below 0.08 at that later collection time, and the later toxicology report contained no detected active THC. (October 23 production, authentic laboratory documents within AGO 000029–000053, pinpoint page to confirm.) That result does not alone establish the blood-alcohol concentration while driving, and Vehicle Code section 23152(a) does not require a 0.08 reading. It is relevant corroborative evidence, not the argument's sole foundation.

Finally, CHP's Penal Code section 851.6 certificate classifies the custody as a detention only and not an arrest. (Pet., Ex. 7.) The certificate and absence of charges matter to the subsequent agency chronology but do not themselves adjudicate the original arrest's probable cause or prove criminal false imprisonment. Under section 679.10(k)(1), prosecution and conviction are not conditions of a certification. The Court should decide the qualifying-activity question from what the officers actually did, knew and were authorized to do, rather than from the prosecutor's later filing decision.

## V. CHP'S APRIL 20 DENIAL MUST CONFORM TO THE DISTINCT CERTIFICATION STANDARD

The April 20 denial identified the **qualifying-criminal-activity** criterion as the basis for rejection. That is precisely why the lawfulness of the roadside and custodial **restraint**, and the evidence CHP received about it, matter to Count One. Petitioner does not invite an independent finding that a short written explanation is unlawful; the demurrer ruling removed any separate explanation cause of action. Rather, the actual denial must stand or fall under the correct statutory elements and on a lawful exercise of the certifying entity's discretion.

Section 679.10(c) explicitly enumerates false imprisonment and unlawful criminal restraint. Section 679.10(k)(1) dispenses with any requirement of an open investigation, charges, apprehension, prosecution or conviction. Subdivision (k)(3) also forbids denial based on specified extraneous criteria, including a victim's criminal history and refusal to cooperate in another matter. Petitioner does **not** allege as an established fact that Serrato relied on a prohibited reason; any such proposition depends on the actual April decision file and the grounds shown there. The Court should reject a construction of the statute that would insulate officers' conduct from certification solely because it was packaged as a DUI investigation, just as it should avoid the converse automatic assumption that a disputed DUI arrest was criminal false imprisonment.

The April 9 request was supported by the recorded encounter, report comparisons, and petitioner's complaint of wrongdoing. The material question is whether CHP considered and evaluated the **specific qualifying offense asserted**, with an accurate account of the physical restraint and relevant evidence, rather than merely the existence of an officers' asserted DUI purpose. Petitioner requests a finding on the actual transmitted materials and contemporaneous decision evidence, and, to the extent discovery remains incomplete, permission for the narrow evidentiary presentation necessary to complete that assessment. The later May 9 internal review and interview memoranda appearing on CHP's privilege log postdate the April 20 denial; their undisclosed contents are not offered as proof of what Serrato knew earlier.

## VI. HELPFULNESS AND THE MANDATORY CONSEQUENCE OF ESTABLISHED ELIGIBILITY

Section 679.10(g)(1) imposes certification where the person was a victim of qualifying criminal activity and has been, is being, or is likely to be helpful in its detection, investigation, or prosecution. Subdivision (h) creates a rebuttable presumption of helpfulness if the victim has not refused reasonably requested assistance. Petitioner supplied CHP his account, the recording transcript, and incident-specific contradictions in support of his April request; he had separately reported the alleged misconduct through the agency and POST complaint channels. (Pet., Exs. 2–3; Petitioner Decl. ¶¶ 10–12, upon verification.) The April 20 denial did not identify unhelpfulness as a ground for refusal. Petitioner will attest, to the extent of personal knowledge, to the absence of any unfulfilled reasonable request for assistance concerning the alleged officer misconduct.

The roadside refusal of a voluntary PAS during an investigation **of Petitioner for DUI** is not on its face a refusal to aid CHP in detecting or investigating **the alleged qualifying crime against Petitioner**. If CHP contends otherwise, the requested assistance, date, relevance, and actual refusal should be identified. Once the qualifying victimhood and helpfulness predicates are established on the facts the Court lawfully finds, the operative phrase of section 679.10(g)(1) is “shall certify,” not merely “may consider.”

## VII. MANDATE IS NECESSARY, AND RELIEF CAN BE SPECIFIC WITHOUT DISPLACING LAWFUL DISCRETION

Code of Civil Procedure section 1085 authorizes a writ to compel an act specially enjoined by law; section 1086 applies where no plain, speedy and adequate ordinary remedy exists. A damages judgment or further correspondence cannot furnish the certification from an authorized certifying official that the statute makes available. The Court may correct failure to exercise discretion under the legally prescribed standard or an abuse of discretion. (*Saleeby v. State Bar* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547, 562; *Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors* (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442–446.) Where only one disposition is lawful on established facts, the Court may command that result. (*California Correctional Supervisors Organization, Inc. v. Department of Corrections* (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 824, 827.)

Petitioner requests a specific judgment: find the asserted qualifying offense and helpfulness proven under section 679.10, vacate the April denial, and direct CHP's authorized certifying official to complete, sign and provide Form I-918, Supplement B within 30 days. If the Court instead identifies a proven legal error or abuse but a genuine remaining agency determination, vacate and remand with instructions to evaluate the false-imprisonment/unlawful-restraint theory under the correct standard, address the record as found, issue a new determination within 30 days, and report compliance. Any timetable is requested as judicial relief, not mischaracterized as an original-request deadline in subdivision (j).

If material facts remain genuinely contested, Petitioner asks the Court to identify only those facts essential to Count One and prescribe the proper hearing procedure. Section 1094 authorizes a noticed-motion judgment when the petition presents no triable material fact or rests solely on an administrative record; section 1090 addresses the Court's authority respecting factual issues raised by a return. Petitioner seeks the shortest lawful route to final judgment, not a procedural shortcut depriving either side of an opportunity to address competent evidence.

## VIII. CONCLUSION

Petitioner requests judgment on Count One, vacatur of the April 20 denial, and a peremptory writ directing certification within 30 days if the statutory predicates permit no lawful alternative. Alternatively, upon proved error or abuse, he requests lawful reconsideration within 30 days and a return to the writ. Any essential contested facts should be resolved through a prompt procedure directed by the Court.
