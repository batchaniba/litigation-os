INTRODUCTION
Asurint’s Opposition does not identify a genuine Rule 23 defect.1 Instead, it largely 
sets forth merits defenses and ad hominem attacks on Plaintiff as purported class-certification 
arguments. But Rule 23 asks whether Plaintiff’s claim under the FCRA is cohesive enough 
to be tried through common proof—not whether Asurint has assembled inflammatory facts it 
hopes to use against Plaintiff. With respect to the FCRA Class certification should be readily 
granted because: (i) Plaintiff is typical of the group he seeks to represent – those “persons in 
California who applied for employment with Vons and on whom Asurint conducted a 
background search and furnished a consumer report containing sex offender registry
information from the Megan’s Law Website that was used by Vons and/or Asurint for 
employment decisions”; (ii) Plaintiff’s claim is common and predominates over any 
individualized issues because Asurint sent the FCRA 613 notice, Pre-Adverse Action Notice, 
and Adverse Action Notice on behalf of Vons, which show that Asurint furnished 
background reports with SOR information to Vons, knowing that Vons was using them in 
violation of the Megan’s Law. Vons has already identified the members of the FCRA Class, 
and the only thing left is to determine whether or not Asurint violated the FCRA with respect 
to the FCRA Class members by furnishing background reports with SOR information to 
Vons knowing they would be used in violation of the Megan’s Law. Accordingly, Plaintiff 
respectfully requests the Court grant class certification of the FCRA Class.
ARGUMENT

Plans & Tr. Funds, 568 U.S. 455, 466 (2013) (“Rule 23 grants courts no license to engage in

unlawful when it knew that Vons would rely on the SOR information for employment 
purposes. What is more, this merits defense applies commonly to all FCRA Class members, 
and therefore does not undermine commonality or predominance.
B. The FCRA Class Is Adequately Defined And Ascertainable
Although not required by Rule 23, courts have required a showing that the class to be
certified is ascertainable. See Walker v. Life Ins. Co. of the Sw., No. CV 10-9198 JVS 
RNBX, 2012 WL 7170602, at *18 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 9, 2012). “Ascertainability requires that
the class definition be ‘definite enough so that it is administratively feasible for the 
court to ascertain whether an individual is a member’ before trial, and by reference to 
‘objective criteria.’” Ehretv. Uber Techs., Inc., 148 F. Supp. 3d 884, 889 (N.D. Cal. 2015) 
(citation omitted). A class definition that relies on objective, verifiable criteria satisfies the
ascertainability requirement. See Keegan v. Am. Honda Motor Co., 284 F.R.D. 504, 521 
(C.D. Cal. 2012). Notably, the Ninth Circuit has held that in the context of a consumer class 
action, that plaintiffs not only do not have to identify class members as a prerequisite to 
meeting their burden under Rule 23, but also do not have to present a feasible methodology

D. Commonality And Predominance Is Satisfied 
The FCRA Class presents a straightforward common question that predominates over 
any individualized issue: whether Asurint conducted a background search and furnished a
consumer report containing SOR information from the Megan’s Law Website knowing that 
the information would be unlawfully used by Vons for employment decisions.
This common question governs Plaintiff and FCRA Class members’ FCRA claim. 
Asurint argues individualized issues arise in resolving Vons’ liability under the Megan’s 
Law. But Vons has already conceded that it does not hire applicants with SOR information 
in their background reports. Vons identified approximately 43 applicants who applied for 
employment, had background reports with SOR information provided by Asurint, and were 
denied employment as a result. Kim Decl. ¶ 15. Each of these 43 applicants received an 
Adverse Action Notice from Asurint. Thus, commonality and predominance with respect to 
the FCRA Class is easily satisfied. 
E. Plaintiff Is Typical Of The FCRA Class
“Typicality is satisfied when the representative’s claims are reasonably coextensive 
with those of absent class members, even if they are not substantially identical.” DZ Rsrv. v. 
Meta Platforms, Inc., 96 F.4th 1223, 1238 (9th Cir. 2024), cert. denied, 145 S. Ct. 1051 
(2025). A unique defense defeats typicality only when there is a danger that the named 
plaintiff will become preoccupied with that defense to the detriment of the class. Id. at 1238-
39. Asurint argues Plaintiff is atypical because he disclosed his conviction. Opp. at 19:19-21. 
Asurint’s argument is irrelevant here. Whether or not Plaintiff disclosed his conviction does 
not exculpate Vons from liability for violating the Megan’s Law or Asurint from violating 
the FCRA knowing that Vons was using the background reports for unlawful purposes . The 
Megan’s Law does not require the SOR information be the but-for cause of the employment 
decision. Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) (prohibiting “use of any information that is 
disclosed” in Megan’s Law Website). The plain language of the statute provides that the “use

G. The FCRA Class Should Be Certified For Injunctive Relief Under Rule 23(b)(2).
Rule 23(b)(2) applies when the defendant has acted on grounds generally applicable to 
the class, making final injunctive relief appropriate for the class as a whole. Parsons, 754 
F.3d at 686-89. That standard is satisfied here. Plaintiff seeks uniform injunctive relief 
requiring “Asurint cease from furnishing consumer reports that include information from the 
Megan’s Law Website knowing that such information will be used in violation of the law.”.

Contrary to Asurint’s contention, whether SOR information may be disclosed is not the 
issue. Plaintiff does not claim that Asurint violated the Megan’s Law by including SOR

issuing background reports to Vons knowing that they will be used for unlawful purposes, i.e.

Professions Code § 17203. Complaint, ¶¶ 73, 74.c. Third, the proposed injunction will 
remedy Asurint’s continuing misconduct. Asurint does not state that it has ceased issuing 
background reports to Vons containing SOR information. Until it does, the requested 
injunction will provide relief to every member of the class. This is precisely the type of 
prospective, policy-directed relief Rule 23(b)(2) is designed to address. Parsons, 754 F.3d at 
688-89.
