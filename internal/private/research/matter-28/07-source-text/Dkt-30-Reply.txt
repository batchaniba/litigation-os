15   I.     Claim Preclusion
16          A.      Plaintiff’s Technical Arguments Are Insufficient to Defeat the
                    Preclusive Effect of the State Court’s Order.
17

18          Plaintiff attempts to evade the application of claim preclusion by attacking the form, rather
19   than the substance, of the state court’s July 15, 2026 order. In that regard, Plaintiff contends that the
20   July 15 order does not constitute a final judgment on the merits due to the order’s “unusual form”
21   and failure to include “dismissal language” or the word “judgment.” ECF No. 23 at 9. Plaintiff also
22   notes that in sustaining the defendants’ demurrer, Judge George Abdallah signed a submitted
23   proposed order, crossing out the word “PROPOSED.”
24          As the California Supreme Court has explained:
25                  It is not the form of the decree but the substance and effect of the
                    adjudication which is determinative. As a general test, which must be
26                  adapted to the particular circumstances of the individual case, it may
                    be said that where no issue is left for future consideration except the
27                  fact of compliance or noncompliance with the terms of the first decree,
                    that decree is final, but where anything further in the nature of judicial
28                  action on the part of the court is essential to a final determination of

 1                  the rights of the parties, the decree is interlocutory.
 2   Lyon v. Goss, 19 Cal. 2d 659, 670 (1942).

 3          The state court’s July 15 order sustained defendants Hope and Katie Palacios’s demurrer to

 4   Plaintiff’s first amended complaint “without leave to amend on the grounds that the First Amended

 5   Complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to state any cause of action.” ECF No. 15-1 at 47. Thus, the

 6   order adjudicated all claims, leaving no further pleading opportunity or issues unresolved. While the

 7   July 15 order did not explicitly state that it is a final judgment, or otherwise direct entry of a final

 8   judgment, the absence of such language does not render the order interlocutory; the order’s substance

 9   expressly resolved all issues in the case. Accordingly, the order constitutes a final judgment on the

10   merits for purposes of claim preclusion. See Griset v. Fair Political Practices Comm’n, 25 Cal. 4th

11   688, 698 (2001) (“Applying [Lyon’s] test here, we conclude the 1991 superior court ruling that was

12   the subject of appellate review in Griset I, supra, 8 Cal.4th 851, was a final judgment. As we explain

13   below, the Superior Court’s denial of plaintiffs’ petition for a writ of mandate disposed of all issues

14   in the action between plaintiffs and the FPPC, because it completely resolved plaintiffs’ allegation—

15   essential to all of plaintiffs’ causes of action—that section 84305 was unconstitutional.”).

16          The San Joaquin County Superior Court’s own docket confirms as much. As reflected in

17   Defendants’ supplemental request for judicial notice, the Superior Court’s docket for Newanforbi v.

18   Palacios reflects the case’s disposition as “Case Disposed” on July 15, 2026, the same date as the

19   order sustaining the demurrer. Defs.’ Supp. Request J. Not., Ex. A. That entry confirms that nothing

20   remained pending in the state court action as of the date of the order. Under Lyon and Griset, this is

21   sufficient to establish that there was a final judgment on the merits.

22          Plaintiff’s reliance on Valvo v. University of Southern California, 67 Cal. App. 3d 887, 892

23   n.3 (1977), and Berri v. Superior Court, 43 Cal. 2d 856, 860 (1955), does not support a different

24   conclusion. See ECF No. 23 at 9. Both decisions addressed whether an order sustaining a demurrer

25   is independently appealable, or vulnerable to reconsideration, before any further proceeding occurs.

26   Importantly, neither case involved consideration of the substance-over-form test articulated in Lyon

27   and Griset.

28          In Valvo, the trial court sustained the respondent’s demurrer without leave to amend and

 1   subsequently issued a separate order of dismissal. 1 67 Cal. App. 3d at 891. The petitioner then moved
 2   for reconsideration of the order sustaining the demurrer, which the trial court denied. Id. Plaintiff
 3   then appealed: (1) the order sustaining the demurrer, (2) the order of dismissal, and (3) the denial of
 4   the motion for reconsideration. Id. The California Court of Appeals dismissed the “purported appeal
 5   from the order sustaining the demurrer,” observing that “[a]n order sustaining a demurrer is not
 6   appealable; an appeal lies only from the ensuing judgment of dismissal.” Id. Ultimately, the Court
 7   of Appeals reversed the judgment of dismissal and directed the trial court “to overrule respondents’
 8   demurrer to the second amended petition.” Id. at 898. Contrary to Plaintiff’s suggestion, Valvo did
 9   not hold that an order sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend can never constitute a final
10   judgment on the merits. Instead, the case stands for the proposition that once a final judgment of
11   dismissal is entered, any appeal lies with the judgment of dismissal rather than the earlier order on
12   the demurrer.
13          Berri also did not involve consideration of whether an order sustaining a demurrer without
14   leave to amend can be considered a final judgment under the substance-over-form test. Instead, Berri
15   involved consideration of California Code of Civil Procedure §583, which applies to dismissal of
16   civil actions for trial delays. Simply put, neither case cited by Plaintiff holds that a state court order
17   disposing of all issues and claims, as confirmed by the court’s own docket, loses its preclusive effect
18   merely because the court did not enter a separate judgment of dismissal. 2
19          B.       Smith is in Privity With the Defendants to the State Court Action
20          Plaintiff also argues that Smith cannot be bound by the state judgment because she was not
21   named as a party in the state action and her conduct as Hope’s policy-making officer was not
22   “represented” in that case. ECF No. 23 at 11-12.
23          Privity does not require that every allegation against a nonparty be litigated verbatim in the

25   of Southern California to reinstate the petitioner as a student in good standing at the university’s
     medical school. 67 Cal. App. 3d at 890.

     the use of a proposed order, explicitly requiring the prevailing party to prepare and submit a proposed
28   order for the court’s review and signature.

 1   prior suit. Instead, for privity between the parties to exist, the nonparty’s interests must be adequately
 2   aligned with, and represented by, a party to the earlier suit. DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber, 61 Cal.
 3   4th 813, 826 (2015). As explained in Defendants’ motion, Katie Palacios, the individual defendant
 4   named in the state action, and Defendant Smith are both Hope employees, which is sufficient to
 5   establish privity between the parties. See Scott v. Kuhlmann, 746 F.2d 1377, 1378 (9th Cir. 1984)
 6   (per curiam) (finding privity for purposes of res judicata in a case where “[d]ifferent individuals
 7   [we]re named defendants in the two suits, but all [were] employees of the FCC who participated in
 8   the” challenged conduct that led to the alleged injury); Drawsand v. F.F. Properties, L.L.P., 866 F.
 9   Supp. 2d 1110, 1127 (N.D. Cal. 2011) (“An employer-employee relationship generally satisfies the
10   privity requirement for matters within the scope of employment.”). 3
11          There is also no merit to Plaintiff’s suggestion that privity is absent due to his own suing Hope
12   under a different name in the state court case than the name used to initiate the instant action.
13   Critically, Plaintiff does not dispute that both cases involve the same corporate defendant. Plaintiff’s
14   unilateral decision to use alternative names for Hope in the two lawsuits that he filed cannot serve as
15   a basis to avoid claim preclusion.
16   II.    The Complaint Fails to Plausibly Allege that Defendants Acted Under Color of State
            Law.
17

18          In his opposition, Plaintiff appears to argue that Hope is a state actor merely because state law
19   required his enrollment in the program, which is governed by an extensive regulatory scheme. ECF
20   No. 23 at 13-14. In that regard, Plaintiff points to four categories of allegations to support his
21   contention that Defendants are state actors: (1) the statutory instruction that a certified provider
22   determine treatment length “in consultation with” a parole officer, Cal. Penal Code § 3008(d)(2); (2)
23   the regulatory requirement of a jointly signed Form 3043, 15 Cal. Code Regs. § 3574(f)(1); (3) Hope’s
24   submission of treatment-status content that was cited in CDCR’s discharge-review report; and a
25   single instance in which a parole agent allegedly told Hope to record Plaintiff’s attendance as a “no
26   show.” Id.

 1          None of these, alone or together, establishes the “substantial degree of cooperation” this
 2   Circuit requires before private conduct becomes state action. Franklin v. Fox, 312 F.3d 423, 445 (9th
 3   Cir. 2002). The regulatory requirement for Hope to consult with CDCR and for both entities to sign
 4   Form 3043 describe the conditions under which Hope is permitted to operate as a contractor. At
 5   most, they require CDCR to exercise some degree of oversight over the parolees it supervises.
 6   Importantly, these regulatory requirements do not demonstrate that the State authored, directed, or
 7   even influenced the content of Hope’s clinical assessments, which is the specific conduct Plaintiff
 8   challenges. Caviness v. Horizon Cmty. Learning Ctr., Inc., 590 F.3d 806, 812 (9th Cir. 2010) (the
 9   inquiry asks whether “the specific conduct of which the plaintiff complains” is fairly attributable to
10   the State). A contract to perform a state-mandated function, without an allegation that the State
11   authored, dictated, or ratified Hope’s clinical judgments, is not enough. Rendell-Baker v. Kohn, 457
12   U.S. 830, 841 (1982).
13          Moreover, a private entity’s participation in a state regulatory or contractual scheme, without
14   more, does not convert its conduct into state action. Id.; Blum v. Yaretsky, 457 U.S. 991, 1005 (1982).
15   “Even extensive government regulation of a private business is insufficient to make that business a
16   state actor if the challenged conduct was not compelled or even influenced by any state regulation.”
17   Caviness, 590 F.3d at 816 (quotations omitted). Plaintiff’s allegations reflect that Defendants
18   privately operated a sex offender management program that was regulated by the state. The
19   complaint’s allegations do not, as Plaintiff contends, “describe coordinated decision-making.” See
20   ECF No. 23 at 14.

 1   retaliation claim. See Nunez v. City of Los Angeles, 147 F.3d 867, 875 (9th Cir. 1998).
 2          Plaintiff’s opposition also fails to demonstrate that the complaint states a due process claim.
 3   Plaintiff does not dispute that a grievance procedure was available for perceived factual errors in the
 4   discharge-review report. See 15 Cal. Code Regs. §§ 3482(a)(2), 3484(a). Instead, Plaintiff argues
 5   that the provided safeguards were insufficient because the deprivation here was “systemic” rather
 6   than the product of a “random and unauthorized act.” ECF No. 23 at 18. In support of this argument,
 7   Plaintiff relies on Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113 (1990), which held that the existence of
 8   postdeprivation remedies does not necessarily foreclose a due process claim challenging the adequacy
 9   of predeprivation procedures. Specifically, Zinermon held that “[i]n situations where the State
10   feasibly can provide a predeprivation hearing before taking property, it generally must do so
11   regardless of the adequacy of a postdeprivation tort remedy to compensate for the taking.” Id. at 132.
12          Plaintiff’s contention that he is entitled to predeprivation safeguards lacks merit. Plaintiff
13   admits that he does not challenge his original conviction or the initial requirement that he complete
14   sex offender therapy as a condition of his parole. ECF No. 23 at 17. Instead, he challenges the
15   requirement that he continue to participate in Hope’s sex offender management program until
16   termination of his parole. Id. at 17-18. Plaintiff, however, does not have a protected liberty in the
17   discontinuation of his sex offender therapy. “There is no right under the Federal Constitution to be
18   conditionally released before the expiration of a valid sentence, and the States are under no duty to
19   offer parole to their prisoners.” Swarthout v. Cooke, 562 U.S. 216, 220 (2011). Moreover, convicted
20   sex offenders who are subject to mandatory sex offender treatment, such as Plaintiff, have already
21   received all procedural protections required under the Fourteenth Amendment. Neal v. Shimoda, 131
22   F.3d 818, 831 (9th Cir. 1997) (“An inmate who has been convicted of a sex crime in a prior adversarial
23   setting, whether as the result of a bench trial, jury trial, or plea agreement, has received the minimum
24   protections required by due process.”); see also Fletcher v. Idaho Dep’t of Corr., 2020 WL 7082690,
25   at *6 n.5 (D. Idaho Dec. 3, 2020) (“Although such an individual has a liberty interest in avoiding
26   mandatory sex offender treatment and registration, that individual—having been convicted in a court

 7   IV.    The Bane Act Claim Still Fails.
 8          Plaintiff’s opposition confirms that the complaint does not sufficiently allege interference
 9   with Plaintiff’s rights “by threat, intimidation, or coercion” within the meaning of California Civil
10   Code § 52.1. See Herrera v. AllianceOne Receivable Mgmt., Inc., 170 F. Supp. 3d 1282, 1288 (S.D.
11   Cal. 2016) (observing that coercion under the Bane Act requires force, physical or moral, constraining
12   the plaintiff to act against his will). Plaintiff argues that the complaint describes Hope’s “gatekeeping
13   role” and knowledge that an adverse characterization in its reports would result in parole
14   consequences is sufficient to establish coercion. ECF No. 23 at 19. These allegations describe the
15   ordinary operation of Hope’s treatment program, which involved transmitting reports to CDCR. The
16   mere fact that unfavorable reports could have adverse parole consequences does not, without more,
17   demonstrate that Hope interfered with Plaintiff’s rights by using threats, intimidation, or coercion.

16   VI.    Plaintiff Concedes That His RICO Claim is Inadequately Pled
17          Plaintiff’s opposition also concedes, at least tacitly, that the complaint’s RICO claim suffers
18   from the same deficiency as his misrepresentation claim. In his opposition, Plaintiff states that he no
19   longer relies on the obstruction predicate, acknowledging that 18 U.S.C. § 1519 “is not among the
20   predicate offenses listed in” 18 U.S.C. § 1961(1). ECF No. 23 at 21. That leaves only the mail- and
21   wire-fraud predicates. With respect to those two predicate acts, the opposition states “an amended
22   complaint can identify each alleged mailing or wire by date, sender, recipient, document, false
23   content, and role in the alleged scheme, and can clarify which Defendant is the RICO ‘person’ and
24   which person and entities compose the enterprise.” Id. at 21. Given that Plaintiff does not dispute
25   this claim’s deficiencies, Defendants’ motion should be granted as to the complaint’s RICO claim.
