# INTRODUCTION

1. This action arises from an accepted Home Depot hire at its distribution and fulfillment facility at 5395 Hopkins Road, Tracy, California. Plaintiff accepted the position and began Home Depot’s onboarding process. Home Depot then required and ran an employment background check. On information and belief, Home Depot used First Advantage Background Services Corp. (“First Advantage”) as the consumer reporting agency for that screening.

2. The First Advantage relationship is not pleaded from guesswork alone. Public federal-court records contain a Home Depot-branded “BACKGROUND CHECK DISCLOSURE AND AUTHORIZATION” identifying First Advantage as the consumer reporting agency Home Depot would use to procure California investigative consumer reports. Other federal litigation describes First Advantage furnishing a consumer report to Home Depot for a prospective employee, and First Advantage itself later publicly featured a Home Depot representative in a program devoted to workforce screening practices. The precise order, package, transmission, and adjudication records for Plaintiff’s screening are in Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession.

3. On information and belief, First Advantage prepared and furnished Plaintiff’s employment background report to Home Depot in connection with the Tracy hire, and Home Depot received and used that report. After the background check, Home Depot ceased the onboarding process and ended, withdrew, or prevented Plaintiff from beginning or continuing the accepted Tracy employment on or about August 2024.

4. Before taking that adverse employment action, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the First Advantage report and did not provide the written Summary of Rights required by the Fair Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”), 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A). Home Depot thereby deprived Plaintiff of the statutory pre-decision opportunity to review the report, dispute its contents with the consumer reporting agency, and respond before the employment decision became final.

5. When Home Depot denied, withdrew, ended, or prevented the employment under circumstances in which it had obtained an investigative consumer report, it also did not advise Plaintiff that such a report had been obtained and did not provide First Advantage’s name and address as required by California Civil Code § 1786.40.

6. California Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) prohibits use of information disclosed pursuant to California’s Megan’s Law website for purposes relating to employment except where another law authorizes the use. Plaintiff alleges, on information and belief and as an ultimate fact, that First Advantage furnished Home Depot information concerning Plaintiff that was disclosed pursuant to § 290.46, including Plaintiff’s California registry information, and that Home Depot used that information for the purpose of deciding whether Plaintiff would begin or continue the accepted Tracy employment.

7. That allegation is supported by specific facts rather than conjecture: Home Depot accepted Plaintiff for the Tracy position and began onboarding him; Home Depot then ran an employment background check through a screening vendor that public records identify as First Advantage; Plaintiff was contemporaneously listed on California’s Megan’s Law website; First Advantage reports concerning this same Plaintiff for other employers during the surrounding period included sex-offender-registry screening and California-registry information; and Home Depot terminated the onboarding sequence after the background check without communicating an independent reason. The transaction-specific report, source fields, adjudication codes, access logs, and decision records are in Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession.

# FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

## A. The Tracy Hire and Onboarding

20. On or about August 2024, Home Depot hired Plaintiff for work at its Tracy distribution / fulfillment operation at 5395 Hopkins Road.

21. Plaintiff accepted the employment and began onboarding. The position was not a Department of Transportation-regulated position within the limited exception described in 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(B)-(C). **[VERIFY BEFORE FILING.]**

22. As part of the onboarding and employment-screening process, Home Depot procured or caused First Advantage to prepare a consumer report concerning Plaintiff for employment purposes.

## B. First Advantage's Screening of Plaintiff and the Comparator Record

23. On information and belief, First Advantage prepared an employment consumer report concerning Plaintiff at Home Depot’s request in or about August 2024 and furnished or made that report available to Home Depot through First Advantage’s employer-screening platform.

24. Plaintiff does not presently possess Home Depot’s copy of that report or its complete First Advantage order file. Home Depot and First Advantage possess the precise order date, completion date, client/requestor profile, package identifier, component results, source fields, adjudication status, client criteria, access history, transmission history, and records identifying the information Home Depot reviewed.

25. Plaintiff alleges that the Home Depot report contained information disclosed pursuant to Penal Code § 290.46, including Plaintiff’s California sex-offender-registry information. That allegation is made on information and belief because the report and source fields are within Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s control, and it is supported by the factual pattern alleged below.

26. At the time of Home Depot’s screening, Plaintiff was publicly listed on California’s Megan’s Law website. Plaintiff’s identity and underlying California registry entry were the same regardless of which employer requested a First Advantage employment screen; what could vary by employer was the screening package, adjudication configuration, or presentation of the underlying data.

27. Plaintiff possesses a First Advantage employment background report concerning himself generated for United Parcel Service during the same general period. In that report, ordinary criminal-search components returned non-adverse results, while the sex-offender-registry component was the adverse or decisional component and expressly identified the source of record as the California Sex Offender Registry. That report demonstrates that First Advantage’s screening of this same Plaintiff was capable of retrieving, reporting, and adjudicating his California registry information in an employment-screening product.

28. In a separate employment screening for Federal Express Corporation, also conducted by First Advantage, FedEx later admitted in its responsive pleading that the report identified Plaintiff as a California registrant. Together, these separate First Advantage screenings of the same Plaintiff provide additional factual support for the allegation that First Advantage’s contemporaneous Home Depot screening likewise contained Plaintiff’s California registry information.

29. Plaintiff does not allege that UPS’s or FedEx’s report is Home Depot’s report. He alleges those comparator records because they concern the same consumer, the same CRA, the same underlying California registry entry, and the same type of employment background screening, and therefore make the alleged contents of the Home Depot report more than speculative.

30. On information and belief, the registry information in the Home Depot report was adverse, decisional, review-triggering, ineligible, or otherwise material to Home Depot’s screening result and employment decision. Home Depot’s actual report, adjudication matrix, access history, applicant-tracking record, reason codes, and decisionmaker communications will identify the precise mechanism.

## C. The Adverse Employment Action

31. After the report was completed and transmitted, Home Depot ceased the onboarding process and stopped Plaintiff from beginning or continuing work at the Tracy facility.

32. The adverse action occurred on or about August 2024, after the First Advantage report had been completed and made available to Home Depot.

33. Home Depot did not identify to Plaintiff any independent post-report event that caused the accepted employment to end. Plaintiff alleges that Home Depot used the report, in whole or in part, in making the decision.

34. The FCRA defines adverse action in the employment context to include a denial of employment or any other decision for employment purposes that adversely affects a current or prospective employee. Home Depot’s withdrawal, ending, prevention, or non-finalization of the accepted employment was an adverse action.

## D. Home Depot Did Not Provide the Required Pre-Adverse Package

35. Before the adverse action became final, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the First Advantage report.

36. Before the adverse action became final, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff the written description of FCRA rights prescribed by 15 U.S.C. § 1681g(c)(3).

37. Plaintiff therefore was not given the report and statutory rights information before Home Depot acted on the report. He lost the statutory opportunity to review the exact information Home Depot was considering and to dispute it with First Advantage before the employment decision.

38. Home Depot’s failure caused concrete injury. Plaintiff lost an accepted employment opportunity and resulting wages and benefits, and he was denied the information and pre-decision process Congress required before an employer may take adverse action based in whole or in part on a consumer report.

## E. Home Depot Did Not Provide the ICRAA Adverse-Action Advice

39. The First Advantage report was an investigative consumer report within the meaning of the California Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (“ICRAA”) because it was prepared for employment purposes and contained information bearing on Plaintiff’s character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living, including criminal / public-record and registry information.

40. When Home Depot denied, withdrew, ended, or prevented the employment under circumstances in which the First Advantage investigative consumer report had been obtained, Home Depot did not advise Plaintiff that the report had been obtained and did not supply First Advantage’s name and address.

41. The omission alleged in paragraph 38 is distinct from the FCRA omission alleged in Count One. Count One concerns Home Depot’s failure **before adverse action** to furnish the report and federal Summary of Rights. Count Two concerns Home Depot’s separate California duty, upon denying employment under circumstances in which an investigative report was obtained, to advise Plaintiff and identify the investigative consumer reporting agency.

42. At the time Home Depot stopped the onboarding process in 2024, Plaintiff knew that the employment opportunity had ended, but Home Depot did not give him the consumer report, identify First Advantage, disclose the report’s contents, provide the statutory pre-adverse package, or otherwise reveal the report-based violations alleged here.

## G. California Registry Information and Home Depot’s Use

45. Plaintiff is a person as to whom information is disclosed on California’s Megan’s Law internet website established under Penal Code § 290.46.

46. Plaintiff alleges, on information and belief and as an ultimate fact, that the First Advantage report furnished to Home Depot contained Plaintiff’s information disclosed pursuant to Penal Code § 290.46.

47. Plaintiff further alleges, on information and belief and as an ultimate fact, that Home Depot received, accessed, considered, and used that § 290.46 information for the purpose of deciding whether Plaintiff would begin or continue the accepted Tracy employment.

48. Plaintiff alleges that the registry information was a substantial factor in Home Depot’s adverse employment decision. The factual basis includes the accepted hire, the intervening First Advantage background check, Plaintiff’s contemporaneous California registry listing, First Advantage’s demonstrated reporting of that same California registry information in other employment screens of Plaintiff, the cessation of onboarding after the screen, and the absence of any communicated independent reason.

49. The temporal sequence was: Home Depot accepted Plaintiff and began onboarding him; Home Depot procured the First Advantage employment screen; First Advantage reported Plaintiff’s registry information; Home Depot received or accessed the screening result; and Home Depot then stopped the accepted employment.

50. Home Depot’s use was for a purpose relating to employment within the meaning of § 290.46(j)(2)(E). The Tracy distribution / fulfillment position was not a position for which Plaintiff is aware of any law authorizing Home Depot to use information disclosed pursuant to § 290.46.

51. The precise report labels, source-field wording, adjudication code, access timestamps, internal reason codes, and identity of the decisionmaker are evidentiary details within Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession. For purposes of this Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that the California registry information reported through First Advantage was used by Home Depot and was a substantial factor in the adverse employment action.

# FIRST CAUSE OF ACTION  
## Fair Credit Reporting Act — 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A)  
### Against Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.

53. Plaintiff realleges paragraphs 1 through 52.

54. Plaintiff was a “consumer,” the First Advantage report was a “consumer report,” Home Depot procured or caused it to be procured for “employment purposes,” and Home Depot was a “person” using the report within the meaning of the FCRA.

55. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A), before taking adverse action based in whole or in part on an employment consumer report, the person intending to take the adverse action must provide the consumer (i) a copy of the report and (ii) a written description of the consumer’s rights under the FCRA.

56. Home Depot took adverse employment action based in whole or in part on the First Advantage report.

57. Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the report before the adverse action.

58. Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff the written Summary of Rights before the adverse action.

59. Home Depot therefore violated § 1681b(b)(3)(A).

60. Home Depot’s noncompliance was negligent within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681o and, on proof, willful within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681n. Willfulness is plausibly supported by Home Depot’s own publicly filed background-check form, which expressly recognized that before information from a consumer or investigative consumer report is used to make an adverse employment decision, Home Depot would provide the applicant the report and a summary of FCRA rights. That standardized language demonstrates corporate awareness of the pre-adverse obligation years before Plaintiff’s screening. Plaintiff pleads negligence and willfulness in the alternative.

61. As a direct result, Plaintiff suffered actual damages including lost employment compensation and the loss of the statutory opportunity to review and respond to the report before the employment decision. If willfulness is proven, Plaintiff also seeks statutory and punitive damages authorized by § 1681n.

# SECOND CAUSE OF ACTION  
## California Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act  
### Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1786.40, 1786.50  
### Against Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.

62. Plaintiff realleges paragraphs 1 through 52.

63. The First Advantage employment background report was an investigative consumer report within ICRAA.

64. California Civil Code § 1786.40(a) provides that when employment involving a consumer is denied under circumstances in which a report regarding the consumer was obtained from an investigative consumer reporting agency, the user shall advise the consumer against whom the adverse action was taken and supply the name and address of the investigative consumer reporting agency making the report.

65. Home Depot denied, withdrew, ended, or prevented Plaintiff’s employment under circumstances in which it had obtained the First Advantage investigative consumer report.

66. Home Depot did not provide the advice required by § 1786.40 and did not supply First Advantage’s name and address at the time of the adverse employment action.

67. This § 1786.40 omission is pleaded as a separate act or omission from the pre-adverse FCRA violation in Count One.

68. Under Civil Code § 1786.50, Plaintiff seeks actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater, together with costs and any attorney’s fees recoverable if counsel later appears. If the violation is proven grossly negligent or willful, Plaintiff seeks punitive damages as authorized by § 1786.50(b). Home Depot’s California-specific background-check disclosure publicly identified First Advantage by name, address, and telephone number years before the events here, supporting an inference that Home Depot was familiar with the identity of its investigative CRA and the California regulatory framework governing those reports.

# THIRD CAUSE OF ACTION  
## Unauthorized Employment Use of Registry Information  
### Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) and (j)(4)(A)  
### Against Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.

69. Plaintiff realleges paragraphs 1 through 52.

70. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(1) authorizes use of information disclosed pursuant to § 290.46 only to protect a person at risk, except as otherwise authorized by law.

71. Section 290.46(j)(2)(E) prohibits use of information disclosed pursuant to § 290.46 for purposes relating to employment except as authorized by paragraph (1) or another provision of law.

72. Plaintiff is informed and believes, and on that basis alleges, that the First Advantage report furnished to Home Depot contained information disclosed pursuant to Penal Code § 290.46, including Plaintiff’s California registry listing or registry-derived information.

73. Home Depot received and used the First Advantage report for an employment purpose.

74. Plaintiff is informed and believes that the registry information was a substantial factor in Home Depot’s decision to deny, withdraw, end, or prevent Plaintiff’s Tracy employment. The basis for that allegation is stated in paragraphs 23 through 28 and includes the timing of the screen and adverse action, Plaintiff’s contemporaneous registry listing, the absence of a communicated independent reason, and the screening records uniquely within Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession.

75. The pleaded use was not authorized by § 290.46(j)(1), and Plaintiff is unaware of another law authorizing Home Depot to use information disclosed pursuant to § 290.46 for the Tracy warehouse / fulfillment position.

76. Home Depot thereby violated § 290.46(j)(2)(E).

77. Section 290.46(j)(4)(A) makes a prohibited user liable for the civil remedies specified in that subdivision. Plaintiff seeks actual damages, any additional amount authorized by the statute, exemplary relief to the extent proven and legally available, attorney’s fees if counsel later appears and the statute authorizes recovery, and any other nonduplicative remedy available under § 290.46(j)(4)(A).

78. Plaintiff does not presently seek preventive relief under § 290.46(j)(4)(B). This complaint alleges Plaintiff’s transaction. If discovery establishes a pattern or practice and Article III requirements are independently satisfied, Plaintiff may seek leave to amend.

4. **Discovery date:** Paragraphs 40–42 are a filing-critical limitations section. Insert the real date and event by which Plaintiff first discovered the FCRA / ICRAA violation. FCRA uses the earlier of two years from discovery or five years from violation; ICRAA provides two years from discovery.

6. **Exhibit A:** If the Home Depot-specific First Advantage report is obtained before filing, consider attaching it only after a hostile contradiction audit and redaction under Fed. R. Civ. P. 5.2. If it is not obtained, do not attach another employer’s report as a substitute.
7. **Entity:** Confirm the report / onboarding documents identify HOME DEPOT U.S.A., INC. as the employer or user. If a different Home Depot affiliate ordered or used the report, fix the defendant before filing.
