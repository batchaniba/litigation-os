TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT PHILOSOPHY

HOPE Program was founded in September of 2008 by Shannon Smith, LCSW, who has
dedicated her professional career to helping individuals convicted of a sexual offense, or
suffering from emotional, behavioral, or psychological problems. Based on the extensive
experience and insights she gained over the years, Ms. Smith has developed a unique vision for a

clinical program that addresses client’s individual needs and risk level.

HOPE Program utilizes the latest best-practice research to provide services to individuals and
families in order to reduce their risk of reoffending or engaging in problematic sexual behaviors.
The primary goal of HOPE is to combine the latest research with practical psychotherapy
techniques to provide effective, personalized, and affordable care that leads to reduced
recidivism and healthier lifestyles. HOPE Program maintains strict adherence to the Association
for the Treatment and Prevention of Sexual Abusers (ATSA) and California Coalition on Sexual
Offending (CCOSO) guidelines and best practices, and is governed by the California Sex
Offender Management Board (CASOMB).

HOPE’s Core Focus is Forensic Mental Health Treatment and our Core Values are:
e Passionate about creating better lives
e Committed to victim prevention
e Approachable and warm
e Collaborative, respectful, motivated, and driven
e Acting with integrity

e Compassionate, conscientious, thoughtful, and professional

We strive to deliver our services according to the following guiding principles:
e Highly personalized service

e Experience and professionalism

The key characteristics of our Program are as follows:

Research-based Treatment

e Strict adherence to the Containment Model while incorporating evidence-based offender
rehabilitation models (i.e., Risk Need Responsivity, Good Lives Model) and strength-based
approaches

e Flexible curriculum and recovery plan tailored to individual needs and risk factors of the
clients

e Graduation criteria aligned to goal achievement and reduction of risk to reoffend

Specialized Psychotherapy

e Experienced CASOMB Certified practitioners trained in sex offender-specific treatment,
psychosexual evaluations (including specialized risk assessments) and personalized case
management techniques

e Identification of client needs based on structured assessment protocols and clinical
diagnoses obtained during the assessment process

@ Goal-based approach to treatment of sexual offenders

e Adaptive case management based on continuous progress monitoring and frequent
assessments

e Clinical staff are members of ATSA and/or CCOSO and professional organizations
providing ethical guidelines and treatment standards for the industry

Efficient Operating Model

e HOPE uses a treatment approach such that all clinical staff are able to provide services
and contribute to the treatment of any client using TreatAnyone (TA), a data management
platform that supports treatment and improves continuity of care. Stakeholders are given
access to TA to assist in maintaining transparency, effective communication, and better
oversight, which provides a closer and more direct collaboration between therapists and

supervising officers.

HOPE PROGRAMS CODE OF PRACTICE FOR SEX OFFENDER
THERAPISTS

In order to achieve our Core Focus and abide by our Core Values, HOPE operates within The
Code of Practice commonly known as The Code of Ethics for sex offender therapists, adapted
from The Association for the Treatment and Prevention of Sexual Abusers (ATSA:

https://www.atsa.com/). ATSA has published Practice Standards and Guidelines, which include a

section on Professional Conduct.

The Code of Practice presents a framework for making professional decisions and augmenting
our moral compass. The strength of HOPE ultimately resides in the commitment of its clinical

team to common goals, beliefs, and values.

It is the responsibility of HOPE and its employees to ensure we are dedicated to supporting
values of basic human dignity and respect, as well as common goals related to community safety
and preventing sexual violence. Employees are responsible for familiarizing themselves with the
ATSA Adult Practice Guidelines, the ATSA Adolescent Practice Guidelines, and this Code of
Ethics, as seen below. For additional information on ATSA guidelines, visit here:

https://members.atsa.com/learn/Details/guidelines-professional-code-of-ethic- 194332.

SAFETY PROTOCOLS

Safety is of upmost importance, and although we have been providing services in a community
setting for several years without incident, it is important to be mindful of the clientele we serve,

maintain healthy boundaries, and take measures to reduce risk as much as possible.

To ensure the safety of our employees and clients:

e Do not keep any secrets with a client at any time.

e Do not offer to hire or provide any money to a client at any time.

e Do not give or accept gifts to/from clients.

e Allservices are to be conducted during the business hours of the agency. Any service
outside of standard operating hours, requires prior written authorization from the
Executive Clinical Director.

e After the conclusion of group, ensure all members of the group leave the building
together. In the evenings, no clinician should ever stay behind and alone with a client
when no other people are nearby.

e All communication with clientele should take place in a HOPE office. Do not engage in
conversations with a client outside of the office. For example, do not have conversations
with clients in the hallway, on the sidewalk, or in any environment that is outside of the
clinic.

e Allinteractions with clientele are to remain professional and appropriate at all times.
Clinicians will not engage in derogatory or vulgar language.

e All social media connections and/or sharing personal contact information with clients is
strictly prohibited. This includes, but is not limited to:

a. Do not friend any clients on Facebook or any other social media applications.

Principles

HOPE Program is committed to good practice in the prevention of suicide and harm to others

physical and emotional safety of the client, staff, and clinicians in training is paramount. Thus,
HOPE Program ensures that staff and clinicians in training receive a level of training and
supervision appropriate to their role in responding to clients at risk of suicide and or dangerous to

others.

Risk Management

HOPE Program acknowledges, and is prepared for, clients disclosing suicide or thoughts of
harming others through a range of avenues including disclosure to staff, clinicians in training,
and/or other clients. Assessment and responses to the risk of suicide and those posing as
dangerous to others are undertaken by staff who are appropriately trained and qualified, using
evidence-based assessment and response practices. All indications of suicide and danger to
others are taken seriously. A first aid kit is available in accessible areas. All staff and clinicians
in training are supported to recognize the limits of individual roles and competencies and

actively facilitate links to further levels of care where necessary.

Related Legislation

Emergency Detention Under Welfare and Institutions Code Section 5150

5150 is the number of the section of the Welfare and Institutions Code, which allows a person
with a mental illness to be involuntarily detained for psychiatric hospitalization. A person on a
5150 can be held in the psychiatric hospital against their will for up to 72 hours. There needs to
be a probable cause to take any person into custody and to place such a person in a county-

designated facility for the initial 72-hour treatment and evaluation (i.e. "5150").

Probable Cause

Probable cause is defined as facts known to the authorized person that would lead a person of
ordinary care and prudence to believe, or to entertain a strong suspicion, that the person detained
is mentally disordered and is a danger to self or others, or is gravely disabled. In other
words, in order to be detained under W& I Section 5150, the person must be, "as a result of a

mental disorder," a danger to self or others or gravely disabled.

Danger to self
This criterion may be either deliberate intention to injure oneself (ie. overdose) or a disregard of
personal safety to the point where injury is imminent (i.e. wandering about in heavy traffic). The

danger must be present, immediate, substantial, physical, and demonstrable.

Examples could include some or all of the following:
e Words or actions showing intent to commit suicide or bodily harm
e Words or actions indicating gross disregard for personal safety
e Words or actions indicating a specific plan to suicide

e Means available to carry out suicide plan (i.e. pills, firearms present or available)

No-Harm Contract
Client No-Harm contracts provide a written and verbal agreement for the client not to engage in
suicidal or self-harming behavior. A client No-Harm contract depends on an established

clinician-client relationship and capacity of the client to provide informed consent.

A written No-Harm Contract template is used by HOPE Program where the client is able to
provide informed consent. The No-Harm Contract includes:
e A guarantee not to harm themselves (should have a specific time frame)
e Assisting the client in suicide proofing their environment (i.e., getting rid of
potentially lethal doses of medications, weapons, etc.)
e Actions that client can implement if suicidal thoughts return
e Identifying referrals client can use to respond to triggers for suicidality (e.g. suicide
hotline phone number, HOPE Program clinician contact info, etc.)
e Support structure that they can use as a resource (e.g. friends, family)
e List of crisis services
e Appointment day/time for the client to check in with HOPE Program clinician on the

phone or in person between the regularly scheduled sessions

CONTAINMENT MODEL

The Containment Model is an interdisciplinary strategy to manage sexual offenders that
combines elements of treatment, supervision, monitoring, and risk management. It includes
interventions designed to produce behavioral changes that are supported by modifications in the
offender’s daily lifestyle and psychological condition. The Containment Model is built upon two

observations:

1) The majority of criminally detected sexual offenders eventually return to communities under

some form of mandated community supervision

35
September 23, 2024

CONFIDENTIAL
HOPE_000259
===== page 36 =====
2) The likelihood of re-offense is reduced when well-planned mechanisms are in place that
support full and productive participation of offenders in community life, while holding them

accountable for the harm they have created and for the choices they made and continue to make.

The model is implemented on a case-by-case basis through collaboration of the various
stakeholders involved in each case. For example, a team that includes a parole agent, a
specialized law enforcement officer, inpatient and outpatient treatment providers, a medical
doctor, a polygraphist, a conditional release supervisor, and in some cases, a victim advocate acts
to “contain” the rehabilitating sexual offender. In some cases, a responsible family member or
friend may be integrated into this model. Each agent thoroughly learns the sexual offender’s
pattern of sexual offending, participates in ongoing regular discussions that identify any
resurfacing of key risk factors, and collaboratively strategizes and coordinates services (e.g.,

level of treatment, probation, etc.) to interrupt the cycle of re-offense.

The Containment Model asserts that reduction in recidivism is achieved only when the
multiple criminal justice agencies involved in each case proactively strategize to close
the gaps that naturally occur when multiple systems work together to promote change. A
thorough understanding of, and full participation in the Containment Model by all
involved agencies is essential to public safety and vital to the success of externally
motivated change. Ideally, externally motivated change can be internalized and the
offender will continue to live a prosocial lifestyle when supervision ends and legally
mandated monitoring mechanisms are discontinued (p. 4). (CCOSO Adult Standards
Guidelines, 2010).

HOPE Program operates under the strict adherence to the Containment Model, as a collaborative
approach to sex offender management. Per California Penal Code 290.09, "The certified sex
offender management professional shall communicate with the offender's probation officer or
parole agent on a regular basis, but at least once a month, about the offender's progress in the
program and dynamic risk assessment issues, and shall share pertinent information with the
certified polygraph examiner as required." The Containment Model must use a victim-centered

approach. The three primary stakeholders who have regular communication are:

2. Participation and open communication by HOPE Program

3. Participation and open communication by the polygraph examiner

These three stakeholders are the core of the Containment Team, although other team members
could participate in the Containment Team Meeting (CTM) to share general information, as

Prior to entering treatment, all clients are required to sign a Release of Information (ROI)

RISK NEED RESPONSIVITY (RNR) MODEL

In order to provide evidence-based treatment, it is required that all services (group, individual
and chaperone training) and treatment plans are delivered under the auspice of the Risk Needs

Responsivity (RNR) Model.

The RNR model was developed in the 1990s by Andrews, Bonta, and Hoge and is applied to a
wide range of criminal populations. RNR has become one of the most influential models guiding
treatment interventions in corrections (Ogloff & Davis, 2004). Hanson, Bourgon, Helmus, and
Hodgson (2009) meta-analysis of 23 recidivism studies indicates that programs that utilize the

Risk-Need-Responsivity Model evidenced lower sexual and general recidivism.

Empirical Research indicates that interventions that adhered to all the three principles of RNR

(i.e., targeted low risk offenders, and their non-criminogenic needs using non-behavioral

did not adhere to any of the three principles, were actually associated with a small increase in
recidivism. Treatment programs that adhered to at least one of the principles showed a small
decrease in recidivism. Treatment programs adhering to two principles showed larger effects.
Treatment interventions that adhered to risk reduction programs, informed by psychological

RELAPSE PREVENTION THERAPY (RP)

RP is a cognitive behavioral approach that was originally designed for use in conjunction with
treatment of addictive behaviors. More recently, it has been used as a stand-alone technique
designed to teach patients how to alter their behavior to recognize and cope with triggering

events, situations, and feelings that are likely to lead to a relapse.

THE GOOD LIVES MODEL

GLM is a strength-based treatment construct based on the notion that sex offenders are both
holders and violators of human rights. It suggests that humans require certain “goods” in
principal areas of life in order to feel whole: Life/Healthy Living, Knowledge, Excellence in
Work and Play, Excellence in Agency, (self-determination and self-agency), Inner Peace,
Relatedness (family/friends), Community, Spirituality, Happiness, and Creativity. The HOPE
treatment program leverages this model to help clinicians understand and evaluate the risks
factors associated with clients’ inability to achieve the basic Human Goods. Additionally, we
strongly favor this treatment philosophy because it also addresses clinician attitudes toward the
offender and the relationship between these attitudes and factors, such as forgiveness, evil, and

TREATMENT CURRICULUM

As a matter of best practice, HOPE Program utilizes an evidence-based curriculum to reduce
recidivism and increase community safety. Sex offender-specific treatment is goal-directed and
solution-focused in order to target risk factors that are associated with reoffending. All sex
offender specific treatment is highly structured and guided by the curriculum. HOPE Program
utilizes their own curriculum, known as Treatment Blocks, developed and written specifically for
HOPE. HOPE also utilizes various supplementary materials based on each client’s specific

treatment needs.

HOPE Program’s curriculum generally consists of four phases that gradually increase clients’
skills and understanding necessary to reduce their individual risk factors and the probability of
re-offending. Advancement to each phase is directly dependent on the client’s ability to
demonstrate measurable progress toward individual treatment goals. The measurement method is
based on STABLE-2007, LS/CMI, and other specialized tools designed to assess and track
change and risk over time. Clients are eligible to graduate only after they have successfully
completed the Maintenance Phase of the treatment program. As noted earlier, the core
curriculum integrates several evidence-based treatment modalities, such as Cognitive Behavioral
Therapy (CBT), social learning theory, and motivational interviewing techniques. Moreover,
RNR, GLM, as well as specific offense descriptive models are also utilized to facilitate

treatment.

Progress in treatment is measured by demonstrating knowledge of the skills discussed in
sessions, insight into the precipitating factors that led to their boundary violations, homework
and curriculum completion, and skill application. The goal is for the client to apply the skills

learned in treatment to their daily lives, thus promoting behavioral changes.

Throughout an offender’s treatment, the client will be provided with different “Treatment
Blocks.” Each Treatment Block is specially designed to address important psychological factors,
long-term vulnerabilities and risk factors, as well as healthy living skills and effective
communication skills, to name a few. Each Block is composed of short learning modules, and in
some cases, a module may be skipped and readdressed at a later date, pending each client’s

ability to address the specific topic at that time in their treatment.

HOPE Program has developed a Chaperone Program designed to educate the potential

Chaperone on the overall dynamics of sex offenders and covers important topics including the

definition of sexual abuse, the difference between a person who molests a child and a pedophile,

Who is Chaperone Training for?

Primary caregivers of adolescent sexual abusers and children with sexual behavior
problems.

Foster care providers with children who may have acted out sexually in the past.
Non-offending parents/caregivers who might need to chaperone a sexual abuser during a
specific event, such as church, a wedding, funerals, etc.

Significant others, friends, family members, and/or co-workers of a sexual offender who

want to assist that person in remaining offense free.

Chaperone Requirements:

Must be 21 years of age or older.

Must have no prior accusation, charge, and/or conviction regarding sexual abuse.

Must be able to cognitively understand the training materials.

Must acknowledge, in writing, that they believe the sexual offense occurred (i.e., not in
denial or colluding with the client).

Must sign the required participant documents.

Must be substance free.

Must report suspected child abuse to appropriate authorities.

Why do Chaperone training?

Sex Offenders do not live in a vacuum. Most persons who have committed a sexual

offense are living in the community.

COMPREHENSIVE INDIVIDUALIZED TREATMENT PLANS (CITPs)

A crucial first step in providing treatment is conducting a Comprehensive Individual Treatment
Plan (CITP) and risk assessment that includes assessments based on historical factors, or static
risks, and some that are based on long-term vulnerabilities or dynamic risks. Prior to starting
treatment, every client referred to HOPE undergoes an evaluation either referred to a Sex
Offender Specific Evaluation or commonly known as a CITP. The content and measures used in

the assessment are determined by the reason for referral and referring organization.

CITPs give an initial understanding of each new client. The CITP consists of a structured clinical
interview with the client and the completion of a combination of SARATSO and HOPE selected
risk assessment tools. A structured clinical interview is an evaluation conducted by a trained
clinician leveraging a variety of assessment methods such as the Level of Service Case
Management Inventory (LS/CMI), STABLE-2007, The LOOK, The Sex Offender Treatment
Readiness Scale (SOTRS), and the Criminal Sentiment’s Scale-Modified (CSS-M), which
depends on the presenting problem and referring agency. Additional testing can be employed, if

Information from assessments is captured in the Comprehensive Individualized Treatment Plan

(CITP), which among other things, identifies treatment needs and interventions for each

individual. The treatment plan for each client is carefully tailored, following the Risk, Need,

Responsivity Principle, to address specific needs identified during the intake and initial

evaluation process.

A CITP will also incorporate Individual treatment goals which serve as the framework for the

overall treatment plan. Individual treatment goals are defined when a mental health needs

assessment and a comprehensive sex offender-specific risk assessment are conducted with each

client. The goals focus on addressing personal skill deficits, predilections, and learned behaviors
that highly correlate with sexual recidivism. Each CITP will include a minimum of three
individual treatment goals; one sexual (obtained from the STABLE-2007), one violent (obtained
