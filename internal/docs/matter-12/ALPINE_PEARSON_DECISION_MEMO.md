# Matter 12 — Alpine Liability and Pearson Posture Decision Memo

**Prepared:** October 6, 2026  
**Purpose:** Internal decision aid; not a pleading and not a substitute for reviewing the operative complaint, contract, correspondence, or court docket.  
**Case:** *Brendan Ngwa Nforbi v. Diamond Truck Sales, Inc.*, San Joaquin Superior Court No. STK-CV-UF-2026-0001094.  
**Preservation rule:** Do not dismiss Alpine, stipulate to arbitration, abandon the February 13, 2026 filing date, or change a settlement position solely from this memo.

## Executive decision

**Keep Alpine in the case for now, but narrow the theory.** The present record does not support treating Alpine as the seller in the April 4, 2023 transaction or as the same legal corporation as Diamond. Alpine was formed September 2, 2025, before this action was filed. Alpine's potential exposure therefore depends principally on what happened to Diamond's assets, inventory, accounts, management, and liabilities—not on Alpine's participation in the original sale.

The current evidence justifies targeted successor/transfer discovery. It does not yet prove successor liability, alter ego, a voidable transfer, or Alpine's independent participation in fraud. The next decision should be driven by transfer records, consideration, ownership/control, and Diamond's post-transfer solvency and operations.

Pearson's current posture is best read as an attempt to secure two threshold exits: dismiss Alpine and send Diamond to arbitration while preserving defense timeliness and cost leverage. That is a procedural position, not proof that no transfer occurred. His reported earlier statement that the matter could “settle promptly” is a favorable signal, but there is still no defense number or concrete settlement proposal in the material presently linked to the portfolio.

## Chronology correction that must govern future drafting

- Original truck sale: April 4, 2023.
- Alpine formation: September 2, 2025.
- State complaint filed: February 13, 2026.

Do not allege that Alpine was formed during this litigation. It existed before filing. If public-facing migration, asset movement, or rebranding occurred after filing, plead that only after obtaining a dated source for the particular event.

These facts support discovery. Standing alone, they do not establish the transfer terms, common ownership, inadequate consideration, insolvency, or assumed liabilities.

### Net posture assessment

Pearson appears to be positioning the defense to reduce the case before discussing value: remove Alpine, compel Diamond to arbitration, preserve limitations arguments, and increase the perceived cost of continuing. That is rational defense lawyering. It should be met with precision rather than escalation:

- concede no inaccurate facts, but stop defending weak formulations;
- ask for the documents that would actually resolve Alpine's status;
- keep settlement communications separate from arbitration terms;
- let a motion create a record only when informal resolution cannot secure the same protections;
- do not assume a motion is harmless: a ruling could eliminate Alpine, compel arbitration, or create adverse reasoning as easily as it could create leverage.

## Decision gates

### Strengthen and retain Alpine if records show any combination of:

- Alpine acquired substantially all operating assets or inventory;
- little or no reasonably equivalent consideration;
- common beneficial ownership or control;
- Diamond ceased meaningful operations or became unable to pay creditors after the transfer;
- Alpine assumed liabilities expressly or by conduct;
- shared books, accounts, payroll, insurance, licenses, or dealer bonds;
- the same VINs moved through a common feed or coordinated bulk migration;
- post-claim transfer activity, retained control, concealment, or insider payments.

### Narrow but retain Alpine if:

- operating continuity is strong but consideration/ownership remains disputed;
- Alpine acquired selected assets but not yet enough is known to characterize the transaction;
- third-party records are outstanding and could resolve the transfer;
- Alpine seeks to rely on Diamond's arbitration agreement while denying any successor relationship.

### Consider voluntary dismissal of Alpine only if reliable records show:

- an arm's-length acquisition for reasonably equivalent value;
- distinct beneficial ownership and control;
- no assumption of relevant liabilities;
- Diamond remained adequately capitalized, solvent, and operational;
- the web/listing overlap was only a third-party advertising artifact;
- no inventory, customer list, domain, phone, equipment, personnel, or goodwill transfer tied the businesses together.

Before any dismissal, obtain a written limitations/tolling position and decide whether dismissal affects discovery access, arbitration arguments, costs, or claim preclusion.

## Arbitration consistency risk

There is a strategic tension: the more Plaintiff argues that Alpine is Diamond's successor and bound to Diamond's liabilities, the more Alpine may argue that it can enforce Diamond's arbitration agreement as a successor or under nonsignatory doctrines. Do not take absolute, inconsistent positions.

Working formulation:

- Alpine is not automatically entitled to arbitration merely because Plaintiff alleges successor liability.
- Whether Alpine may enforce the clause depends on the agreement, the asserted doctrine, and developed facts.
- Plaintiff may plead successor liability in the alternative while requiring Alpine to prove the basis on which it claims contractual enforcement.
- Any stipulation should state exactly which defendant, claims, defenses, forum, fees, and timeliness positions it covers.

This issue requires contract-specific legal research before briefing.

## Settlement operating rule

Do not dismiss Alpine merely to induce negotiations. Alpine currently supplies discovery and collectability leverage, but an unsupported theory can also distract from Diamond's core liability.

A practical sequence is:

1. request contractual mediation if the agreement makes it a required first step;
2. ask Pearson to identify the factual basis for denying successor/transfer liability and to produce the core transfer agreement voluntarily;
3. obtain a response to the existing settlement demand without reducing it first;
4. if no useful response, serve focused discovery and allow a properly framed motion or opposition to create a record;
5. revalue Alpine after Tier 1 documents—not from branding evidence alone.
