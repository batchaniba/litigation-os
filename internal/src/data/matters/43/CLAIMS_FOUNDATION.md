## The case that survives

A complaint that survives a demurrer, and Rule 12(b)(6) if the case is removed, is a short complaint. It pleads two counts against the company that made the offer and sent the letter. It does not plead every statute that touches a background check.

The offer is the injury. On October 22, 2026, The Hertz Corporation offered, and Brendan Ngwa Nforbi accepted, a full-time Manager Trainee job in Palo Alto at $23 an hour. On November 2, 2026, the same company wrote that it had reviewed the background report and that a preliminary decision rescinding that offer already existed. The wage, the job, and the accepted offer are what make the damages real. The report is what makes the statutory counts real.

The report and the letter do not say the same thing. The report flags three bodies of information **Client Review Required**: the driving record, one Santa Clara court record (case C1761642), and two registry subrequests that the report itself says are California registry information governed by Penal Code section 290.46. A separate subrequest on the name Brendan Ngwa is **Not in Registry** and **Meets Company Standards**. The letter then says the review may cost him the job "including" this line, and only this line:

> [Criminal Information] Alcohols related offenses-Does not meet

That mismatch is the lawsuit. Plead it as two alternative readings. Do not adopt the company's reading as a fact, and do not allege a fact the letter does not state.

**Reading A — conviction history.** The heading Hertz chose is criminal information. The only criminal court record in the report is case C1761642, and Hertz flagged it Client Review Required. The notice does not identify that case, any court, or any conviction. If the preliminary decision rests at least in part on conviction history, Government Code section 12952(c) required an individualized assessment of the Manager Trainee duties and a notice that names the conviction and states a real deadline. A category, "Does not meet," is not that assessment.

**Reading B — registry information.** The same review covered the results Hertz had just been told to review. Those results include the registry lines, the Penal Code section 290.46 warning HireRight wrote into the report on October 30, and the report's own statement that proper use of the report is the company's responsibility. The letter identifies no person at risk. If Hertz used information disclosed pursuant to section 290.46 for this employment decision, section 290.46(j)(2)(E) and (j)(4)(A) supply the second count.

Reading A is the count to build the case on. It matches the words the company printed ("Criminal Information," "preliminary decision recinding the offer," "specific criminal information"). It is filed after a Civil Rights Department right-to-sue notice, and not before.

Reading B is drafted below and kept separate, so that a demurrer aimed at the source problem cannot take Reading A with it. It is not filed on today's letter alone. The letter specifies the alcohol category and does not say the registry was a ground. Filing Reading B before the company identifies the items, or before the source records exist, is the motion to dismiss. The response already drafted in the pleading-architecture memo is what forces that identification without announcing the theory.

## Count 1 — Government Code section 12952

Unlawful employment practice, Fair Employment and Housing Act. Defendant: The Hertz Corporation only. This count incorporates the offer facts, the report facts about case C1761642 and the MVR, and the letter. It does not incorporate the registry-source allegations from Count 2.

Section 12952(c)(1) requires an individualized assessment before an employer denies a job solely or in part because of conviction history. The assessment asks whether the conviction history has a direct and adverse relationship with the specific duties of the job, considering the nature and gravity of the offense, the time that has passed since the offense and the completion of the sentence, and the nature of the job. Title 2, California Code of Regulations, section 11017.1(c)(1) describes that assessment as a reasoned, evidence-based determination. A policy of refusing a category of offense is not that determination. The Civil Rights Department's Fair Chance guidance states the same limit: an employer cannot simply refuse anyone with a given kind of conviction.

Section 12952(c)(2) requires the preliminary written notice to contain notice of the disqualifying conviction or convictions, a copy of the conviction-history report if any, and an explanation of the right to respond before the decision becomes final and the deadline by which to respond. Regulation 11017.1(c)(2) requires the same, and it provides that an emailed notice is deemed received two business days after it is sent and that the response period is then at least five business days from receipt. The statute and the regulation require the notice to state the deadline. They do not say that pasting the regulation into a blank bracket is a deadline.

**ALTERNATIVE allegations, all of them document-supported.**

1. The preliminary decision was based at least in part on conviction history. The notice headings and the mitigation sentence say "criminal information." The only criminal court record flagged Client Review Required is case C1761642. The notice does not identify it. That is a violation of section 12952(c)(2)(A), whether or not the company also looked at the driving record.
2. The MVR block was reviewed as one Client Review Required section. That block contains a conviction, docket A302962, Vehicle Code section 4000(a), which is not an alcohol offense. A category output of "Alcohols related offenses-Does not meet" does not identify that conviction either. Considering the block "in part" because of a conviction, without naming it, violates the same subdivision.
3. The words "Company's eligibility criteria" and "Does not meet," with no statement of the Manager Trainee duties, no statement of time passed, and no statement of gravity, are a category result. Section 12952(c)(1) required the weighing even though subdivision (c)(1)(B) does not require the employer to write the weighing down. The violation to plead is that the decision was reached by the category. Do not plead that the statute required a particular outcome. The court record is serious. The offense date is February 26, 2017, the disposition is January 12, 2018, and the report prints a parole/release date of December 2, 2020. More than eight years passed between the offense and the offer. An assessment would have had to weigh that gravity, that time, and the specific duties. The letter does none of it. Plead the skip. Do not plead that gravity was slight.
4. The deadline line is an unfilled bracket. It does not state a date. It recites conflicting clocks: five business days from the date of the letter, five business days from receipt, and a deemed email receipt two business days after sending. That is a violation of section 12952(c)(2)(C) and regulation 11017.1(c)(2)(E). Leaving the applicant unable to tell when the right to respond closes also supports section 12952(a)(4), interference with the right this section gives him.
5. The chemical-test suspension prints a reinstatement date of May 19, 2026, and the disqualification prints a clear date of November 30, 2025. Both had passed before the offer. If the company contends the category refers to those license actions, the category still does not account for the end dates printed in the company's own report. That fact supports "this was a matrix," and it is mitigation. It is not a separate count, and it is not an allegation that the license is currently valid. Only the report's printed dates are in the file.

**PLEAD the elements that are already complete.** The conditional offer was made before the inquiry. The notice, if conviction history is part of the decision, was sent on November 2 and is missing the conviction identification and a deadline. Those notice facts do not wait for a final denial.

**DO NOT PLEAD** a final denial, a violation of section 12952(c)(5), or lost wages as a present fact, until a final decision or a lost start is documented. When a final denial arrives, amend to add the missing final notice: the denial itself, any internal appeal, and the right to file with the Civil Rights Department. If he disputes accuracy in writing within the first five business days and states that he is taking specific steps to obtain evidence, section 12952(c)(3) gives five more business days. **DO NOT PLEAD** that the company failed to make a conditional offer first, failed to enclose the report, or considered an arrest that did not result in conviction. The no-contest disposition is a conviction. The license actions are not pleaded as arrests.

**Exhaustion.** This count is an unlawful employment practice under the Fair Employment and Housing Act. It is not filed in court until the Civil Rights Department has issued a right-to-sue notice. Government Code sections 12960 through 12965. The charge can be opened now, because the notice already went out, and an immediate right-to-sue can be requested when the charge is filed. It should not say the offer has been finally rescinded, and it should not state a settlement figure.

## Count 2 — Penal Code section 290.46(j)(2)(E) and (j)(4)(A)

Held, not filed, until the company's answer or the source records support the use allegation. Defendant, when it is filed: The Hertz Corporation only. This count incorporates the offer, the registry portion of the report, the October 30 researcher notes, the Department of Justice sentence, the report's "proper use is your responsibility" note, and the letter's statements that the company reviewed the results and that a preliminary decision rescinding the offer exists. It does not incorporate Count 1's conviction-history allegations, and Count 1 does not incorporate this count. In the complaint, each count incorporates only the paragraphs that support it.

Section 290.46 is the Megan's Law internet-website statute, not section 290 standing alone. Subdivision (j)(1) authorizes use of information disclosed pursuant to that section only to protect a person at risk. Subdivision (j)(2)(E) prohibits use of that information for employment, except as (j)(1) or another law allows. Subdivision (j)(3) leaves authorized access under Penal Code sections 11105 and 11105.3, and Labor Code section 432.7, in place. Subdivision (j)(4)(A) provides the remedies: actual damages; an amount determined by the trier of fact not exceeding three times the actual damage and not less than $250; attorney's fees; exemplary damages, or a civil penalty not exceeding $25,000. The "or" is in the statute. Plead the exemplary damages and the civil penalty in the alternative. Do not pray them as a stack. Subdivision (j)(4)(B) is a pattern injunction. One file does not plead a pattern.

**PLEAD.**

- The report furnished to the company contains California Sex Offenders' Registry information. The company's researchers were told, inside the report, before the report was even completed, that this information may not be used for employment except to protect a person at risk, and they were pointed at section 290.46.
- The company reviewed the results. The package those results belong to was adjudicated Client Review Required under the company's own library. The letter describes a preliminary decision rescinding the accepted offer.
- The letter identifies no person at risk at the Palo Alto Manager Trainee job. It does not mention the registry. The protective order printed on case C1761642 is a fact in the report. Do not hide it, and do not allege it is the person-at-risk finding the statute requires. The letter never mentions it. The (j)(1) exception is the company's to establish if the information was information disclosed pursuant to section 290.46.
- On information and belief, the registry information was part of the review that produced the preliminary decision, because the company said it reviewed the results, the results flagged that information for client review, and "including" the alcohol line does not exclude the rest.

**ALTERNATIVE on the source.** Plead that the California registry information the researchers themselves described as subject to section 290.46 is information disclosed pursuant to that section. In the same paragraphs, plead that the report says the search was performed through the United States Department of Justice. Do not allege "from the Megan's Law website" as a fact. The Department of Justice distinguishes its public National Sex Offender Public Website, which reads jurisdictional public sites, from the nonpublic National Sex Offender Registry, which is limited to criminal-justice users. That distinction, set out in the claims audit, is the discovery target: product name, URL or web service, upstream source, query, payload, and time. It is not a pleaded fact. Do not search the Megan's Law website to try to prove the source. If the proof shows a channel saved by subdivision (j)(3), or a decision that never used the registry information, this count fails and Count 1 remains.

*Donaleski v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 2009 WL 1296257 (E.D. Cal. May 8, 2009), is the defense case on causation. An employment decision that rests on an independent court record or on the driving record, and not on website information, is not a section 290.46 use. Confirm the citation against the opinion before a brief relies on it. Count 2 therefore does not allege that the alcohol line is a pretext. It alleges the review, the warning, and the alternative that the flagged registry information was part of the results the letter says the company reviewed. The response in the pleading-architecture memo asks the company to identify every item and say whether each is a conviction, a licensing action, or another record. That question is asked before the company writes an after-the-fact "MVR only" memorandum. If the answer is a documented independent driving-record decision, Count 2 comes out.

**DO NOT PLEAD** HireRight as the user. **DO NOT PLEAD** that the alcohol line and the registry line are the same piece of information. **DO NOT PLEAD** a completed denial as an element. Use, if it happened, happened when the company reviewed the information for this employment decision and stated a preliminary decision rescinding the offer. Wage loss is additional damage, not the element of use.

This is the count a demurrer is aimed at. Keep it short so a sustained demurrer to this count has nothing to take with it.

## Damages, according to proof

No figure in this section is a demand, a counter, or a number to send. No settlement figure is stated. The statutory amounts are the remedies in the code. They are not a walk-away and they are not a floor to open with.
