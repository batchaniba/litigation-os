# Cluster 5 — Carrillo / Vons / Asurint stress test
## Employer use, "person at risk," CRA liability, causation, and discovery architecture

**Compiled:** September 18, 2026  
**Comparator:** *Roberto Sandoval Carrillo v. The Vons Companies, Inc.; One Source Technology LLC d/b/a Asurint*, C.D. Cal. No. 8:25-cv-00191-SRM-KES.  
**Purpose:** Turn the newly obtained Carrillo filings into an adversarial stress test of Cluster 5 rather than treating party briefing as precedent.

---

# 1. Bottom line

Carrillo materially strengthens the **employer-side discovery and causation architecture** of Cluster 5, but it simultaneously exposes a major weakness in the existing **CRA/vendor-side theory**.

The durable takeaways are:

1. A mature § 290.46 employment case can produce **company-wide screening data, standardized adjudication labels, PMK testimony, adverse-action templates, and outcome cohorts**. Cluster 5 discovery should seek those materials expressly.
2. The employer defense is likely to move from **"we did not use Megan's Law information"** to **"the use was authorized to protect a person at risk"** once registry use is proven.
3. The § 290.46(j)(1) exception is **not a blanket safety exception**. The current statute authorizes use only to protect a person at risk. Existing cases make purpose/intent and job-specific statutory authorization central.
4. ***Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Services, Inc.*, 182 Cal.App.4th 1644 (2010)** is a severe obstacle to a direct § 290.46 claim against a CRA that merely accesses, compiles, or republishes registry information.
5. *Mendoza* is also a severe obstacle to an **ICRAA § 1786.20(c)** theory premised solely on the proposition that § 290.46 is an "equal employment opportunity law or regulation."
6. Because FCRA § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii) uses materially similar "equal employment opportunity law or regulation" language, a vendor-side FCRA theory based solely on "CRA knew the employer would violate § 290.46" is **not safe**.
7. A CRA's sending pre-adverse and adverse-action notices **on behalf of an employer does not, without more, make the CRA the employment decisionmaker**.
8. First Advantage-specific cases make the problem sharper: courts repeatedly hold that even applying employer-defined adjudication criteria and returning "eligible / ineligible" can remain CRA activity rather than an adverse employment action.
9. **Matter 31 therefore should not be treated as presumptively additive liability.** It is a conditional vendor-liability investigation. It becomes much stronger only if evidence establishes a genuinely vendor-specific statutory violation: inaccurate reporting, § 1681k failure, invalid/missing employer certification, unauthorized permissible-purpose use, failure to follow a delegated duty, or conduct that crosses from reporting into independent decisionmaking.
10. Employer-side Matters 25/26/27 remain more natural § 290.46 vehicles. Matter 26 remains the cleanest because the report itself identifies the California Sex Offender Registry source and ordinary criminal searches were clear.

# 5. Stress test: "person at risk" is the employer's serious defense

Current Penal Code § 290.46(j)(1):

> A person is authorized to use information disclosed pursuant to this section only to protect a person at risk.

Section 290.46(j)(2)(E) then prohibits employment-related use except as authorized by paragraph (1) or another provision of law.

## A. Mendoza does not decide the employer exception

*Mendoza* says that "a strong argument might be made" that employees are persons at risk whom an employer has a duty to protect from a sexually violent predator.

That statement is **dictum**. The defendant in Mendoza was the screening company, not the employer, and the court disposed of the § 290.46 claim because the CRA merely reproduced / republished the information rather than acting on it.

Do not write that Mendoza "holds employees are persons at risk."

## B. Cross v. Cooper is useful on purpose / intent

***Cross v. Cooper*, 197 Cal.App.4th 357 (2011)** explains that the propriety of a § 290.46 use depends on whether the use was **intended to protect a person at risk**. In Cross, intent was a contested factual issue.

The Cluster 5 answer to a safety defense therefore should be evidence-driven:

- Who supposedly was the person at risk?
- What concrete job duty created the risk?
- Was the analysis written at the time?
- What information about the conviction was considered?
- Was there individualized assessment?
- Was there a categorical rule?
- Did the employer consider recency, actual duties, supervision, work location, access to private homes/minors/vulnerable populations, and mitigating information?
- Was "safety" articulated contemporaneously or invented in litigation?

## C. Pastrana shows the much stronger "other provision of law" defense

***Pastrana v. Local 9509 / Pacific Bell*** is dangerous where a defendant can identify a **specific statute affirmatively authorizing** the use for the exact job.

PacBell was a public utility, and the plaintiff's position required entering private residences. Penal Code § 11105(c)(10)(A) specifically authorized public utilities to obtain criminal history for employees entering private residences. The court held that authorization brought PacBell within § 290.46's savings clause.

Cluster lesson:

> Generalized "we are allowed to conduct background checks" is not the same as Pastrana. Search for a specific statutory authorization tied to the employer and actual job.

# 5A. Stress test: "any other provision of law" / Labor Code § 432.7 is not a universal escape hatch

Current § 290.46(j)(2) permits employment-related use if authorized by paragraph (1) **or any other provision of law**, and § 290.46(j)(3) specifically says the section does not affect authorized access to or use of information pursuant to listed statutes including Labor Code § 432.7.

That cross-reference should not be read as a blanket rule that every employer may use Megan's Law information merely because § 432.7 allows consideration of some convictions.

Current Labor Code § 432.7 is primarily a **restriction** on employer use of certain arrest / diversion / sealed or dismissed records. Its relevant exceptions are narrower:

- subdivision (m) permits inquiry concerning a **particular conviction** where federal/state law requires the employer to obtain that conviction information, requires a firearm, prohibits someone with that conviction from the position, or prohibits the employer from hiring them;
- subdivision (n) preserves compliance where the employer is actually **required by law** to conduct criminal background checks or restrict employment based on criminal history.

Therefore, if a Cluster 5 defendant invokes § 432.7, force it to identify the external law that creates the requirement / prohibition for the actual job. A company background-check policy is not itself the external law.

### Transportation matters

Current FMCSA materials require motor carriers to investigate driving records, safety-performance history, and—where applicable—drug/alcohol Clearinghouse status. FMCSA guidance also states that a felony conviction does **not necessarily disqualify** a CDL holder from CMV operation unless the governing disqualification rules apply. Nothing located in this pass creates a general federal motor-carrier requirement to query California's sex-offender registry for ordinary freight-driver employment.

That matters to UPS / FedEx / Swift / RK:

- a required MVR, prior-employer safety inquiry, or Clearinghouse query can be lawful and mandatory;
- it does not automatically authorize use of Megan's Law website-derived information;
- Defendants should identify the precise statute/regulation if they contend the particular conviction or registration status legally disqualified Plaintiff.

By contrast, *Pastrana* involved a genuinely specific authorization: a public utility and a statute expressly permitting criminal-history access for employees who enter private residences.

# 7. CRA stress test: Mendoza is the controlling California obstacle

This is the most important correction generated by the Carrillo packet.

## A. Direct § 290.46 against a CRA

*Mendoza* held that the Legislature distinguished **use** from reproduction / republication. The statutory damages remedy did not extend to a screening business that merely accessed, compiled, and republished MLW information without itself acting on it.

The opinion's bottom line:

> the cause of action, if any, lies against the prospective employer, not the screening company.

Therefore:

**Do not add a § 290.46 count against First Advantage or Accurate merely because the CRA sourced / furnished the registry item.**

A viable attempt to distinguish Mendoza would need facts showing conduct beyond republication, such as:

- independent decision authority;
- independently imposed hold / rejection;
- vendor-created criteria rather than employer criteria;
- a vendor policy that itself determines employment consequences;
- contractual delegation of the actual hiring decision.

Even then, do not assume success; analogous FCRA decisions often still treat adjudication against employer-defined criteria as CRA activity.

---

# 8. ICRAA § 1786.20(c): Mendoza is directly adverse

Civil Code § 1786.20(c) bars an investigative CRA from making an employment-purpose inquiry where the employer's making of the inquiry would violate applicable federal or state **equal employment opportunity law or regulation**.

*Mendoza* rejected an ICRAA claim based on Megan's Law because the plaintiff's predicate assumed that § 290.46 was such an equal-employment-opportunity law.

The court reasoned that Megan's Law's restriction did not make registered sex offenders a protected class and saw no basis to treat the statute as an employment-discrimination law for § 1786.20(c).

That is binding published California intermediate appellate authority absent contrary California Supreme Court law.

**Matter 31 consequence:** an ICRAA § 1786.20(c) claim cannot safely be predicated simply on:
> First Advantage queried Megan's Law, and the employer could not lawfully use it for employment.

Find an **independent ICRAA violation** or demote the theory.

---

# 9. FCRA § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii): same-phrase problem

FCRA § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii) requires the employer to certify to the CRA that report information will not be used in violation of any applicable federal or state **equal employment opportunity law or regulation**.

Carrillo's FCRA theory against Asurint treats § 290.46 as the qualifying law and argues knowledge of Vons's actual use defeats reliance on the certification.

The defense stress test is strong:

1. The federal text requires a certification.
2. Asurint says Vons gave the certification.
3. *Mendoza* interpreted materially similar language in ICRAA and rejected Megan's Law as an equal-employment-opportunity law.
4. No binding Ninth Circuit authority located in this pass says § 290.46 is an EEO law for § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii).
5. Courts addressing § 1681b(b)(1) generally treat the CRA's duty as obtaining the employer certification, not auditing each downstream hiring decision.

**Conclusion:** do not make § 1681b(b)(1)(A)(ii) the load-bearing Matter 31 theory unless:
- employer certification was missing / facially invalid; or
- new controlling authority supports treating § 290.46 as the qualifying EEO law.

The Carrillo plaintiff may ultimately win that dispute, but party briefing is not law.

---

# 10. FCRA § 1681e(b): lawful-but-harmful is not the same as inaccurate

Section 1681e(b) requires reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy.

If First Advantage accurately reports:
> the subject is registered in California

then the fact that an employer may be legally prohibited from **using** that accurate information does not itself make the report inaccurate.

A § 1681e(b) claim needs a real accuracy / misleadingness problem, for example:

- wrong person;
- stale registration status;
- wrong jurisdiction;
- materially incomplete disposition;
- duplicate / mismatched record;
- source falsely attributed;
- misleading presentation that materially changes meaning.

Do not plead § 1681e(b) as a disguised § 290.46 use claim.

---

# 11. FCRA § 1681k: a more natural vendor-side inquiry

Section 1681k applies where a CRA furnishes employment-purpose public-record information likely to have an adverse effect.

The CRA must either:

1. notify the consumer at the time the public-record information is reported, including the recipient's name/address; or
2. maintain strict procedures designed to ensure the public-record information is complete and up to date.

This is fact-specific and potentially more natural than forcing § 290.46 into § 1681b(b)(1).

Cluster work:
- identify whether each First Advantage / Accurate / InfoMart notice is actually a § 1681k notice;
- identify report-to-employer timestamp;
- compare consumer-notice timestamp;
- determine whether the notice identifies the employer;
- if relying on strict procedures instead, discover those procedures;
- determine whether the registry data was complete/current.

# 12. Sending adverse-action notices does not automatically make the CRA the employer

Carrillo's plaintiff emphasizes that Asurint sent § 613 notices, pre-adverse notices, and adverse notices on Vons's behalf.

That proves workflow involvement and potentially knowledge.

It does **not automatically prove the CRA made the employment decision.**

This matters directly to Matter 31 because the current prose says First Advantage itself "generated and transmitted every notice."

First Advantage-specific federal decisions repeatedly distinguish:

- generating a report;
- applying employer-defined criteria;
- sending notices pursuant to employer workflow;

from:

- possessing authority to deny employment.

### *Muir v. Early Warning Services / First Advantage*
Even a "Decline" message did not make First Advantage the adverse-action decisionmaker where Wells Fargo retained final authority.

### *Frazier v. First Advantage*
The court rejected allegations that First Advantage's employer-criteria coding converted it from CRA to user / decisionmaker where the employer retained ultimate decision authority.

### *Williams v. First Advantage*
First Advantage's adjudication under employer criteria was treated as an evaluation leading to a decision, not itself the adverse employment decision.

### *Goode v. LexisNexis*
Represents the more plaintiff-favorable edge case where the employer effectively adopted the CRA coding wholesale. But later courts frequently distinguish / decline to follow it.

**Matter 31 consequence:** notice letterhead is not enough. Discover delegation and actual authority.

Not:

> "First Advantage furnished two registry hits, therefore additive FCRA/ICRAA liability exists."

## Claims to test, not assume

### Direct § 290.46
**Presumptively weak under Mendoza** unless evidence shows First Advantage itself acted on the information rather than merely reporting it.

### FCRA § 1681b(b)(1)(A)
Potential only if:
- employer certification was absent, facially deficient, or otherwise legally invalid on an independently actionable ground.

Do not rely solely on "the employer later violated § 290.46."

### FCRA § 1681e(b)
Potential only with a provable accuracy / materially misleading report problem.

### FCRA § 1681k
Potentially viable depending notice timing / strict procedures / completeness-currentness.

### FCRA § 1681i
Potential if a real dispute was made and reinvestigation failed; not currently the core facts.

### ICRAA
Identify an independent provision actually violated. Do not rest § 1786.20(c) on Megan's Law after Mendoza.

### UCL
Derivative. No stronger than the underlying surviving violation, and federal equitable-standing / adequate-remedy rules may matter if filed federally.

### Negligence per se
Treat as evidentiary doctrine / derivative theory, not an independent substitute for a failed statutory duty.

# 21. Pleading discipline after Carrillo

## Safe employer allegation
> Defendant used information disclosed from the California Megan's Law source in making or implementing the challenged employment decision.

Use only where the report / admission / evidence supports it.

## Unsafe leap
> Because a CRA ran a background check and the applicant was later rejected, the rejection necessarily resulted from Megan's Law.

Lerma / Donaleski destroy that shortcut.

## Safe vendor allegation
> The CRA performed [specific statutory act], and that conduct violated [specific CRA duty].

## Unsafe vendor allegation
> The employer's unlawful use automatically makes the CRA liable for furnishing accurate information.

Mendoza is the answer to that.

# 24. Final strategic assessment

Carrillo **raises** the value of Cluster 5 as an employer-side litigation program because it demonstrates that discovery can expose standardized registry classifications and applicant outcomes.

It **lowers** confidence in a broad CRA liability program.

That is a productive result.

The cluster should be split conceptually into:

### Employer track
**Source → receipt → adjudication → actual use → exception / authorization → decision → damages.**

### CRA track
**Specific CRA duty → specific CRA breach → injury.**

Do not bridge the CRA track with the employer's alleged § 290.46 violation unless controlling law actually supplies that bridge.

This separation makes the entire cluster harder to attack.
