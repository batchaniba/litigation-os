19                                             INTRODUCTION

20

21             The demurrer can be resolved on settled pleading and statutory-construction principles.

22   CHP's own contemporaneous records—its dashcam audio-video recording, its sworn

23   arrest-investigation report, and its Penal Code section 851.6 detention certificate stating that no

24   accusatory pleading was filed and reclassifying the incident as a detention only for insufficient

25   grounds for making a criminal complaint—were all placed before the certifying official as part of

26   Petitioner's April 9, 2026 certification request. (Pet. ¶¶ 55-58, Exs. 2-8.) CHP's denial letter does

27   not mention any of them. It does not identify which statutory element of Penal Code section

28   679.10 it found unsatisfied. It does not name a single enumerated qualifying offense. It does not

                                                    Page 1
               OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1   acknowledge the statutory rebuttable presumption of helpfulness. (Pet. ¶ 62.)

 2

 3          Respondent's demurrer asks the Court to do three things it cannot do at the pleading stage.

 4   First, it asks the Court to resolve disputed factual questions—whether Petitioner was the victim

 5   of qualifying criminal activity—that are merits questions for the writ hearing. Second, it asks the

 6   Court to adopt a construction of Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2) that would

 7   eliminate the written-explanation requirement AB 1261 enacted—reading the first sentence of

 8   subdivision (i)(2) out of the statute entirely. Third, it asks the Court to import into subdivision (h)

 9   a threshold requirement—that the agency must first request cooperation before the presumption

10   of helpfulness attaches—that appears nowhere in the text and would place the agency in sole

11   control of whether the presumption ever operates.

12

13          Petitioner does not ask this Court, on demurrer, to decide disputed facts or to order

14   execution of Form I-918, Supplement B on the pleadings alone. Petitioner seeks judicial review

15   of CHP's compliance with the mandatory procedures governing a denial under section

16   679.10—including the duty to apply the governing statutory framework, consider the helpfulness

17   presumption, and provide an adequate written explanation. At minimum, the appropriate remedy

18   is vacatur and remand for a new determination under the correct legal standards. The Petition

19   alleges each element of each cause of action; the demurrer should be overruled. In the alternative,

20   leave to amend should be freely granted.

19                                          LEGAL STANDARD

20

21          A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading. The Court "treat[s] the demurrer as

22   admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of

23   fact or law." (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) Facts appearing in exhibits attached to

24   the pleading and incorporated by reference are also accepted as true. (Barnett v. Fireman's Fund

25   Ins. Co. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 500, 505.) A demurrer must be overruled if the pleading states a

26   cause of action on any theory. (Longshore v. County of Ventura (1979) 25 Cal.3d 14, 21.) The

27   same standards govern demurrers to mandamus petitions. (Pinto Lake MHP LLC v. County of

28   Santa Cruz (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 1006, 1012.) If any defect can be cured by amendment, leave

                                                    Page 3
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1   to amend must be granted. (McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th

 2   72, 78.)

 3

 4          Under these standards, the Petition survives demurrer because: (a) it alleges CHP failed to

 5   apply mandatory statutory standards to CHP's own evidentiary record; (b) it incorporates by

 6   reference CHP's own documents—the dashcam transcript, the detention certificate, the arrest

 7   report—which support rather than negate the allegations; and (c) it states a justiciable controversy

 8   over whether CHP's conclusory denial satisfies section 679.10.

10                                              ARGUMENT

11

12   I. RESPONDENT'S DEMURRER IMPROPERLY ASKS THE COURT TO

13      RESOLVE DISPUTED MERITS QUESTIONS AT THE PLEADING STAGE.

14

15          Respondent's brief repeatedly argues that the underlying incident does not, in fact,

16   constitute qualifying criminal activity. (See Dem. MPA at 13-15.) That argument mistakes the

17   office of a demurrer. On demurrer, a court "may not consider . . . the truth of the allegations, but

18   only whether they state a cause of action." (Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General

19   Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 213-214.) The Petition alleges that Petitioner is the victim of

20   enumerated qualifying offenses and cites the specific documentary evidence supporting each.

21   (Pet. ¶¶ 4, 34-46, 62(a)-(h), 74-76.) Whether that record ultimately proves qualifying criminal

22   activity is a merits question for the writ hearing—not a pleading question for demurrer.

23

24          The Petition incorporates by reference fourteen exhibits, including the denial letter itself

25   (Ex. 1), Petitioner's eight-page legal analysis identifying four contradictions by dashcam

26   timestamp (Ex. 3), the verbatim dashcam transcript (Ex. 5), the CHP arrest report containing the

27   disputed statements (Ex. 6), and the section 851.6 detention certificate (Ex. 7). On demurrer, the

28   Court must consider these exhibits as part of the pleading. (Barnett, supra, 90 Cal.App.4th at p.

                                                   Page 4
                OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1   505.) These are not mere allegations; they are documents in CHP's own custody that were placed

 2   before the certifying official and are admitted for all purposes on demurrer. Respondent's

 3   insistence that Petitioner's transcript "undermines" his allegations (Dem. MPA at 15) asks the

 4   Court to weigh competing inferences from documents—precisely what demurrer law prohibits.

 5

 6          This conclusion is reinforced by a parallel federal proceeding. In Newanforbi v. Serrato,

 7   No. 4:26-cv-05953-ASK (N.D. Cal.), the United States District Court reviewed substantially the

 8   same underlying allegations—based on the same dashcam recording, the same arrest report, and

 9   the same detention certificate—and concluded at screening that Petitioner's pleaded facts

10   plausibly support inferences of false imprisonment based on fabricated evidence and a plausible

11   retaliation claim arising from the certification denial itself. (Order dated June 29, 2026, at 3-4.)

12   That order is not binding on this Court and does not decide California mandate law, but it is

13   persuasive authority that Respondent's competing factual inferences do not render Petitioner's

14   allegations facially deficient at the pleading stage.

15

16   II. THE FIRST CAUSE OF ACTION STATES A CLAIM: SECTION 679.10

17      DOES NOT PERMIT CHP TO DENY CERTIFICATION UNDER AN

18      UNSTATED OR LEGALLY ERRONEOUS STANDARD.

19

20          Section 679.10 uses mandatory language throughout: a certifying entity "shall process"

21   requests within the statutory deadline (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (j)); "shall fully complete and

22   sign" the Form I-918, Supplement B (id., subd. (i)(1)); and "shall certify victim helpfulness"

23   when the statutory conditions are met (id., subd. (g)(1)). "Shall" is mandatory. (Gov. Code, § 14;

24   Haggis v. City of Los Angeles (2000) 22 Cal.4th 490, 498-499; Common Cause v. Board of

25   Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 443.)

26

27          Mandamus law distinguishes between the duty to act and the manner of acting. Mandamus

28   "does not lie to compel a public agency to exercise discretionary powers in a particular manner,

                                                    Page 5
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1   only to compel it to exercise them in some manner"—that is, under the legal standards the

 2   Legislature has prescribed. (Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442.)

 3   A public entity likewise "has a ministerial duty to comply with its own rules and regulations

 4   where they are valid and unambiguous." (Kern County Hospital Authority v. Dept. of Corrections

 5   & Rehabilitation (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1313, 1325.) Galzinski v. Somers (2016) 2 Cal.App.5th

 6   1164 applied these principles to a law enforcement agency: even though the substance of a police

 7   department's response to a citizen complaint was discretionary, mandamus lay to compel the

 8   department to perform its procedural obligations—to conduct an investigation sufficient to

 9   support a finding, and to make one of the enumerated findings. (Id. at pp. 1171-1173; accord, CV

10   Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 283 [agency's "failure to

11   follow its own procedures provides the basis for the issuance of a traditional writ of mandate"].)

12   Here the procedural obligations arise not from a published internal procedure but from a

13   statute—a stronger footing still.

14

15          Accordingly, even assuming CHP retains fact-finding judgment concerning whether a

16   requestor is a victim of qualifying criminal activity, ordinary mandate reaches CHP's failure to

17   exercise that judgment under the controlling statutory framework, its failure to apply the

18   mandatory presumption, and action taken in derogation of statutory standards. (Common Cause,

19   supra, 49 Cal.3d at p. 442; Cal. Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995)

20   10 Cal.4th 1133, 1154.) The Court need not decide now whether the pleaded record compels

21   certification. It need decide only whether the Petition plausibly alleges that CHP failed to

22   perform, or lawfully exercise, duties section 679.10 imposes.

23

24          The Petition alleges the denial: (a) applied an unstated "standard" nowhere found in

25   section 679.10; (b) ignored the enumerated qualifying offenses the statute incorporates by

26   reference from 8 U.S.C. section 1101(a)(15)(U)(iii); (c) failed to address CHP's own detention

27   certificate reflecting that no accusatory pleading was filed—a material agency record submitted

28   with the request; (d) failed to address CHP's own dashcam recording; and (e) gave no explanation

                                                   Page 6
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1   whether it improperly treated the absence of a charge, investigation, or prosecution as

 2   dispositive—considerations subdivision (k) prohibits from serving as a basis for refusal. (Pet. ¶¶

 3   62-63, 74-76.) The Petition plausibly alleges that CHP failed to apply the governing standards to

 4   the material records before it. That is sufficient to state a claim under section 1085.

 5

 6          Respondent's reliance on Catholic Charities CYO v. Chertoff (N.D. Cal. 2008) 622

 7   F.Supp.2d 865 (Dem. MPA at 8) is misplaced. Catholic Charities construed the federal U-visa

 8   statute in 2008, before California enacted section 679.10 in 2015 and long before AB 1261's 2024

 9   amendments imposed the procedural obligations at issue here. The Legislature enacted section

10   679.10 precisely because federal law was permissive and the resulting unfettered discretion had

11   produced systemic under-certification. A pre-statute federal decision does not defeat a state-law

12   writ claim that turns on the state statute's mandatory commands.

13

14          Petitioner has also pleaded the two remaining writ elements: a "clear, present and

15   beneficial" right because CHP's denial forecloses access to the certification required to pursue a

16   U-visa petition and leaves Petitioner without an administrative mechanism to obtain review (Pet.

17   ¶¶ 51-54, 76); and no adequate alternative remedy—section 679.10 provides no administrative

18   appeal, USCIS has no power to compel state certification, and no comparable state-court process

19   exists (Pet. ¶ 77; Code Civ. Proc., § 1086).

21   III. THE SECOND CAUSE OF ACTION STATES A CLAIM: RESPONDENT'S

22      READING OF SECTION 679.10(i)(2) WOULD NULLIFY THE WRITTEN-

23      EXPLANATION REQUIREMENT AB 1261 ENACTED.

24

25          Section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2) contains two sentences:

26

27                  "If a certifying entity does not certify a Form I-918 Supplement B certification,

28          they shall provide a written explanation for the denial of the Form I-918 Supplement B

                                                    Page 7
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1          certification. The written denial shall include specific details of any reasonable requests

 2          for cooperation and a detailed description of how the victim refused to cooperate."

 3

 4          The first sentence is a stand-alone command: the entity "shall provide a written

 5   explanation for the denial." The second sentence adds a specific application: if the denial rests on

 6   non-cooperation, the explanation must include details about the requests and the refusal. The

 7   second sentence supplements the first; it does not replace it. (Manufacturers Life Ins. Co. v.

 8   Superior Court (1995) 10 Cal.4th 257, 274; DuBois v. Workers' Comp. Appeals Bd. (1993) 5

 9   Cal.4th 382, 388 ["[A] construction making some words surplusage is to be avoided."].)

10

11          The two-sentence structure is not accidental. The first sentence is the general rule: any

12   denial must be accompanied by a written explanation. The second sentence is a specific

13   application for cooperation-based denials. To read the second sentence as the only requirement

14   would mean that an agency could deny certification based on a failure to prove

15   victimization—the most common and most consequential category of denial—with no

16   explanation at all. That is precisely the result AB 1261 was enacted to prevent.

17

18          The legislative history removes any doubt. The Legislative Counsel's Digest of AB

19   1261—which "is printed as a preface to every bill considered by the Legislature" and is entitled to

20   great weight in construing the enactment (Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42

21   Cal.4th 1158, 1169-1170)—describes the requirement without qualification: "The bill would

22   require a certifying entity that does not certify the form regarding 'victim helpfulness' to provide a

23   written explanation for the denial of the certification." (Legis. Counsel's Dig., Assem. Bill No.

24   1261 (2023-2024 Reg. Sess.), Stats. 2023, ch. 679.) The Digest states the written-explanation

25   duty as a free-standing obligation attaching to every non-certification—not as a duty confined to

26   cooperation-based denials. Had the Legislature intended the narrower duty Respondent proposes,

27   the Digest would have said so. The Court may take judicial notice of the Digest as an official

28   legislative act. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (c).)

                                                    Page 8
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1           The Attorney General's Information Bulletin No. 2024-DLE-05, incorporated by reference

 2   into the Petition (Pet. ¶ 32, Ex. 10), instructs law enforcement agencies statewide that the

 3   reason-for-denial "should be clearly articulated and specific." The Attorney General's

 4   construction is entitled to "substantial weight." (Yamaha Corp. of America v. State Bd. of

 5   Equalization (1998) 19 Cal.4th 1, 12-14.) Respondent's demurrer does not mention the Bulletin,

 6   does not distinguish it, and does not offer any competing agency interpretation.

 7

 8           A legally adequate explanation need not resemble a judicial opinion or discuss every item

 9   submitted. But it must state enough of the actual ground for denial to disclose whether CHP

10   rejected the request because it found no qualifying criminal activity, no substantially similar

11   activity, insufficient victim status, lack of helpfulness, or another legally permissible basis. CHP's

12   letter merely restates the ultimate conclusion and does not disclose the operative reasoned basis

13   for that conclusion. (Pet. ¶¶ 62, 82.) The ministerial character of the reason-giving duty is not in

14   serious dispute: like the duty to investigate and make findings enforced in Galzinski, the duty to

15   explain is a procedural obligation that does not depend on the merits of the underlying

16   certification decision. (Galzinski, supra, 2 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1171-1173; cf. Topanga Assn. for a

17   Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-515.) The second cause of

18   action states a claim.

20   IV. THE THIRD CAUSE OF ACTION STATES A CLAIM: RESPONDENT'S

21      CIRCULAR CONSTRUCTION OF SUBDIVISION (h) FLIPS THE

22      PRESUMPTION OF HELPFULNESS ON ITS HEAD.

23

24           Section 679.10, subdivision (h) provides: "[T]here is a rebuttable presumption that a

25   victim is helpful, has been helpful, or is likely to be helpful to the detection, investigation, or

26   prosecution of that qualifying criminal activity, if the victim has not refused or failed to provide

27   information and assistance reasonably requested."

28

                                                    Page 9
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1          Respondent argues the presumption does not apply because Petitioner has not alleged that

 2   CHP requested cooperation. (Dem. MPA at 17.) That construction would render the presumption

 3   meaningless. Under Respondent's reading: (a) the presumption applies only if the agency first

 4   requests cooperation; (b) but if the agency never requests cooperation, the presumption never

 5   attaches; (c) thus, agencies could defeat the presumption simply by never asking for

 6   help—placing the agency in sole control of whether the presumption ever operates. That is the

 7   opposite of what a statutory presumption does.

 8

 9          The statute expressly makes refusal or failure to provide reasonably requested assistance

10   the relevant condition. The Petition alleges no request, refusal, or failure; it further alleges

11   affirmative provision of information through the citizen complaint, the POST complaint, the

12   DMV proceedings, and production of the dashcam transcript. (Pet. ¶¶ 7, 87.) CHP's denial letter

13   contains no finding of refusal or failure to cooperate and identifies no fact that could rebut the

14   presumption. (Pet. ¶ 62(f).) At minimum, those allegations plausibly require CHP to address the

15   presumption rather than silently disregard it. The failure to apply a controlling statutory

16   presumption is itself reviewable by mandamus. (Cf. Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th

17   805, 819.)

18

19   V. THE FOURTH CAUSE OF ACTION STATES A CLAIM: AN ACTUAL

20      CONTROVERSY EXISTS OVER THE CONSTRUCTION OF SECTION 679.10.

21

22          Respondent argues declaratory relief is "advisory" or "moot." (Dem. MPA at 17-20.)

23   Neither succeeds. The Petition asks the Court to declare the parties' rights with respect to a

24   concrete controversy: whether CHP's April 20, 2026 denial letter satisfies subdivision (i)(2). (Pet.

25   ¶ 93(c).) That is an "actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective

26   parties." (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060; Linda Vista Village San Diego Homeowners Assn., Inc. v.

27   Tecolote Investors, LLC (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 166, 181.)

28

                                                   Page 10
             OPPOSITION TO DEMURRER — NEWANFORBI v. CALIFORNIA HIGHWAY PATROL, ET AL.
 1          Griset v. Fair Political Practices Commission (2001) 25 Cal.4th 688 (Dem. MPA at

 2   19-20) does not support dismissal at the pleading stage. Griset held that once writ claims were

 3   adjudicated, declaratory relief was resolved by res judicata. (Id. at pp. 697-700.) Griset did not

 4   hold—and could not hold—that a declaratory cause of action is subject to demurrer merely

 5   because it might later be resolved by the writ decision. Petitioner does not seek an abstract

 6   construction of section 679.10 divorced from an actual dispute. If the Court concludes declaratory

 7   relief is duplicative of ordinary mandate, Petitioner requests leave to treat the requested

 8   declaration as ancillary to the mandate remedy rather than dismissing it without leave to amend.

 9

10   VI. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, LEAVE TO AMEND SHOULD BE GRANTED.

11

12          Should the Court sustain any part of the demurrer, leave to amend should be granted

13   freely. "[I]t is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any

14   reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action." (Kong v. City of Hawaiian

15   Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037.) This is the first challenge

16   to the pleading; leave to amend has not previously been denied.

17

18          If the Court identifies any pleading gap, Petitioner can amend to: (1) clarify that the

19   requested principal remedy is vacatur and remand for a new decision under the correct statutory

20   standards, rather than an immediate compelled certification; (2) identify more specifically the

21   factual basis of each alleged qualifying or substantially similar criminal activity; (3) plead with

22   greater specificity the materials submitted to CHP and the absence of any refusal of a reasonable

23   request for assistance; (4) distinguish the detention certificate as evidence that no accusatory

24   pleading was filed, rather than an adjudication of probable cause; and (5) narrow the requested

25   declaratory relief to the concrete controversy concerning the legal sufficiency of CHP's denial

26   procedure. No prejudice would result from granting leave to amend.
