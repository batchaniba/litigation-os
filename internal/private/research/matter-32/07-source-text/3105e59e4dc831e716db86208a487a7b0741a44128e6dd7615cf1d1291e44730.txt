23   II.   LEGAL BACKGROUND REGARDING U-VISA CERTIFICATION.
24         Federal immigration law allows an individual s in the United States to apply for a U visa,
25   when the individual has suffered “substantial physical or mental abuse” as a result of enumerated
26   qualifying “criminal activity” and the individual has been helpful to the investigation or
27   prosecution of the crime. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(U)(i),(iii) (2012). In 2000, Congress created

 1   the U visa as part of the reauthorization of the Violence Against Women Act to provide

 2   immigrant crime victims an avenue to obtain lawful immigration status and thus encourage

 3   cooperation with law enforcement by undocumented victims of crime. Senate Rules Committee

 4   Report, 2015 California Senate Bill No. 674, California 2015-2016 Regular Session (May 31,

 5   2015)/ As articulated by a co-sponsor of the Act’s reauthorization, one of the primary objectives

 6   was to “remov[e] obstacles inadvertently interposed by our immigration laws that many [sic]

 7   hinder or prevent battered immigrants from fleeing domestic violence safely and prosecuting their

 8   abusers by allowing an abusive citizen or lawful permanent resident to blackmail the abused

 9   spouse through threats related to the abused spouse’s immigration status.” 146 Cong. Rec. S10,

10   192 (daily ed. Oct. 11, 2000) (statement of Sen. Hatch).

11         As part of his or her application, a U visa applicant must file a Form I-918, Supplement B,

12   U Nonimmigrant Status Certification (“Form B”) signed by a qualifying law enforcement agency

13   or other certifying entity. 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(2)(i) (2007). The certification must state that the

14   applicant “has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful” in the investigation or

15   prosecution of criminal activity. 8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(1). Those qualified to certify Form B

16   include state or local law enforcement officials. 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(3). Under federal law, the

17   denial to certify a Form B is a “discretionary act and not subject to judicial review.” Catholic

18   Charities CYO v. Chertoff, 622 F. Supp. 2d 865, 887 (N.D. Cal. 2008) (“[T]he statute does not

19   contain any mandatory language with regard to actions by law enforcement officials.

20   Specifically, there is no statutory directive to law enforcement officials to issue [law enforcement

21   certifications].”). As a result, some law enforcement agencies declined to issue Form B

22   certifications altogether. See Leslye E. Orloff et. al., Mandatory U-Visa Certification

23   Unnecessarily Undermines the Purpose of the Violence Against Women Act's Immigration

24   Protections and Its "Any Credible Evidence" Rules-A Call for Consistency, 11 Geo. J. Gender &

25   L. 619, 637 (2010) (describing various police departments nationwide that stopped issuing Form

26   B certifications).

 3   survive a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff's complaint “must contain sufficient factual matter,

 7   the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S.

 8   at 678.

 9         Although generally in reviewing a motion to dismiss the court must accept as true all

10   allegations contained in the subject Complaint, “threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of

11   action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id. at 678.

12         B.    Plaintiff’s First Cause of Action for Violation of 14th Amendment
                 Substantive Due Process Fails as There is No Constitutional or Statutory
13               Right To U-Visa Certification
14         42 U.S.C. Section 1983 “provides a cause of action for the deprivation of any rights,

15   privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws of the United States.” Wilder v.

16   Virginia Hosp. Ass'n, 496 U.S. 498, 508 (1990) (internal quotation marks omitted). “Section 1983

17   is not itself a source of substantive rights but merely provides a method for vindicating federal

18   rights elsewhere conferred.” Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S. 266, 271 (1994) (citation and internal

19   quotation marks omitted). To state a cognizable Section 1983 claim, a plaintiff must allege the

20   violation of a right protected by the Constitution and laws of the United States, and that the

21   alleged deprivation was committed by a person who acted under color of state law. 42 U.S.C. §

22   1983. See also Florer v. Congregation Pidyon Shevuyim, N.A., 639 F.3d 916, 921 (9th Cir. 2011).

23   An individual defendant is not liable on a civil rights claim unless the facts establish the

24   defendant's personal involvement in the constitutional deprivation or a causal connection between

25   the defendant's wrongful conduct and the alleged constitutional deprivation. See Hansen v. Black,

26   885 F.2d 642, 645 (9th Cir. 1989); Johnson v. Duffy, 588 F.2d 740, 743-44 (9th Cir. 1978).

 2         Here, Plaintiff’s Fourteenth Amendment substantive due process cause of action fails as a

 3   matter of law because Plaintiff has no substantive due process right to U-visa certification. See

 4   e.g. Perez Perezv. Wolf 943 F.3d 853 [noting the decision to sign or decline to sign a certification

 5   is discretionary].) United States v. Biao, No. 98CR2812-BTM, 2011 WL 607087, at *1 (S.D. Cal.

 6   Feb. 11, 2011) (citing Orosco v. Napolitano, 598 F.3d 222, 226 (5th Cir.2010).)

 7         Although, California Penal Code Section 679.10 requires that upon request, a certifying

 8   entity “shall fully complete and sign the Form I-918 Supplement B certification,” (Cal Pen. Code,

 9   § 679.10, subd. (i)(1)), nowhere in section 679.10 is there a requirement that the certifying entity

10   must find that the requestor is, in fact, a victim of a qualifying criminal activity, as those terms are

11   defined in the statute, or to find that the requestor has been helpful. In fact, Section 679.10

12   contemplates that certifying entities retain discretion to find otherwise. The statute provides, in

13   relevant part:

14         If a certifying entity does not certify a Form I-918 Supplement B certification, they shall

15         provide a written explanation for the denial of the Form I-918 Supplement B certification.

16   (§ 679.10, subd. (i)(2) (emphasis added).)

17         As U-visa certification is a discretionary act, Plaintiff has no substantive right to a U-visa

18   certification.

11   declaratory relief is not a proper cause of action under the facts alleged in the FAC. Plaintiff’s

 8         F. Defendant Serrato is Entitled to Qualified Immunity as to Each Claim Brought
 9         Against Him in his Personal Capacity as No “Clearly Established” Law Existed to
10         Place Defendant on Notice that a Denial of U-Visa Certification in the Circumstances
11         Alleged Within the FAC Would Violate Any Constitutional Right.
12         “[Q]ualified immunity protects government officials ‘from liability for civil damages
13   insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of
14   which a reasonable person would have known.’” Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009).
15   “When properly applied, it protects all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly
16   violate the law.” Ashcroft v. al Kidd, 563 U.S. 731, 743 (2011) (simplified). “Under [Supreme
17   Court] precedents, law enforcement personnel are entitled to qualified immunity under §1983
18   unless (1) they violated a federal statutory or constitutional right, and (2) the unlawfulness of their
19   conduct was ‘clearly established at the time.’” District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48, 62-63
20   (2018) (emphasis added). A plaintiff must overcome both prongs of qualified immunity. Felarca
21   v. Birgeneau, 891 F.3d 809, 815 (9th Cir. 2018); see also Smith v. Adepa, 81 F.4th 994, 1004 n.4
22   (9th Cir. 2023) (“[T]he burden is not on the officers to prove they fit perfectly within the facts of
23   a case granting qualified immunity; the burden is on the plaintiff to show a violation of a clearly
24   established right in the specific circumstances at issue.”).
25         Here, the FAC alleges that qualified immunity “does not apply.” (FAC 18:9.) However,
26   Plaintiff does not identify any case law that would have placed Defendant Serrato on notice that it
27   would be unlawful to deny Plaintiff’s request for U-visa certification. Instead, Plaintiff alleges
