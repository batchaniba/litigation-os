1. Executive summary: top findings

    1. Absolute immunity is the biggest threat to the damages counts (Counts I–II), and the
       FAC's "she acted as an advocate, not a judge" theory does not escape it as pleaded. Butz
       gives absolute immunity to agency adjudicators. It also extends it to agency officials performing
       functions "analogous to those of a prosecutor" [W/F]. Imbler protects advocacy "intimately
       associated with the judicial phase" [F]. Marshaling and offering exhibits at the hearing is either
       adjudicative or prosecutorial, and both are absolutely immune. The viable path is the Cleavinger
       line: the "independence" and procedural-safeguard factors. Cleavinger denied absolute
       immunity to prison discipline-committee members who were "not 'independent'" [W/F]. Cal.
       DUI Lawyers supports that line: it describes DMV hearing officers as agency employees
       assigned an advocacy role (77 Cal.App.5th 517) [W; text read on Justia]. Reframe ¶¶98 and
       118 around the Cleavinger factors (lack of structural independence and safeguards), not
       around "advocacy." A Westlaw search found no published California or Ninth Circuit decision
       on quasi-judicial immunity for a DMV APS hearing officer. It found two unreported C.D. Cal.
       rulings (Rasmussen v. Cal. DMV, 2009 WL 605784; 2008 WL 5274611) that barred hearing-
       officer claims on quasi-judicial immunity and Heck [WK, search results only]. The Sykes lead
       (9th Cir. 1974) was read in full: it is not a hearing-officer case (see § 2) [O].
    2. Preclusion is a more serious risk than Heck. Under Univ. of Tenn. v. Elliott [F], federal courts
       give an unreviewed state agency's judicial-capacity factfinding the preclusive effect it would get
       in state court. Miller v. County of Santa Cruz and White v. City of Pasadena apply this in the
       Ninth Circuit [F]. Johnson v. City of Loma Linda [F] holds that if a party does not challenge an
       agency's adverse findings by a mandate action, "those findings are binding in later civil actions."
       No timely Veh. Code § 13559 petition (30 days) was filed. The Sacramento mandamus petition
       (No. 26WM000135, filed around May 2026) was abandoned after the fee waiver was denied.
       The best answer is that a hearing without a neutral adjudicator lacks the "judicial

   capacity" and "adequate opportunity to litigate" that preclusion requires. That is a strong
   argument, but it is untested in this specific setting.
3. Mercer is no longer good authority for the proposition the FAC uses it for. Veh. Code §
   40300.5(e) (added in 1996) superseded Mercer's presence-rule holding. Troppman v. Valverde
   (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1121, 1136, fn. 11, says so [WK]. Westlaw flags Mercer yellow, "Superseded
   by Statute as Stated in" Troppman v. Gourley and Dyer [W]. Calling Mercer "binding" and
   "controlling" (¶¶4(b), 59, 91, 102(b)) is a candor problem. Reframe to a no-probable-cause
   and lawful-arrest theory. Troppman, 40 Cal.4th at p. 1137, fn. 12 [WK], preserves the
   requirement that the DMV find a lawful arrest, "dependent upon an officer's possessing probable
   cause" [F].
4. Qualified immunity on Count I is now a serious risk, because the clearly established law
   comes from state intermediate courts that are in open conflict, and the California Supreme
   Court is reviewing the question. Romane (review granted Aug. 13, 2025, S291093) and Chi
   (review granted May 13, 2026, S296201, deferred pending Romane) [WK]. Chi says the Clarke
   line "has taken a wrong turn" [WK]. That makes "beyond debate" very hard to show. Cal. DUI
   Lawyers (2022) expressly held that combining advocate and adjudicator in one DMV hearing
   officer violates the Fourteenth Amendment. It also produced a permanent injunction against
   that practice [read on Justia]. But Supreme Court law requires "controlling authority" or "a
   robust 'consensus of cases of persuasive authority'" (Wesby) [F], so that the question is "beyond
   debate" (al-Kidd) [F]. Knudsen (case-by-case) and the KeyCite-flagged Chi and Romane
   decisions (no advocacy found) undercut "beyond debate." Plead the existing injunction plus
   Candelaria's specific acts. Argue Hope v. Pelzer obviousness and Murchison as the general
   rule. Expect QI to be decided early. Recommended focus: the Gov. Code § 11425.30(b)–(c)

2. Issue 1: Quasi-judicial absolute immunity for the DMV hearing officer

Controlling law.

    • Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478, 511–13 (1978) [W, yellow; core holding intact].
    • Federal administrative law judges and agency officials receive absolute immunity when the role
      is "functionally comparable" to a judge's [F].
    • Officials "performing certain functions analogous to those of a prosecutor" receive the same
      protection [F].
    • The rationale rests on built-in safeguards: insulation from political influence, the adversary
      process, and correctability of error on appeal (511–12) [W].
    • Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193, 202 (1985) [W] lists the six factors: (a) need to perform the
      function without harassment; (b) safeguards that reduce the need for damages actions; (c)
      insulation from political influence; (d) importance of precedent; (e) adversary nature of the
      process; (f) correctability of error on appeal.
    • It denied absolute immunity to prison discipline-committee members. They were "not
      'independent'" and were in a relationship "hardly ... conducive to a truly adjudicatory
      performance" [F].
    • Forrester v. White, 484 U.S. 219, 227–30 (1988) [W]: immunity follows function, not title.
    • Stump v. Sparkman, 435 U.S. 349 (1978), and Mireles v. Waco, 502 U.S. 9 (1991) [F]: judicial
      acts are immune even when erroneous or malicious. The exceptions are non-judicial acts and
      acts "taken in the complete absence of all jurisdiction" [F, phrase matched in Mireles].
    • Imbler v. Pachtman, 424 U.S. 409 (1976) [F]: prosecutorial acts "intimately associated with the
      judicial phase" are absolutely immune.

Ninth Circuit.

    • Mishler v. Clift, 191 F.3d 998 (9th Cir. 1999) [F]: a state medical board receives absolute
      immunity for quasi-judicial acts.
    • Olsen v. Idaho State Bd. of Medicine, 363 F.3d 916 (9th Cir. 2004) [F]: absolute immunity for
      quasi-judicial and quasi-prosecutorial acts. Westlaw notes Olsen as a citing reference on
      Forrester [W].
    • Buckwalter v. Nevada Bd. of Med. Examiners, 678 F.3d 737 (9th Cir. 2012) [F]: applies the
      Cleavinger factors. It addresses absolute immunity for a board's summary license suspension.
    • Swift v. California, 384 F.3d 1184, 1191–92 (9th Cir. 2004) [W]: parole officials are immune for
      adjudicative acts but not for law-enforcement-type acts.
    • DMV hearing officers specifically [WK]. A Westlaw search for quasi-judicial immunity of
      DMV hearing officers returned 0 results in California state courts and the Ninth Circuit. It
      returned 2 unreported C.D. Cal. orders: Rasmussen v. Cal. Dep't of Motor Vehicles, 2008 WL
      5274611, and 2009 WL 605784 (C.D. Cal.). Correction: an earlier version said both held
      hearing-officer claims barred by quasi-judicial immunity. That was wrong. Read on Westlaw,
      neither order decided quasi-judicial immunity; the 2008 order says in fn. 3 that it did not
      reach it. The 2008 order applied Heck because the plaintiff had pleaded nolo contendere to Veh.
      Code § 23152(a), and the suspension stemmed from that conviction. The 2009 order denied the



                                 Privileged research memo — Page 4 of 30
       defendants' request for § 1988 fees (citing Vernon v. City of Los Angeles, 27 F.3d 1385, 1402
       (9th Cir. 1994)). So no case was found that applies the Butz/Cleavinger framework to a
       DMV hearing officer. The immunity defense rests on that framework alone.
     • Sykes v. California (DMV), 497 F.2d 197 (9th Cir. 1974) [O: full text read on OpenJurist
       and CourtListener, Oct. 25, 2026; NOT KeyCited; the pin "201" was not confirmed
       because the free text lacks F.2d page breaks]. This was reported as holding that a DMV
       hearing officer has quasi-judicial immunity. The full text does not support that description.
     • There was no DMV hearing officer and no APS hearing. The DMV defendant, Hoover, was a
       DMV "representative" who investigated a car dealer and had "discretionary power to grant or
       refuse licenses to sell motor vehicles."
     • The court called the district attorneys' immunity "quasi-judicial." It rested Hoover's immunity on
       a different, broader rule: immunity for "public employees acting within the scope of their
       discretionary duties and official functions" (citing Silver v. Dickson, 403 F.2d 642 (9th Cir.
       1968)). Under that rule, "his motives are considered immaterial."
     • It also held the DMV itself is not a "person" under § 1983.
     • Assessment: Sykes is verified to exist and is published Ninth Circuit authority. But it is not a
       serious adverse controlling authority on a hearing officer's adjudicative immunity. Its
       "discretionary function" absolute-immunity rule predates Scheuer v. Rhodes, 416 U.S. 232
       (1974), and Butz (1978). Those cases generally give executive officials only qualified immunity,
       and the modern test is functional (Forrester, Cleavinger). Whether a later Ninth Circuit case
       expressly limited Sykes on this point was not checked [U; KeyCite Sykes before relying on
       this]. Expect Defendants to cite it anyway; the answer is that it predates the functional test and
       involved licensing discretion, not adjudication.
     • Implications for Counts I–II (damages). Even without Sykes, Butz and Cleavinger make
       absolute immunity the defense's strongest argument against Candelaria. With Rasmussen
       corrected, though, Defendants have no DMV-specific case. The argument is by analogy only,
       which helps the Cleavinger route. If it succeeds, Counts I–II end. Absolute immunity does not
       bar prospective Ex parte Young relief against Director Gordon in his official capacity
       (Counts III–IV), because the immunity protects individuals from personal damages liability
       only. (On Heck, Rasmussen is distinguishable because it rested on a conviction. Brendan had
       none; see § 4.)

How it cuts.

     • Against Brendan:
     • Conducting the hearing, ruling on objections, admitting exhibits, and deciding the case are core
       adjudicative acts.
     • Marshaling and offering DMV exhibits is prosecutorial in nature. Under Butz and Imbler, that is
       also absolutely immune. ¶¶98 and 118 argue that advocacy defeats immunity, which gets the
       doctrine backwards.
     • California provides review by writ (Veh. Code § 13559; CCP § 1094.5), which supports the
       "correctability of error" factor.




                                  Privileged research memo — Page 5 of 30
     • The ruling would likely be made on the pleadings, and immunity would defeat the damages
       counts entirely. Counts III–IV against Gordon are unaffected, because absolute immunity does
       not bar official-capacity prospective relief.
     • For Brendan (the Cleavinger route):
     • The APS hearing officer is a subordinate civil-service employee of the agency that is a party, not
       an independent ALJ. Veh. Code § 14112(b) makes only subdivision (a) of Gov. Code §
       11425.30 inapplicable to license proceedings [O, leginfo]. Subdivision (a) is the general bar on a
       presiding officer who served as "investigator, prosecutor, or advocate." Subdivisions (b) and (c)
       are not mentioned in § 14112(b), and § 14112(a) applies the APA to "[a]ll matters in a hearing
       not covered by this chapter" [O]. See § 3A.
     • CDLA describes the hearing officer as performing both advocate and fact-finder roles under
       DMV practice [Justia text; confirm specific record details, such as manager review of decisions,
       before pleading them — U].
     • Those facts map onto Cleavinger factors (b), (c), and (e).
     • The same logic shows that the process lacks the Butz "safeguards" that justify absolute
       immunity.

Strategy.

    1. Amend ¶¶98 and 118, or argue in opposition, using the Cleavinger factors. Rely on CDLA's
       findings about the structure of DMV hearings, and on the Feb. 11, 2025 decision's own wording
       ("no evidence was presented to the contrary").
    2. Identify conduct outside the hearing that is administrative or investigative rather than
       adjudicative, such as acts before the hearing or record-keeping. Forrester and Swift say such acts
       get no absolute immunity. Plead those acts only if they are true.
    3. Preserve the issue for appeal. Denial of absolute immunity is immediately appealable by the
       defendant [U, collateral-order doctrine; verify].
    4. Keep Counts III–IV fully independent of Counts I–II so that an immunity ruling does not take
       down the whole case.



3. Issue 2: Qualified immunity and "clearly established" law

Controlling law.

     • Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223 (2009) [F]: courts may decide either prong first.
     • Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S. 731 (2011) [F]: existing precedent must place the question
       "beyond debate."
     • District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48 (2018) [F]: the rule must be dictated by "controlling
       authority" or "a robust 'consensus of cases of persuasive authority.'"
     • Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002) [W]: a general rule can give fair warning in an
       obvious case.
     • Drummond ex rel. Drummond v. City of Anaheim, 343 F.3d 1052 (9th Cir. 2003) [F]: quoting
       earlier authority, "in the absence of binding precedent, a court should look to whatever



                                  Privileged research memo — Page 6 of 30
       decisional law is available." Only that fragment was matched; read the full passage before
       quoting more.
     • Due-process baseline:
     • In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955) [W]: "[N]o man can be a judge in his own case"; "A
       fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process."
     • Withrow v. Larkin, 421 U.S. 35, 46–47 (1975) [W]: combining investigative and adjudicative
       functions does not by itself violate due process. A challenger "must overcome a presumption of
       honesty and integrity in those serving as adjudicators" [F].
     • Caperton, 556 U.S. 868, 883–84 (2009) [W]: the test is the "probability of actual bias" [F].

What California decisions do in federal court.

     • Cal. DUI Lawyers Ass'n v. DMV (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 517 [W, yellow: distinguished by
       Kazelka v. DMV].
     • The court held (paraphrase) that combining the roles of advocate and adjudicator in a single
       DMV hearing officer violates due process under the Fourteenth Amendment and Cal. Const. art.
       I, § 7 [Justia text; re-read before quoting].
     • It held Veh. Code § 14112(b) unconstitutional to that extent.
     • It directed a permanent injunction against DMV "having its APS hearing officers function as
       advocates for the position of the DMV in addition to being finders of fact" [Justia].
     • It is therefore a federal-law holding, not only a state-law one.
     • A state intermediate appellate court's reading of the Fourteenth Amendment does not bind a
       federal court. It is persuasive at most.
     • The later cases point different ways:
     • Knudsen v. DMV (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 186, 207 [W]: "The analysis will necessarily depend
       on the unique facts of each hearing."
     • Clarke v. Gordon (2024) 104 Cal.App.5th 1267, 1277 [W]: found advocacy where the hearing
       officer "marshalled, identified, and offered into evidence the DMV's exhibits."
     • Romane v. DMV (2025) 110 Cal.App.5th 1002 (4th Dist., Div. 1, D083569, filed Apr. 23,
       2025) [WK]. Review granted Aug. 13, 2025 (S291093). A hearing officer who merely
       introduces law-enforcement documents is "collecting and developing evidence, not advocating"
       (~pp. 1016–19). It distinguished Clarke, 104 Cal.App.5th 1267. Because review was granted, it
       may be cited only for persuasive value or to show a conflict (Cal. Rules of Court, rule
       8.1115(e)).
     • Kazelka v. DMV (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 1239 (1st Dist., Div. 3, A163664) [WK, no flag; O,
       slip opinion]. It held the dual-role claim forfeited (p. 1254). The task notes said the merits were
       not decided, but the slip opinion goes on: "even if it were not forfeited, his due process claim
       fails on the merits." It found no advocacy where the officer had only served a records subpoena.
       Chi cites Kazelka at pp. 1255–1256 as "finding no due process violation." So Kazelka is an
       alternative merits holding against the dual-role claim, not only a forfeiture case.
     • Chi v. DMV (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 473 (1st Dist., Div. 5, A172237, filed Mar. 24, 2026,
       modified Apr. 7, 2026) [WK]. Review granted May 13, 2026 (S296201), deferred pending
       Romane. Citable only for persuasive value or to show the conflict. Holding (p. 483): the hearing
       officer "did not function as a prosecutor or an advocate"; the combined role "is inquisitorial,



                                  Privileged research memo — Page 7 of 30
       rather than adversarial, and does not, by itself, offend due process." At ~p. 484 it says the case
       law "has taken a wrong turn." It rejects the Clarke approach and requires an unacceptable risk of
       actual bias [O, slip opinion].
     • Bottom line: the CDLA/Clarke line is now in open conflict with Romane, Kazelka, and Chi, and
       the California Supreme Court has taken up the issue. That strengthens Candelaria's qualified-
       immunity defense and weakens any argument that the law was clearly established on Jan. 24,
       2025. A federal court is unlikely to find a rule "beyond debate" while the state's highest court is
       still reviewing it.

How it cuts.

     • For Brendan:
     • The general rule (a neutral decisionmaker; no one judges their own case) is as old as Murchison.
     • By Jan. 2025, the DMV and its Director had been subject for nearly three years to a permanent
       injunction against exactly this dual role.
     • A reasonable hearing officer would know that "marshal, offer, admit, decide" was the conduct
       Clarke condemned.
     • Hope allows obviousness without a factually identical case.
     • Against Brendan:
     • There is no Supreme Court or Ninth Circuit case holding that an APS hearing officer's dual role
       violates due process. Withrow points the other way at a general level.
     • Knudsen makes the question fact-specific. Romane, Kazelka, and Chi went the other way, and
       two of them are under Supreme Court review. Courts routinely grant QI in that situation.
       Realistically, expect to lose Count I damages on QI unless the Hope obviousness argument
       carries.
     • QI would not apply to Counts III–IV (official capacity, prospective relief). QI is a defense to
       damages only [U for a specific cite].

Strategy.

    1. Plead specific facts tracking Clarke's three acts: marshaling and offering, ruling on her own
       exhibits, and the lack of any separate DMV representative. Cite any DMV form or policy
       language (e.g., DS 2304, if its text is confirmed) as a policy admission [U].
    2. Argue the "obvious case" route (Hope, Murchison), plus Drummond's look-to-available-
       decisional-law rule. Add the 2022 injunction as notice to the agency. (CDLA named the
       Director; Candelaria's personal notice is a fact question.)
    3. Concede candidly that QI is contested, so that losing it does not cost credibility on Counts III–
       IV.

3A. Recommended focus: the Gov. Code § 11425.30(b)–(c) theory and prospective
relief

What the statutes say [O, leginfo].

     • Veh. Code § 14112(a): "All matters in a hearing not covered by this chapter shall be governed,
       as far as applicable, by Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 11500) ... of the Government
       Code."
     • Veh. Code § 14112(b): "Subdivision (a) of Section 11425.30 of the Government Code does not
       apply to a proceeding for issuance, denial, revocation, or suspension of a driver's license
       pursuant to this division."
     • Gov. Code § 11425.30(b) lets a person serve as presiding officer at successive stages, or after
       acting only as a decisionmaker or advisor on a preliminary determination, "[n]otwithstanding
       subdivision (a)." Subd. (c) applies the same separation-of-functions rules to "the agency head or
       other person or body to which the power to hear or decide in the proceeding is delegated."

The theory. § 14112(b) disables only subdivision (a). It does not say subdivisions (b) and (c) are
inapplicable. So the argument is that the rest of § 11425.30, through § 14112(a), still governs DMV
hearings "as far as applicable." Subdivision (b) defines the narrow conditions under which one person
may wear more than one hat. Subdivision (c) extends the separation-of-functions rules to whoever has
delegated power to decide. A hearing officer who marshaled and offered the DMV's case falls outside
the (b) safe harbors.

Candor. This is an inference from statutory structure, not a holding. Subdivisions (b) and (c) are
written as exceptions to, and extensions of, subdivision (a). A court may read § 14112(b) as taking the
whole framework out of license hearings. No case was found adopting or rejecting this reading [U]. It
is also a state-law theory, and a federal court cannot enjoin state officials on the basis of state law
(Pennhurst State Sch. & Hosp. v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 89 (1984) [U, not pulled]). Use it to show what
process the State itself requires (the Mathews "value of additional safeguards" factor), and to frame the
federal due-process claim. Do not plead it as a stand-alone claim for an injunction.

Why focus here.

    1. It does not depend on Clarke or on CDLA's federal holding, both now in conflict and under
       review.
    2. Prospective relief against Gordon (Counts III–IV) is not subject to qualified or absolute
       immunity.
    3. The SR-22 condition (§ 13353.4(b)) and the record entry are the continuing effects that keep
       Counts III–IV live.

3B.4 Background: how the split developed

    1. CDLA (2d Dist., Div. 4, 2022): the DMV's practice of having one hearing officer act as both the
       DMV's advocate and the adjudicator violates due process under the Fourteenth Amendment and
       Cal. Const. art. I, § 7 (pp. 532–533). The court affirmed a permanent injunction [read on Justia].
    2. Knudsen v. DMV (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 186: the test is the function performed, not the title.
       "The analysis will necessarily depend on the unique facts of each hearing" (p. 207) [W]. It found
       a violation and treated it as structural error.
    3. Clarke v. Gordon (2024) 104 Cal.App.5th 1267 (4th Dist., Div. 3): the officer "marshalled,
       identified, and offered into evidence the DMV's exhibits" (p. 1277) [W]. The court held that was
       advocacy, structural error, and reversed with directions to grant the writ.
    4. Kazelka v. DMV (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 1239 (1st Dist., Div. 3): the claim was forfeited (p.
       1254). Alternative merits holding: "even if it were not forfeited, his due process claim fails on
       the merits." Serving a records subpoena was not advocacy (pp. 1255–56) [WK + O].
    5. Romane (2025) 110 Cal.App.5th 1002 (4th Dist., Div. 1): the officer introduced three
       documents (DS-367, arrest report, driving record), admitted the driver's body-camera video, and
       let counsel argue. That was "collecting and developing evidence, not advocating" (about pp.
       1016–19) [WK]. The court distinguished Clarke and reversed. Review granted.
    6. Chi (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 473 (1st Dist., Div. 5): the officer "did not function as a prosecutor
       or an advocate." The combined role "is inquisitorial, rather than adversarial" (p. 483). The
       Clarke line "has taken a wrong turn" (about p. 484) [WK]. Review granted and held.

The result is an intra-district and inter-district conflict. The 4th District has Clarke (Div. 3) against
Romane (Div. 1). The 2d District's CDLA has its functional reading in Knudsen. The 1st District has
Kazelka and Chi against the Clarke line.




                                  Privileged research memo — Page 12 of 30
How the Court framed the question [A]. The issue is not limited to "what counts as advocacy." It
asks when the officer's "relationship with the DMV" creates an "unacceptable risk of bias." That is
the structural-bias language of Withrow/Caperton-type analysis. It opens the door to arguments about
employment, supervision, and lack of independence, not just conduct at the hearing. That framing fits
Brendan's Cleavinger-independence theory (§ 2) and the § 11425.30(b)–(c) structure argument (§ 3A).
It also means the Court could adopt a test that neither side of the current split uses.

3B.5 Analysis for Nforbi v. Candelaria, N.D. Cal. No. 3:26-cv-08272-RFL

(a) Qualified immunity and "clearly established" law (Count I damages) [A].

     • Clearly established law is judged as of the conduct (Jan.–Feb. 2025). At that time the published
       cases were CDLA, Knudsen, and Clarke, all for the driver, and Kazelka (Feb. 2025) went the
       other way. Romane (Apr. 2025) and Chi (2026) came later. Strictly, they cannot make the law
       less clear as of January 2025. Expect Defendants to argue that they show the question was
       always debatable, and courts often accept that framing.
     • The sources in Brendan's favor are state intermediate decisions. Ninth Circuit QI analysis looks
       first to Supreme Court and Ninth Circuit authority, and then to a "robust consensus" of
       persuasive authority (Wesby) [F]. A state appellate split that the state high court has taken up is
       close to the opposite of a consensus. Realistic outcome: QI on Count I damages is likely
       unless the Hope/Murchison obviousness argument carries.
     • Even if Romane is decided for the driver, that would not retroactively clearly establish the
       law for January 2025 conduct [A]. At most, it would confirm that CDLA/Clarke stated the law
       correctly. Courts are divided on whether that helps, and it rarely defeats QI when the decision
       came after the conduct.
     • QI is no defense to Counts III–IV (official-capacity prospective relief). It also does not answer
       absolute immunity (§ 2), which is a separate defense.

(b) Disclose the pending review, or seek a stay? [A]

     • Disclose: yes. Any brief to Judge Lin that relies on CDLA/Clarke should say: "but see Romane
       (review granted Aug. 13, 2025, S291093); Chi (review granted May 13, 2026, S296201)."
       Defendants will raise it anyway. Raising it first protects credibility and avoids the candor
       problem flagged in § 10, rows 21 and 26.
     • Do not move for a stay now. A federal court's discretionary stay pending another proceeding
       (Landis v. North American Co., 299 U.S. 248 (1936); Lockyer v. Mirant, 398 F.3d 1098 (9th Cir.
       2005)) [GK, cites not pulled this session] weighs hardship and prejudice. Defendants' response
       to the FAC is due Oct. 27, 2026. The immunity questions (absolute immunity, QI) do not turn
       on Romane. A stay would delay Counts III–IV, which are Brendan's strongest claims. If
       Defendants ask for a stay, the response is that the immunity issues and the § 11425.30 theory
       are independent of Romane. Offer, as an alternative, a joint notice to the court when Romane is
       decided.

     • Option: after Defendants respond, file a short notice of pending authority, or include a footnote
       in the opposition. Either one flags Romane and promises a notice of supplemental authority
       when it is decided [A].

(c) Pullman abstention and certification risk [A].

     • Pullman requires (1) a sensitive area of social policy, (2) a federal constitutional question that a
       definitive state-law ruling could avoid, and (3) an uncertain state-law question [GK]. Count I is

4. Issue 3: Heck / Edwards and preclusion from the unreviewed DMV decision

Heck and Edwards.

    • Heck v. Humphrey, 512 U.S. 477 (1994) [F]: a § 1983 damages claim is barred if judgment for
      the plaintiff would "necessarily imply the invalidity" of a conviction or sentence that has not
      been set aside.
    • Edwards v. Balisok, 520 U.S. 641 (1997) [F]: extended Heck to a prison-discipline procedural
      claim based on "deceit and bias" of the hearing officer.



                                 Privileged research memo — Page 15 of 30
     • Nonnette v. Small, 316 F.3d 872 (9th Cir. 2002) [F]: Heck does not bar a § 1983 claim where
       habeas was unavailable because the challenged custody had been fully served.
     • Lyall v. City of Los Angeles, 807 F.3d 1178 (9th Cir. 2015) [WK, yellow: "Overruling
       Recognized by" Freeman v. Mata, 2023 WL 4291850 (C.D. Cal.) and Sherman v. Bryant (Cal.
       Ct. App. 2020); O, slip opinion read]. The Nonnette exception is narrow: it "affects only former
       prisoners challenging loss of good-time credits, revocation of parole or similar matters" (p.
       1192). A plaintiff who did not timely pursue available relief is like the plaintiff in Guerrero v.
       Gates, 442 F.3d 697 (9th Cir. 2006), and is barred (pp. 1191–92). "[T]imely pursuit of available
       habeas relief" is "an important prerequisite" for escaping Heck. What was overruled was not
       read, in either Freeman or Sherman [U]. Check before citing.
     • Application: if Heck reaches the APS decision at all, Lyall hurts. Brendan did not file a timely §
       13559 petition, and the late Sacramento petition was abandoned. That looks like Guerrero (no
       timely pursuit), not Nonnette. The best answer is still that Heck requires a "conviction or
       sentence" and there was none.

Application to a civil license suspension: unsettled [U]. A free-source search (CourtListener) found
nothing either way [O, negative search only]. A targeted Westlaw search found no California or
federal authority applying Heck to an administrative license suspension where there was no
conviction [WK]. The only hits were Rasmussen and an off-point Second Circuit case, Green v.
Jackson, 36 F. App'x 663. Rasmussen (C.D. Cal. 2008) applied Heck only because the plaintiff had
pleaded nolo contendere to § 23152(a), and the suspension stemmed from that conviction. Brendan had
no conviction (the DA declined to file), so Rasmussen is distinguishable, and on its own reasoning it
supports him. Arguments:

     • For Brendan: there is no "conviction or sentence," the DA declined to file, and habeas was
       never available. Heck's logic is tied to the custody and habeas channel.
     • Against Brendan: Edwards shows that a claim of a biased adjudicator can imply the invalidity of
       the result. Some courts extend Heck-type reasoning to other unreversed adjudications.

Preclusion (the bigger risk).

     • Univ. of Tenn. v. Elliott, 478 U.S. 788 (1986) [F]: when a state agency acts "in a judicial
       capacity," federal courts give its factfinding the "same preclusive effect" it would have in state
       court. This applies to § 1983 claims.
     • Kremer v. Chemical Constr. Corp., 456 U.S. 461 (1982) [F, existence only]: preclusion of state-
       court judgments.
     • Miller v. County of Santa Cruz, 39 F.3d 1030 (9th Cir. 1994) [F] and White v. City of Pasadena,
       671 F.3d 918 (9th Cir. 2012) [F]: apply Elliott to unreviewed California administrative findings.
       The analysis uses United States v. Utah Constr. & Mining Co., 384 U.S. 394, 422 (1966) (cited
       in White) [F as cited].
     • Johnson v. City of Loma Linda (2000) 24 Cal.4th 61 [F]: unless a party challenges the agency's
       adverse findings "by means of a mandate action in superior court, those findings are binding in
       later civil actions." The dissent refers to the Westlake "requirement that a party exhaust judicial
       remedies" [F].



                                  Privileged research memo — Page 16 of 30
     • People v. Sims (1982) 32 Cal.3d 468 [F, existence and topic only]: collateral estoppel from
       administrative decisions.
     • Veh. Code § 13559(a) [F, leginfo]: allows a petition for review "within 30 days of the issuance
       of the notice of determination." Review is on the record. Subdivision (b) bars collateral estoppel
       only in a later criminal prosecution; it says nothing about civil suits.
     • Facts:
     • The decision issued Feb. 11, 2025, so the 30-day window closed around mid-March 2025.
     • The Sacramento petition (26WM000135) was filed around May 2026, outside that window. It
       was abandoned after the fee waiver was denied and the fee deadline lapsed (around July 27,
       2026, per matter records).
     • Pull the docket to see whether it was dismissed and whether the dismissal was with or without
       prejudice.
     • Patsy v. Board of Regents, 457 U.S. 496 (1982) [W] (no exhaustion requirement for § 1983)
       answers administrative exhaustion. It does not answer preclusion by unreviewed findings. ¶117
       conflates the two.

How it cuts.

     • The DMV found that the officer had reasonable cause, that there was an arrest, and that there
       was a refusal. If those findings are binding, Count II ("fair hearing") and the damages-causation
       theory, which depends on showing the result was wrong, weaken sharply.
     • Brendan's counter is that preclusion applies only to agencies acting "in a judicial capacity" with
       an adequate opportunity to litigate (Elliott; Utah Construction). A tribunal whose adjudicator
       also served as advocate arguably fails that test. That is the very defect CDLA found
       unconstitutional.
     • Issue preclusion also should not reach the procedural question of whether the tribunal was
       neutral, because the DMV never decided it.

Strategy.

    1. Frame Count I as a structural claim about the tribunal, not a challenge to the correctness of the
       findings.
    2. Argue that the hearing lacked the "judicial capacity" and fairness preconditions for preclusion.
    3. Do not argue that the suspension findings were wrong as a basis for damages unless preclusion
       is defeated.
    4. Correct ¶117. Patsy is about exhaustion; address Elliott and Johnson directly.
    5. Disclose the Sacramento petition accurately if it is raised. Do not let the defense frame it as
       concealed.

5. Issue 4: Procedural due process adequacy (Mathews, Mackey, Parratt/Zinermon)

Controlling law.

     • Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976) [W; pin 344–45 is loosely supportive, so recheck
       343–45]: the three-factor balance (private interest, risk of error and value of added safeguards,
       government interest).
     • Bell v. Burson, 402 U.S. 535, 539 (1971) [W]: a driver's license is a protected property interest.
     • Mackey v. Montrym, 443 U.S. 1, 10–11 (1979) [W]: upheld summary suspension for refusal
       before a hearing, applying Mathews.
     • Dixon v. Love, 431 U.S. 105 (1977) [F]: upheld an initial summary decision based on official
       records, followed by a full hearing.
     • Loudermill, 470 U.S. 532, 541 (1985) [W]: what process is due is a federal question. Property
       "cannot be defined by the procedures provided for its deprivation."
     • Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S. 254, 271 (1970) [W]: "the decision maker should state the reasons
       for his determination and indicate the evidence he relied on." This was said in the welfare-
       termination context; its application to APS hearings is argued by analogy.
     • Parratt v. Taylor, 451 U.S. 527 (1981) and Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517 (1984) [F]: for
       "random and unauthorized" deprivations, an adequate state post-deprivation remedy satisfies
       due process.
     • Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S. 113 (1990) [F]: Parratt does not apply where the deprivation
       occurs at a "specific, predictable point" in an established procedure, and the state could have
       provided pre-deprivation safeguards.

How it cuts.

     • Mackey and Dixon defeat any claim that summary suspension without a pre-suspension hearing
       is unconstitutional. Do not plead that.
     • A Parratt/Zinermon dilemma:
     • If Candelaria's dual role followed DMV policy (the Driver Safety Manual or DMV forms),
       Zinermon applies, Parratt does not, and the § 13559 writ does not cure the defect. That reading
       helps Counts I and III–IV.
     • If DMV had abolished the dual role after the 2022 injunction and Candelaria departed from the
       new policy, Defendants will call her conduct "random and unauthorized" and argue that § 13559
       and § 1094.5 review was adequate (Parratt/Hudson). That helps them on Count I. It also
       undercuts the ongoing-policy premise of Counts III–IV.
     • Discovery priority: DMV's post-CDLA written policies and Driver Safety Manual revisions in
       effect on Jan. 24, 2025.
     • Count II ("no reasoned engagement"; "no evidence was presented to the contrary") risks being
       treated as a claim that the decision was erroneous, which is a state-law writ issue, rather than as
       a constitutional procedure defect. Goldberg's statement-of-reasons rule is the best federal hook.
       Plead that the decision ignored record evidence that was actually submitted (the § 851.6
       certificate and the dashcam), so that the statement of reasons was illusory.

6. Issue 5: Mercer superseded by Veh. Code § 40300.5(e); reframing to no probable
cause

     • Mercer v. DMV (1991) 53 Cal.3d 753, 760–61 [W, yellow: "Superseded by Statute as Stated in"
       Troppman v. Gourley (2005) and Dyer v. DMV (2008)]. It held that, under the statutes then in
       force, a warrantless DUI arrest required the misdemeanor to be committed in the officer's
       presence, meaning observed volitional movement.
     • Veh. Code § 40300.5 [F, leginfo; subd. (e) added by Stats. 1996, ch. 1078]: permits a
       warrantless DUI arrest on reasonable cause when, among other conditions, "(e) The person may
       destroy or conceal evidence of the crime unless immediately arrested."
     • Troppman v. Valverde (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1121 [WK, yellow flag: distinguished by Elmore v.
       Gordon (Cal. Ct. App., 2d Dist., Dec. 30, 2021); footnotes read on screen and matched to the
       SCOCAL slip text]:
     • Holding: proof of driving immediately before the arrest is not required for a § 13353 refusal
       suspension. Troppman abrogated Weber v. Orr, Medina, and Jackson on that point [WK]. (Full
       citations for those three were not recorded.)
     • Footnote 11 (p. 1136): Mercer held the lawful-arrest requirement unmet because the law then
       required the officer to observe volitional movement. "Thereafter, the Legislature took such
       action, allowing warrantless arrest for a misdemeanor driving-while-under-the-influence
