 2                                            INTRODUCTION
 3         Petitioner asserts that Respondent California Highway Patrol (“CHP”) failed to perform the

 4   following purported mandatory duties: first, to certify Petitioner’s submitted Form I-918,

 5   Supplement B pursuant to California Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (b); second, to

 6   provide a written explanation of the specific reasons for denying to certify Petitioner’s submitted

 7   Form I-918, Supplement B pursuant to California Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (g); and

 8   third, to apply a rebuttable presumption of helpfulness to Petitioner’s certification submission

 9   pursuant to California Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (h). Based on his conclusory

10   allegations, Petitioner seeks a writ of ordinary mandate pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure

11   section 1085, compelling CHP either to certify Petitioner’s Form I-918, Supplement B, or to issue

12   a new written decision with further explanation for why Petitioner’s certification request was

13   denied.

14         However, Petitioner’s cause of action for failure to certify his Form I-918, Supplement B is

15   wholly without merit because no such ministerial duty exists to issue such a certification under

16   the allegations pled in the petition. Specifically, the Penal Code contemplates that the entity

17   certifying the submitted Form I-918 will exercise its discretion in determining whether the

18   requestor was the victim of a qualifying criminal activity, and, subject to statutory presumptions,

19   whether the requestor was helpful to the investigation or prosecution of that criminal activity.

20   Accordingly, Petitioner cannot contend that CHP has failed to perform a duty mandated by law.

21         Moreover, even assuming a ministerial duty exists, the Petition alleges no factual basis for

22   such a writ to issue. Petitioner fails to allege facts sufficient to support any of his four causes of

23   action to remedy CHP’s supposed failure to perform its mandatory duty to apply a presumption of

24   helpfulness to Petitioner and certify his Form I-918, Supplement B, or to provide an explanation

25   in the form demanded by Petitioner. Nor has Petitioner alleged any facts that, if true, would

26   demonstrate that CHP has failed to comply with state law governing the certification of such

27   immigration visa forms. Accordingly, there are no grounds alleged in the Petition to order CHP

28   to exercise its statutory authority to certify Petitioner’s Form I-918, Supplement B or to revise its
                                                        6
                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   written denial. Finally, for the same reasons that Petitioner is not entitled to a writ of ordinary

 2   mandate, he is also not entitled to declaratory relief.

 3         No additional facts that Petitioner could allege would alter this conclusion. CHP therefore

 4   respectfully requests that the Court sustain this demurrer without leave to amend.

 5                                             BACKGROUND
 6   I.    LEGAL BACKGROUND
 7         Federal immigration law allows a victim of a one or more of several enumerated crimes in

 8   the United States to apply for a U Visa, or U nonimmigrant status, when the victim has suffered

 9   “substantial physical or mental abuse” as a result, possesses information concerning the crime,

10   and has been helpful to the investigation or prosecution of the crime. (8 U.S.C. §

11   1101(a)(15)(U)(i) (2012).) In 2000, Congress created the U Visa as part of the reauthorization of

12   the Violence Against Women Act to provide immigrant crime victims an avenue to obtain lawful

13   immigration status and thus encourage cooperation with law enforcement by undocumented

14   victims of crime. (Senate Rules Committee Report, 2015 California Senate Bill No. 674,

15   California 2015-2016 Regular Session (May 31, 2015); see also Danielle Kalil, Certified

16   Disaster: A Failure at the Intersection of the U Visa and the Child Welfare System, 35 Geo.

17   Immigr. L.J. 513, 522 (2021) [“The U visa was meant to serve as an investigative tool to aid law

18   enforcement.”].) As articulated by a co-sponsor of the Act’s reauthorization, one of the primary

19   objectives was to “remov[e] obstacles inadvertently interposed by our immigration laws that

20   many [sic] hinder or prevent battered immigrants from fleeing domestic violence safely and

21   prosecuting their abusers by allowing an abusive citizen or lawful permanent resident to

22   blackmail the abused spouse through threats related to the abused spouse’s immigration status.”

23   (146 Cong. Rec. S10, 192 (daily ed. Oct. 11, 2000) (statement of Sen. Hatch).)

24         Relevantly, as part of his or her application, a U Visa applicant must file a Form I-918,

25   Supplement B, U Nonimmigrant Status Certification (“Form B”) signed by a qualifying law

26   enforcement agency. (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(2)(i) (2007).) The certification must state that the

27   applicant “has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be helpful” in the investigation or

28   prosecution of criminal activity. (8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(1).) Those qualified to certify Form B
                                                        7
                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   include state or local law enforcement officials. (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(3).) Under federal law, the

 2   denial to certify a Form B is a “discretionary act and not subject to judicial review.” (Catholic

 3   Charities CYO v. Chertoff (N.D. Cal. 2008) 622 F. Supp. 2d 865, 887 [“[T]he statute does not

 4   contain any mandatory language with regard to actions by law enforcement officials.

 5   Specifically, there is no statutory directive to law enforcement officials to issue [law enforcement

 6   certifications].”].) As a result, some law enforcement agencies declined to issue Form B

 7   certifications altogether. (See Leslye E. Orloff et. al., Mandatory U-Visa Certification

 8   Unnecessarily Undermines the Purpose of the Violence Against Women Act’s Immigration

 9   Protections and Its “Any Credible Evidence” Rules-A Call for Consistency, 11 Geo. J. Gender &

10   L. 619, 637 (2010) [describing various police departments nationwide that stopped issuing Form

11   B certifications].)

12         In 2015, the California Legislature, seeking to impose certain procedural requirements on

13   qualifying law enforcement agencies upon receipt of a Form B, added Section 679.10 to the

14   California Penal Code. (Legislative Counsel’s Digest, 2015 California Senate Bill No. 674,

15   California 2015-2016 Regular Session (February 27, 2015).) Specifically, the Penal Code now

16   requires that upon request, a certifying entity “shall fully complete and sign the Form I-918

17   Supplement B certification” and “shall certify victim helpfulness . . . when the victim was a

18   victim of a qualifying criminal activity and has been helpful, is being helpful or is likely to be

19   helpful in the detection or investigation or prosecution of that qualifying criminal activity.” (Pen.

20   Code, §§ 679.10, subds (g)(1) & (i)(1). The Penal Code also now requires a Form B certification

21   denial to be accompanied with a written explanation for the denial, which “shall include specific

22   details of any reasonable requests for cooperation and a detailed description of how the victim

23   refused to cooperate.” (Id. § 679.10, subd. (i)(2).) However, while the Penal Code now requires

24   that certifying entities process Form B certifications upon receipt, the Penal Code does not require

25   the certifying entity to find that the requestor is a victim of a qualifying criminal activity, or to

26   find that the requestor has been helpful in the absence of supporting facts or the presence of an

27   unrebutted presumption. In other words, certifying entities retain discretion to deny a Form B

28

                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   certification request upon determination that the applicant does not meet the requirements for

 2   issuance of the certification.

 3                                              ARGUMENT
 4   I.    LEGAL STANDARD
 5         “Rules governing demurrers to civil complaints apply to mandamus actions.” (Pinto Lake

 6   MHP LLC v. County of Santa Cruz (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 1006, 1012; see Code Civ. Proc.,

 7   § 1109.) A petition or complaint must contain “a statement of the facts constituting the cause of

 8   action, in ordinary and concise language.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (a)(1).) A petitioner

 9   or plaintiff must “set forth the essential facts of his case with reasonable precision and with

10   particularity sufficient to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source and extent of his cause of

11   action.” (Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Ass’n, Inc. v. Truck Ins. Exchange (2005) 132

12   Cal.App.4th 1076, 1099 [citation omitted].) Failure to plead sufficient facts subjects the petition

13   or complaint to demurrer for failure to state facts constituting a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc.,

14   § 430.10, subd. (e); Berger v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 989,

15   1006.) “[A]llegations constituting legal conclusions are not provisionally admitted for the

16   purposes of a demurrer.” (Kenneth Mebane Ranches v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th

17   276, 291.)

18   II.   A WRIT SHOULD NOT ISSUE AGAINST RESPONDENT BECAUSE THE PETITION FAILS
           TO STATE FACTS CONSTITUTING ANY CAUSE OF ACTION
19

20         “To obtain writ relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085, the petitioner must show

21   [1] there is no other plain, speedy, and adequate remedy; [2] the respondent has a clear, present,

22   and ministerial duty to act in a particular way; and [3] the petitioner has a clear, present and

23   beneficial right to performance of that duty.” (County of San Diego v. State of California (2008)

24   164 Cal.App.4th 580, 593.)

25         A.     Respondent Has No Ministerial Duty to Certify Petitioner’s Form B
26         Petitioner’s first cause of action seeks a writ of mandate under Section 1085 for the CHP’s

27   alleged failure to perform its “mandatory duty” under Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (b)

28   to certify his request for Form B certification. (Pet., ¶ 73.) However, Petitioner alleges no facts
                                                       11
                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   to show that Respondent has a ministerial duty to interpret Section 679.10’s definitions of

 2   qualifying criminal activity or helpfulness in a manner favorable to Petitioner’s interests, or that

 3   any other grounds for writ relief are present.

 4         A ministerial duty is one “that a public officer is required to perform in a prescribed manner

 5   in obedience to the mandate of legal authority and without regard to his own judgment or opinion

 6   concerning such act’s propriety or impropriety, when a given state of facts exists.” (County of Los

 7   Angeles v. City of Los Angeles (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 643, 653-654.) Where a statute leaves

 8   room for discretion, a challenger must show the official acted arbitrarily, beyond the bounds of

 9   reason, or in derogation of the applicable legal standards. (Cal. Correctional Supervisors Org.,

10   Inc. v. Dept. of Corrections (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 824, 827.)

11         Whether an entity’s action was ministerial or quasi-legislative is a question of statutory

12   interpretation. (County of Los Angeles v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 214 Cal.App.4th at p. 653.)

13   Courts “examine the language, function and apparent purpose of the statute.” (Weinstein v.

14   County of Los Angeles (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 944, 965 [citations and quotation marks omitted].)

15   “In order to construe a statute as imposing a mandatory duty, the mandatory nature of the duty

16   must be phrased in explicit and forceful language.” (Collins v. Thurmond (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th

17   879, 914.)

18         Contrary to Petitioner’s assertions, Respondent does not have a ministerial duty to certify a

19   Form B request. To be sure, Section 679.10 requires that upon request, a certifying entity “shall

20   fully complete and sign the Form I-918 Supplement B certification.” (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd.

21   (i)(1).) Section 679.10 also requires that upon request, a certifying entity “shall certify victim

22   helpfulness . . . when the victim was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity and has been

23   helpful, is being helpful or is likely to be helpful in the detection or investigation or prosecution

24   of that qualifying criminal activity.” (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (g)(1).) But nowhere in Section

25   679.10 is there a requirement that certifying entities invariably find that the requestor is, in fact, a

26   victim of a qualifying criminal activity, as those terms are defined in the statute, or to find that the

27   requestor has been helpful. In fact, Section 679.10 contemplates that certifying entities retain

28   discretion to find otherwise. The statute provides, in relevant part:
                                                        12
                                                             Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1          If a certifying entity does not certify a Form I-918 Supplement B certification, they
            shall provide a written explanation for the denial of the Form I-918 Supplement B
 2          certification.

 3          (§ 679.10, subd. (i)(2) (emphasis added).)

 4          Indeed, as Petitioner himself acknowledges, whether “the underlying conduct constitutes

 5   qualifying criminal activity” “is committed in the first instance to the certifying entity.” (Pet., ¶

 6   94.)
            B.    Petitioner Has No Clear, Present and Beneficial Right to the Performance
 7                of a Ministerial Duty
 8          Even if a ministerial duty to apply Section 679.10 in Petitioner’s favor existed, Petitioner
 9   has no “clear, present and beneficial right to the performance of that duty.” (County of San Diego
10   v. State of California, supra, 164 Cal.App.4th at p. 593.) Petitioner’s factual allegations do not
11   support his bare legal conclusions that he was the victim of “false imprisonment, unlawful
12   criminal restraint, felonious assault, obstruction of justice, perjury.” See Pet., ¶ 82.
13          Petitioner’s primary factual basis for alleging that he was the victim of numerous crimes,
14   including being confined “on the basis of [a] fabricated arrest report” (Pet., ¶ 41), is his own
15   transcription of CHP’s dashcam recording of the November 1, 2024 incident (see CHP Dashcam
16   Recording Transcript, Ex. 5 to Petition), which he submitted to CHP along with his Form B
17   certification request. (Pet., ¶¶ 44-48; see also Petitioner’s Formal Certification Request, Ex. 2 to
18   Petition; Petitioner’s Supporting Legal Analysis and Evidentiary Summary, Ex. 3 to Petition.)
19   Petitioner alleges that this transcription proves CHP officers subjected him to “false
20   imprisonment, felonious assault, and obstruction of justice on November 1, 2024,” and that
21   “CHP’s own Detention Certificate confirms the arrest lacked justification.” (See Pet., ¶¶ 48-49;
22   see also Ex. 3 to Petition, at 8.) In short, Petitioner alleges that there exist multiple contradictions
23   between the dashcam recording (as transcribed by Petitioner) and the CHP officers’ sworn arrest
24   report, showing that his arrest was unjustified and so he was the victim of a qualifying criminal
25   activity. (Pet., ¶¶ 43-48.)
26          But as pled, at least two of the “contradictions” identified by Petitioner as “smoking gun[s]”
27   in CHP’s Arrest-Investigation Report, see Ex. 6 to Petition, are not contradictions at all. (See
28

 1   Pet., ¶ 43.) Indeed, as pled, these allegations provide probable cause for the CHP officers to have

 2   arrested Petitioner. First, Petitioner alleges that a CHP officer provided a sworn statement that he

 3   “could smell the distinct odor of an alcohol beverage emitting from within the vehicle” and “from

 4   Newanforbi’s breath and person,” but challenges this statement by pointing to the dashcam

 5   recording, in which “[n]either officer verbalizes detection of any odor of alcohol.” (Pet., ¶ 44.)

 7   not exist. Second, Petitioner asserts that the CHP Arrest-Investigation Report misleadingly

 8   implied that an alcoholic beverage was present in the vehicle but goes on to allege that “[a]n

 9   empty Mike’s Hard Lemonade can was observed.” (Pet., ¶ 47.)

10         These allegations, taken together, support a finding that the CHP officers had probable

11   cause to arrest Petitioner for driving under the influence, and that they did not subject Petitioner

12   to “false imprisonment.” (See Pen. Code, § 236 [“False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of

13   the personal liberty of another.”].) “Probable cause exists when the facts known to the arresting

14   officer would persuade someone of ‘reasonable caution’ that the person to be arrested has

15   committed a crime.” (People v. Thompson (2006) 38 Cal. 4th 811, 818.) In Thompson, the

16   California Supreme Court held that probable cause to arrest the defendant for a DUI existed

17   where the officer did not see the defendant drinking, but did see him have difficulty walking, toss

18   an empty vodka bottle out of his car, and pass out again in the vehicle. Id. at p. 819. Here, based

19   on Petitioner’s own allegations, the officers witnessed Petitioner asleep in his vehicle on the side

20   of the road, an empty alcohol bottle in Petitioner’s vehicle, smelled the “strong distinct odor of an

21   alcoholic beverage coming from Newanforbi’s breath and person,” and demonstrated a series of

22   Field Sobriety Tests to Newanforbi, “which he did not perform as explained and demonstrated.”

23   (Pet., ¶¶ 34-35, 43-48; CHP Arrest-Investigation Report, Ex. 6 to Petition.) Petitioner’s own

24   transcription of CHP’s dashcam recording supports the officers’ arrest-investigation report. (See

25   Ex. 5 to Petition.) In Petitioner’s transcription, the officers identify Mike’s Hard Lemonade as

26   “source alcohol” and tell Petitioner that “we’ve got you sleeping right here.” (See Ex. 5 to

27   Petition, at 4, 6.) At one point, Petitioner declined to perform one of the sobriety tests, saying that

28   he did not think he could perform it. (See id. at 9.)

           C.    Respondent Has No Ministerial Duty to Provide a Written Explanation for
12               Its Certification Denial In the Form Demanded By Petitioner

13         Petitioner’s second cause of action seeks a petition for writ of mandate under Section 1085

14   for the CHP’s alleged failure to perform its “ministerial” duty to provide a reason for its

15   certification denial. (Pet., ¶¶ 79-84.) However, Petitioner alleges no facts to show that

16   Respondent has a ministerial duty to provide an explanation in the form demanded by Petitioner.

17         Petitioner insists that the CHP was required to “identify which statutory element fails,”

18   “name a single enumerated qualifying offense considered or rejected,” “engage with the dashcam

19   evidence in CHP’s own custody,” “reconcile its conclusion with CHP’s own section 851.6

20   detention certificate,” “apply the rebuttable presumption of helpfulness,” or “explain what

21   ‘standard of qualifying criminal activity’ was applied, or how the ‘similar activity’ catch-all in

22   section 679.10(d) was analyzed.”

23         But the statute does not support Petitioner’s demands. Section 679.10. subdivision (i)(2) is

24   clear (and only two sentences long). It provides: “If a certifying entity does not certify a Form I-

25   918 Supplement B certification, they shall provide a written explanation for the denial of the

26   Form I-918 Supplement B certification. The written denial shall include specific details of any

27
     1
      Petitioner appears to interpret “obstruction of justice” as the fabrication of evidence. See
28   Petitioner’s Supporting Legal Analysis and Evidentiary Summary, Ex. 3 to Petition, at 3-4.
                                                        15
                                                             Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   reasonable requests for cooperation and a detailed description of how the victim refused to

 2   cooperate.” (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (i)(2).) In other words, the only specificity the statute

 3   requires from a written denial is “specific details of any reasonable requests for cooperation and a

 4   detailed description of how the victim refused to cooperate.” (See Pen. Code § 679.10, subd.

 5   (i)(2).) Simply put, Petitioner has alleged no such requests for cooperation (and there were none,

 6   given the nature of the incident), and so the statute does not require specific details of nonexistent

 7   requests for cooperation.

 8         As such, the CHP’s denial letter, incorporated into the petition, meets the requirements of

 9   section 679.10. It informed Petitioner that the November 1, 2024 incident did “not meet the

10   standard of qualifying criminal activity identified in Section 679.10 of the California Penal Code

11   (PC).” (CHP Denial Letter, Ex. 1 to Petition.) The denial letter further explained that CHP’s

12   review “found no substantially similar qualifying criminal activity as defined in Section 679.10(d)

13   PC.” (See id.) It thus provided a “written explanation” for the denial (no qualifying criminal

14   activity) and therefore, as noted, did not need to provide details regarding requests or refusals to

15   cooperate.

16         D.     The Rebuttable Presumption of Helpfulness Is Not Applicable On the
                  Facts Alleged
17

18         Petitioner’s third cause of action seeks a petition for writ of mandate under Section 1085 for
19   the CHP’s failure to apply the rebuttable presumption of helpfulness pursuant to Penal Code
20   section 679.10(h). (Pet., ¶ 86.) However, Petitioner has not shown that Respondent has a
21   ministerial duty to apply the rebuttable presumption on the facts alleged.
22         The statute provides, in relevant part: “[T]here is a rebuttable presumption that a victim is
23   helpful, has been helpful, or is likely to be helpful to the detection or investigation or prosecution
24   of that qualifying criminal activity, if the victim has not refused or failed to provide information
25   and assistance reasonably requested by law enforcement.” (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subd. (h).)
26         Petitioner insists that “[t]he presumption attaches as a matter of law on this record” because
27   “[h]e has never refused or failed to provide any information or assistance reasonably requested by
28
                                                       16
                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   CHP, POST, the DMV, or any other investigating entity.” (Pet., ¶ 87.) While it may be true that

 2   Petitioner has never refused to assist with an investigation into criminal activity, he has not

 3   alleged that CHP (or any other agency, for that matter) requested information and assistance into

 4   “the detection, investigation, or prosecution of a qualifying criminal activity.” (See Pen. Code, §

 5   679.10, subd. (h).) Accordingly, the presumption is plainly not applicable.

25         Petitioner is not entitled to any of the requested relief. First, to the extent Petitioner seeks

26   advisory opinions on purely legal questions, these requests are not cognizable. Section

27
     2
      Petitioner cites Section 679.10, subdivision (g), but this appears to be an error, as that section
28   does not set forth any requirement for written explanations of a certification denial.
                                                          17
                                                               Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   1060 provides: “Any person . . . who desires a declaration of his or her rights or duties with

 2   respect to another . . . may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of

 3   the respective parties, bring an original action . . . in the superior court for a declaration of his or

 4   her rights and duties . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060.) “The fundamental basis of declaratory

 5   relief is the existence of an actual, present controversy over a proper subject.” (Linda Vista

 6   Village San Diego Homeowners Ass’n, Inc. v. Tecolote Investors, LLC (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th

 7   166, 181 [citations and quotation marks removed].) “The ‘actual controversy’ language in Code

 8   of Civil Procedure section 1060 encompasses a probable future controversy relating to the legal

 9   rights and duties of the parties. For a probable future controversy to constitute an ‘actual

10   controversy,’ however, the probable future controversy must be ripe. A controversy is ripe when

11   it has reached, but has not passed, the point that the facts have sufficiently congealed to permit an

12   intelligent and useful decision to be made.” (County of San Diego v. State of California, supra,

13   164 Cal.App.4th at p. 606 [emphasis in original] [citations and quotation marks omitted].)

14         Critically, “[d]eclaratory relief operates prospectively to declare future rights, rather than to

15   redress past wrongs.” (County of San Diego v. State of California, supra, 164 Cal.App.4th at p.

16   607, quoting Canova v. Trustees of Imperial Irrigation District Employee Pension Plan (2007)

17   150 Cal.App.4th 1487, 1497.) A declaratory judgment “serves to set controversies at rest before

18   they lead to repudiation of obligations, invasion of rights or commission of wrongs; in short, the

19   remedy is to be used in the interests of preventive justice, to declare rights rather than execute

20   them.” (Id. at p. 608, quoting Babb v. Superior Court (1971) 3 Cal.3d 841, 848.) “An actual

21   controversy under the declaratory relief statute is one which admits of definitive and conclusive

22   relief by judgment within the field of judicial administration as distinguished from an advisory

23   opinion upon a particular or hypothetical state of facts.” (Monterey Coastkeeper v. Central Coast

24   Regional Water Quality Control Bd. (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 1, 13 [internal citations and quotation

25   marks omitted].) “Where the allegations of the complaint reveal the controversy to be . . . an

26   attempt to obtain an advisory opinion from the court, the fundamental basis of declaratory relief is

27   lacking.” (Rhonda S. v. Kaiser Foundation Health Plain, Inc. (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 643, 648.)

28
                                                        18
                                                             Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1         In line with these principles, “[a]n action for declaratory relief is not appropriate to review

 2   an administrative decision.” (State of California v. Superior Court (1974) 12 Cal.3d 237, 249;

 3   see also Zetterberg v. State Dept. of Public Health (1974) 43 Cal.App.3d 657, 663 [“A difference

 4   of opinion as to the interpretation of a statute as between a citizen and a governmental agency

 5   does not give rise to a justiciable controversy [for declaratory relief].”]; Monterey Coastkeeper v.

 6   Central Coast Regional Water Quality Control Board, supra, 76 Cal.App.5th at p. 18

 7   [“Declaratory relief generally is not available to use the courts to tell an administrative agency

 8   how to do its job.”].)

 9         Petitioner’s declaratory relief claim is manifestly improper because it seeks advisory

10   opinions regarding the proper interpretation and application of Penal Code sections 679.10,

11   subdivisions (d), (i)(2), (h), and (k), and therefore no actual, ripe controversy exists. Courts may

12   not expound on the meaning of statutes guiding agency action on purely legal grounds when no

13   present controversy exists. (See, e.g., Zetterberg v. State Dept. of Public Health, supra, 43

14   Cal.App.3d at p. 663 [rejecting plaintiffs’ request for the court to declare that defendant state

15   agencies had the power and duty to enforce pollution standards under an interpretation of a state

16   law].) Further, there is no “future controversy.” (County of San Diego v. State of California,

17   supra, 164 Cal.App.4th at p. 606.) Respondent has already considered Petitioner’s certification

18   request and denied it.

19         Second, to the extent that Petitioner asks the Court to declare that Respondent must reverse

20   or further expound on its denial of Petitioner’s certification request, such relief is inappropriate,

21   because declaratory relief cannot unwind a past administrative decision. Courts have rejected

22   similar efforts to use declaratory relief to redress prior administrative action. (State of California

23   v. Superior Court, supra, 12 Cal.3d at p. 249 [rejecting plaintiffs’ attempt to use declaratory relief

24   to challenge state agency’s denial of permit]; Tejon Real Estate, LLC v. City of Los Angeles

25   (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 149, 155 [denying plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim that sought to

26   compel court to interpret city regulations differently than city departments had].)

27         There is yet one more reason why Petitioner’s declaratory relief claim fails to state a cause

28   of action. Where a petitioner proceeds by a combined petition for writ of mandate and civil
                                                       19
                                                            Memorandum of Points and Authorities (26CV191136)
 1   action for declaratory relief, a ruling in favor of the respondent on the writ petition entitles the

 2   respondent to a judgment disposing of the entire matter if the ruling resolves allegations essential

 3   to petitioner’s civil claims. (Griset v. Fair Political Practices Commission (2001) 25 Cal.4th 688,

 4   697-700.) In Griset, for example, the Supreme Court held that the trial court’s resolution of

 5   plaintiff’s writ claims on the constitutionality of a law necessarily disposed of plaintiff’s causes of

 6   action to enjoin future enforcement of the law and to enjoin a state agency from enforcing fines

 7   for violation of that law. (Id. at pp. 699-700.)

 8         Here, petitioner seeks a declaration that CHP’s April 20, 2026 denial letter, as a matter of

 9   law, does not satisfy Penal Code section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2)’s requirement for a written

10   explanation. (See Pet., ¶ 93(c).) If Petitioner is not entitled to a writ of ordinary mandate because

11   the Court determines that there is no ministerial duty compelling CHP to provide a written denial

12   in the form demanded by Petitioner, the declaration Petitioner seeks becomes moot.

13         For these reasons, Petitioner is not entitled to any writ of ordinary mandate, and

14   accordingly, his declaratory relief claim is moot.
