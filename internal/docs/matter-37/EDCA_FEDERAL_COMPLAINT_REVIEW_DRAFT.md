# INTRODUCTION

1. This action arises from an accepted Home Depot hire at its warehouse, distribution, and fulfillment facility at 5395 Hopkins Road, Tracy, California. Plaintiff accepted the position and began Home Depot’s onboarding process. Home Depot then required and ran an employment background check. On information and belief, Home Depot used First Advantage Background Services Corp. (“First Advantage”) as the consumer reporting agency for that screening.

2. The First Advantage relationship is not pleaded from guesswork alone. Public federal-court records contain a Home Depot-branded “BACKGROUND CHECK DISCLOSURE AND AUTHORIZATION” identifying First Advantage as the consumer reporting agency Home Depot would use to procure California investigative consumer reports. Other federal litigation describes First Advantage furnishing a consumer report to Home Depot for a prospective employee, and First Advantage itself later publicly featured a Home Depot representative in a program devoted to workforce screening practices. The precise order, package, transmission, and adjudication records for Plaintiff’s screening are in Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession.

3. On information and belief, First Advantage prepared and furnished Plaintiff’s employment background report to Home Depot in connection with the Tracy hire, and Home Depot received and used that report. After the background check, Home Depot ceased the onboarding process and ended, withdrew, or prevented Plaintiff from beginning or continuing the accepted Tracy employment on or about August 2024.

4. Before taking that adverse employment action, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the First Advantage report and did not provide the written Summary of Rights required by the Fair Credit Reporting Act (“FCRA”), 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A). Home Depot thereby deprived Plaintiff of the statutory pre-decision opportunity to review the report, dispute its contents with the consumer reporting agency, and respond before the employment decision became final.

5. When Home Depot denied, withdrew, ended, or prevented the employment under circumstances in which it had obtained an investigative consumer report, it also did not advise Plaintiff that such a report had been obtained and did not provide First Advantage’s name and address as required by California Civil Code § 1786.40.

6. California Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) prohibits use of information disclosed pursuant to California’s Megan’s Law website for purposes relating to employment except where another law authorizes the use. Plaintiff alleges, on information and belief and as an ultimate fact, that First Advantage furnished Home Depot information concerning Plaintiff that was disclosed pursuant to § 290.46, including Plaintiff’s California registry information, and that Home Depot used that information for the purpose of deciding whether Plaintiff would begin or continue the accepted Tracy employment.

7. That allegation is supported by specific facts rather than conjecture: Home Depot accepted Plaintiff for the Tracy position and began onboarding him; Home Depot then ran an employment background check through a screening vendor that public records identify as First Advantage; Plaintiff was contemporaneously listed on California’s Megan’s Law website; First Advantage reports concerning this same Plaintiff for other employers during the surrounding period included sex-offender-registry screening and California-registry information; and Home Depot terminated the onboarding sequence after the background check without communicating an independent reason. The transaction-specific report, source fields, adjudication codes, access logs, and decision records are in Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s possession.

# FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

## A. The Tracy Hire and Onboarding

23. Plaintiff accepted the employment and began onboarding. The Tracy Center is a warehouse, distribution, and fulfillment facility. On information and belief, the position for which Plaintiff was hired was warehouse, distribution, or fulfillment work at that facility and was not a position regulated by the Secretary of Transportation within the limited exception in 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(B)-(C). ## B. First Advantage's Screening of Plaintiff and the Comparator Record

25. On information and belief, First Advantage prepared an employment consumer report concerning Plaintiff at Home Depot’s request in or about August 2024 and furnished or made that report available to Home Depot through First Advantage’s employer-screening platform.

26. Plaintiff does not presently possess Home Depot’s copy of that report or its complete First Advantage order file. Home Depot and First Advantage possess the precise order date, completion date, client/requestor profile, package identifier, component results, source fields, adjudication status, client criteria, access history, transmission history, and records identifying the information Home Depot reviewed.

27. Plaintiff alleges that the Home Depot report contained information disclosed pursuant to Penal Code § 290.46, including Plaintiff’s California sex-offender-registry information. That allegation is made on information and belief because the report and source fields are within Home Depot’s and First Advantage’s control, and it is supported by the factual pattern alleged below.

28. At the time of Home Depot’s screening, Plaintiff was publicly listed on California’s Megan’s Law website. Plaintiff’s identity and underlying California registry entry were the same regardless of which employer requested a First Advantage employment screen; what could vary by employer was the screening package, adjudication configuration, or presentation of the underlying data.

29. Plaintiff possesses a First Advantage employment background report concerning himself generated for United Parcel Service during the same general period. In that report, ordinary criminal-search components returned non-adverse results, while the sex-offender-registry component was the adverse or decisional component and expressly identified the source of record as the California Sex Offender Registry. That report demonstrates that First Advantage’s screening of this same Plaintiff was capable of retrieving, reporting, and adjudicating his California registry information in an employment-screening product.

30. In a separate employment screening for Federal Express Corporation, also conducted by First Advantage, FedEx later admitted in its responsive pleading that the report identified Plaintiff as a California registrant. Together, these separate First Advantage screenings of the same Plaintiff provide additional factual support for the allegation that First Advantage’s contemporaneous Home Depot screening likewise contained Plaintiff’s California registry information.

31. Plaintiff does not allege that UPS’s or FedEx’s report is Home Depot’s report. He alleges those comparator records because they concern the same consumer, the same CRA, the same underlying California registry entry, and the same type of employment background screening, and therefore make the alleged contents of the Home Depot report more than speculative.

32. On information and belief, the registry information in the Home Depot report was adverse, decisional, review-triggering, ineligible, or otherwise material to Home Depot’s screening result and employment decision. Home Depot’s actual report, adjudication matrix, access history, applicant-tracking record, reason codes, and decisionmaker communications will identify the precise mechanism.

## C. The Adverse Employment Action

33. After the report was completed and transmitted, Home Depot ceased the onboarding process and stopped Plaintiff from beginning or continuing work at the Tracy Center.

34. The adverse action occurred on or about August 2024, after the First Advantage report had been completed and made available to Home Depot.

35. Home Depot did not identify to Plaintiff any independent post-report event that caused the accepted employment to end. Plaintiff alleges that Home Depot used the report, in whole or in part, in making the decision.

36. The FCRA defines adverse action in the employment context to include a denial of employment or any other decision for employment purposes that adversely affects a current or prospective employee. Home Depot’s withdrawal, ending, prevention, or non-finalization of the accepted employment was an adverse action.

## D. Home Depot Did Not Provide the Required Pre-Adverse Package

37. Before the adverse action became final, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the First Advantage report.

38. Before the adverse action became final, Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff the written description of FCRA rights prescribed by 15 U.S.C. § 1681g(c)(3).

39. Plaintiff therefore was not given the report and statutory rights information before Home Depot acted on the report. Had Home Depot complied, Plaintiff would have been able, before the employment decision became final, to identify that the report contained his California registry information, challenge or explain the report as appropriate, and advise Home Depot that Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) prohibited use of information disclosed pursuant to § 290.46 for this employment purpose.

40. Home Depot’s failure caused concrete injury. Plaintiff lost an accepted employment opportunity and resulting wages and benefits. The omitted pre-adverse process was directly tied to that injury because it deprived Plaintiff of the opportunity to identify and challenge Home Depot’s contemplated use of the same registry information that Plaintiff alleges Home Depot was legally prohibited from using for the employment decision.

## E. Home Depot Did Not Provide the ICRAA Adverse-Action Advice

41. The First Advantage report was an investigative consumer report within the meaning of the California Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (“ICRAA”) because it was prepared for employment purposes and contained information bearing on Plaintiff’s character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living, including criminal / public-record and registry information.

42. When Home Depot denied, withdrew, ended, or prevented the employment under circumstances in which the First Advantage investigative consumer report had been obtained, Home Depot did not advise Plaintiff that the report had been obtained and did not supply First Advantage’s name and address.

43. The omission alleged in paragraph 42 is distinct from the FCRA omission alleged in Count One. Count One concerns Home Depot’s failure **before adverse action** to furnish the report and federal Summary of Rights. Count Two concerns Home Depot’s separate California duty, upon denying employment under circumstances in which an investigative report was obtained, to advise Plaintiff and identify the investigative consumer reporting agency.

# FIRST CAUSE OF ACTION  
## Fair Credit Reporting Act — 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A)  
### Against Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.

56. Plaintiff realleges paragraphs 1 through 55.

57. Plaintiff was a “consumer,” the First Advantage report was a “consumer report,” Home Depot procured or caused it to be procured for “employment purposes,” and Home Depot was a “person” using the report within the meaning of the FCRA.

58. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A), before taking adverse action based in whole or in part on an employment consumer report, the person intending to take the adverse action must provide the consumer (i) a copy of the report and (ii) a written description of the consumer’s rights under the FCRA.

59. Home Depot took adverse employment action based in whole or in part on the First Advantage report.

60. Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff a copy of the report before the adverse action.

61. Home Depot did not provide Plaintiff the written Summary of Rights before the adverse action.

62. Home Depot therefore violated § 1681b(b)(3)(A).

63. Home Depot’s noncompliance was negligent within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681o and, on proof, willful within the meaning of 15 U.S.C. § 1681n. Willfulness is plausibly supported by Home Depot’s own publicly filed background-check form, which expressly recognized that before information from a consumer or investigative consumer report is used to make an adverse employment decision, Home Depot would provide the applicant the report and a summary of FCRA rights. That standardized language demonstrates corporate awareness of the pre-adverse obligation years before Plaintiff’s screening. Home Depot has also previously litigated FCRA employment-screening obligations in federal court, including *Harris v. Home Depot U.S.A., Inc.*, 114 F. Supp. 3d 868 (N.D. Cal. 2015), which addressed Home Depot’s employment consumer-report disclosure practices. Plaintiff does not presently plead a claim under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(A). *Harris* is alleged as additional evidence that Home Depot is a sophisticated FCRA user of employment consumer reports, together with the *Ford* form’s own pre-adverse language. Plaintiff pleads negligence and willfulness in the alternative.

64. As a direct result, Plaintiff suffered actual damages including lost employment compensation and the loss of the statutory opportunity to review and respond to the report before the employment decision. If willfulness is proven, Plaintiff also seeks statutory and punitive damages authorized by § 1681n.

80. Section 290.46(j)(4)(A) makes a prohibited user liable for the civil remedies specified in that subdivision. Plaintiff seeks actual damages, any additional amount authorized by the statute, exemplary relief to the extent proven and legally available, attorney’s fees if counsel later appears and the statute authorizes recovery, and any other nonduplicative remedy available under § 290.46(j)(4)(A).
