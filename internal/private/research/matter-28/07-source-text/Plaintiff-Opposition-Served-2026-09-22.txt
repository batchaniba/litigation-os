20         IV. LEGAL STANDARD AND THE PROPER SCOPE OF JUDICIAL NOTICE

21

22          A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests the legal sufficiency of the complaint. The Court accepts

23   well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draws reasonable inferences in Plaintiff's favor.

24   Hishon v. King & Spalding, 467 U.S. 69, 73 (1984); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678

25   (2009). A self-represented pleading is liberally construed, and leave to amend must be granted

26   unless it is absolutely clear the defects cannot be cured. Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94

27   (2007); Lopez v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1130-31 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc); see Fed. R. Civ. P.

28   15(a)(2) (leave to amend shall be freely given when justice so requires).

                                                   Page 7
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1

 2          Preclusion is an affirmative defense that ordinarily is not raised by motion; it may

 3   support dismissal at the pleading stage only where its elements are established by the

 4   complaint and judicially noticeable materials with no disputed issue of material fact. Scott v.

 5   Kuhlmann, 746 F.2d 1377, 1378 (9th Cir. 1984) (per curiam). Plaintiff does not oppose notice

 6   of the existence, filing dates, and legal effect of the state-court filings. He opposes treating

 7   allegations and arguments in those papers as true, or treating grounds argued in a demurrer

 8   memorandum as findings made by the court. See Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc., 899

 9   F.3d 988, 999 (9th Cir. 2018).

10

11          The Court should therefore notice what Exhibit C actually does--and what it does not

12   do. It sustains a demurrer; it does not dismiss the action, enter judgment, identify the ground

13   adopted, or make a merits finding. Defendants may not transform their own advocacy in

14   Exhibit B into findings absent from Exhibit C.

16                                             V. ARGUMENT

17

18   A. Defendants Have Not Established Claim or Issue Preclusion.

19

20          California claim preclusion requires "(1) the same cause of action (2) between the

21   same parties or parties in privity with them (3) after a final judgment on the merits in the first

22   suit." DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber, 61 Cal. 4th 813, 824 (2015); see Boeken v. Philip

23   Morris USA, Inc., 48 Cal. 4th 788, 797 (2010). The absence of a final judgment defeats claim

24   preclusion as to both Defendants. The separate absence of party identity or established privity

25   independently defeats the defense as to Smith. And because the order identifies no ground

26   actually decided, it cannot establish issue preclusion.

27

28

                                                    Page 8
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   1. Exhibit C is not a final judgment and therefore cannot preclude either Defendant.

 2

 3          Defendants attach no judgment. Exhibit C is titled as an order, directs that a demurrer

 4   be sustained without leave to amend, and contains no dismissal language. That distinction is

 5   dispositive. California requires a dismissal to be entered in writing as a judgment. Cal. Code

 6   Civ. Proc. § 581d. "Until entry of judgment, a ruling on a demurrer is not res judicata and may

 7   be reconsidered and changed by the trial court." Valvo v. University of Southern California, 67

 8   Cal. App. 3d 887, 892 n.3 (1977). Consistently, an order sustaining a demurrer without leave

 9   to amend is not appealable because it is not the final judgment. Berri v. Superior Court, 43

10   Cal. 2d 856, 860 (1955).

11

12          The unusual form of Exhibit C reinforces rather than changes that result.

13   "PROPOSED" was crossed out in the caption when the judge signed it, but each page still

14   bears the proposed-order footer. The document also bears "FILE BY FAX." Those features

15   may show that the lodged proposal became an entered order; they do not convert the order into

16   a judgment of dismissal. Nor does the May 22 proof of service establish service--or even

17   existence--of a later judgment. On the materials Defendants presented, the state action never

18   reached the final disposition required for claim preclusion.

19

20          This defect applies to both HOPE and Smith. Plaintiff does not argue that every

21   demurrer ruling is forever incapable of preclusive effect. He argues the narrower point

22   established by Defendants' packet: no ensuing judgment is before this Court.

23

24

25

26

27

28

                                                  Page 9
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   2. The generic order establishes no issue that was actually and necessarily decided.

 2

 3           Issue preclusion requires a determination of the identical issue that was actually

 4   litigated, necessarily decided, final, and asserted against a party or privy. DKN Holdings, 61

 5   Cal. 4th at 825. Exhibit C cannot satisfy those requirements. Defendants urged four grounds:

 6   causation, failure to exhaust, habeas as the asserted vehicle, and failure to state a claim. The

 7   order adopts none of them. It states only that the first amended complaint "fails to allege facts

 8   sufficient to state any cause of action." It contains no finding about the truth of Plaintiff's

 9   allegations, Smith's conduct, the blanket policy, state action, or any federal element.

10

11           Goddard v. Security Title Insurance & Guarantee Co., 14 Cal. 2d 47 (1939), does not

12   supply the missing finding. Goddard explains that the effect of a judgment following a general

13   demurrer depends on the defect adjudicated: a judgment may reach the merits where the

14   pleading establishes an absolute bar, but not where the defect is formal or curable by a

15   different pleading. Id. at 52-53. Here, there is no judgment and no identified basis from which

16   this Court could determine what issue, if any, the state court necessarily resolved.

17

18           Nor may Defendants select exhaustion from their own demurrer brief after the fact. If

19   the state ruling rested on prematurity or a failure to satisfy a precondition, that would not

20   adjudicate the underlying merits. Perez v. City of San Bruno, 27 Cal. 3d 875, 882-83 (1980).

21   And state administrative exhaustion is not a prerequisite to a § 1983 action. Patsy v. Board of

22   Regents, 457 U.S. 496, 516 (1982); see also Heath v. Cleary, 708 F.2d 1376, 1380 & n.4 (9th

23   Cir. 1983) (a prior state disposition based on nonexhaustion did not decide the unadjudicated

24   substantive federal claims). Those authorities are an alternative response if Defendants

25   contend the state court adopted exhaustion--not an invitation to infer a ground the order does

26   not state.

27

28           Defendants' "could have been litigated" argument does not cure the threshold problem.

                                                    Page 10
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   New legal labels generally do not avoid California claim preclusion where the same primary

 2   right was reduced to a final judgment. But without a final judgment, there is no claim

 3   preclusion to extend.

 4

 5   3. Defendants have not established that Smith is in privity with a state-court party for

 6   the conduct alleged here.

 7

 8          Smith was not a party to the state case. Defendants therefore must establish privity,

 9   which under California law requires more than a relationship label. It requires an identity or

10   community of interest, adequate representation of the nonparty's interest in the first action, and

11   circumstances making it fair to bind the nonparty. DKN Holdings, 61 Cal. 4th at 826. The

12   California Supreme Court has cautioned that jointly liable actors are not automatically privies

13   merely because a plaintiff previously sued one of them. Id. at 824-26.

14

15          Neither of Defendants' principal authorities establishes privity on these pleadings. In

16   Scott, the prior and later defendants were federal employees who all participated in the same

17   challenged agency inquiry; the Ninth Circuit described privity among officers of the same

18   government with respect to the same issue. 746 F.2d at 1378. Here, the Complaint attributes

19   distinct personal conduct to Smith: she allegedly possessed exclusive policy authority,

20   approved and maintained the statewide rule, issued a September 2024 manual perpetuating it,

21   personally assigned work to Plaintiff, received direct notice, and declined to correct the policy.

22   ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 12, 42-44, 54.

23

24          Drawsand v. F.F. Properties, L.L.P., 866 F. Supp. 2d 1110 (N.D. Cal. 2011), is

25   likewise distinguishable. Defendants cite page "1227," but the pertinent discussion appears at

26   page 1127. There, the earlier dismissal with prejudice was a final judgment, the employer and

27   employees were sued over the same property and the same conduct, and the complaint itself

28   alleged that the employees acted within the course and scope of employment. Id. at 1127.

                                                  Page 11
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   Here, the necessary final judgment is absent, and whether the prior HOPE defendants

 2   represented Smith's interests concerning her distinct, individual policy conduct is not

 3   established by the complaint or RJN.

 4

 4   B. The Complaint Plausibly Alleges That Defendants Acted Under Color of State Law.

 5

 6   1. The inquiry concerns the specific challenged conduct, not HOPE's status in the

 7   abstract.

 8

 9          The question is not whether every act of a private treatment provider is state action. It

10   is whether the conduct challenged here--jointly generating and using mandatory

11   treatment-retention records that affected continued parole supervision--is fairly attributable to

12   the State. Caviness v. Horizon Community Learning Center, Inc., 590 F.3d 806, 812 (9th Cir.

13   2010). State action may be established under any of several related approaches, including joint

14   action and close nexus; the inquiry is necessarily fact-bound. Rawson v. Recovery Innovations,

15   Inc., 975 F.3d 742, 747-48 (9th Cir. 2020).

16

17          The Complaint alleges far more than a government contract:

18

19          - State law requires Plaintiff's participation and assigns the duration decision to the

20          certified provider in consultation with the parole officer and with court approval. Cal.

21          Penal Code § 3008(d)(2); ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 14-16, 18-19.

22

23          - The implementing regulation requires a good-cause retention determination

24          memorialized on a Form 3043 signed by the treatment provider and parole agent. 15

25          Cal. Code Regs. § 3574(f)(1).

26

27          - HOPE allegedly supplied the treatment-status content and recommendations that the

28          containment team and CDCR used. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 31-37.

                                                   Page 13
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1

 2           - A parole agent allegedly directed HOPE to record a no-show despite acknowledging

 3           the absence resulted from a technological error. Id. ¶ 34.

 4

 5           - CDCR's discharge report expressly incorporated HOPE's assessment and

 6           recommendation before denying discharge. Id. ¶¶ 35-37.

 7

 8           Those allegations plausibly describe coordinated decision-making, not parallel private

 9   and public activity. A private person who is a willful participant in joint action with state

10   officials acts under color of state law. Rawson v. Recovery Innovations, Inc., 975 F.3d 742,

11   747-48 (9th Cir. 2020); see also Franklin v. Fox, 312 F.3d 423, 445 (9th Cir. 2002).

12   Professional judgment does not categorically break attribution; Rawson rejected that shortcut

13   and required a close, fact-intensive assessment of the State's involvement in the challenged

14   conduct. 975 F.3d at 753-54.

15

16           The alleged March 31 directive is particularly concrete. A state agent did not merely

17   acquiesce in a private report; he allegedly instructed the private provider what false attendance

18   result to record, with a supervisor copied. ECF No. 1 ¶ 34. The later CDCR decision allegedly

19   relied on HOPE's treatment information. Id. ¶¶ 35-37. Those facts permit a reasonable

20   inference of willful joint participation.

21

22           That the November 2025 discharge-review report also recites other supervision-related

23   factors--including prior parole-violation history, employment status, time served on parole,

24   and a stability assessment--does not defeat that inference. Those additional factors, even if

25   judicially noticed for their existence, do not render HOPE's Form 3043 participation

26   implausible as joint action; at most they raise a factual question of comparative causation

27   unsuited to resolution on the pleadings, and they do not make amendment futile.

28

                                                   Page 14
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   2. Defendants' published authorities are distinguishable on their own terms.

 2

 3          Caviness supports focusing on the conduct alleged. There, a charter school's public

 4   educational role did not make its post-employment statements state action because the

 5   complaint did not allege state involvement in those employment decisions. 590 F.3d at 812,

 6   816. Here, the State allegedly participated in the challenged reporting and retention process

 7   itself: the provider and parole agent consult, sign the prescribed form, and feed the result into

 8   discharge review. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 31-37.

 9

10          Florer v. Congregation Pidyon Shevuyim, N.A., 639 F.3d 916 (9th Cir. 2011), arose

11   from a contract chaplain's ecclesiastical decision that the plaintiff did not qualify for requested

12   Jewish materials and services. The record contained no evidence that corrections officials

13   wanted that result, jointly classified the plaintiff, or deprived him of prison-provided benefits

14   because of it. Id. at 923-26. Here, no ecclesiastical or comparably private professional

15   standard is at issue. The Complaint alleges an administrative, state-prescribed retention

16   process; direct instructions from a parole agent; a jointly signed form; and actual use of

17   HOPE's output in a discharge decision.

18

19          Rawson, not Florer, supplies the closer analytical model. Although the defendants in

20   Rawson were nominally private and exercised medical judgment, the Ninth Circuit held that

21   the totality of their integration into the State's involuntary-commitment process could make

22   their conduct state action. 975 F.3d at 747-54. Plaintiff need not show identical facts. Rawson

23   establishes the relevant point at this stage: professional discretion is not dispositive, no single

24   fact controls, and the relationship must be assessed as a whole.

25

26          Defendants also overread Franklin. Its "meeting of the minds" formulation governs a

27   conspiracy route to joint action. 312 F.3d at 445. Plaintiff also pleads direct willful

28   participation and interdependence in a shared administrative process. He need not allege that

                                                   Page 15
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   CDCR and HOPE expressly agreed that their objective was to violate the Constitution. The

 2   allegations that they jointly generated the disputed inputs and used them to maintain

 3   mandatory treatment are enough to proceed to a factual record.

 4

 5   3. West reinforces the plausibility of attribution.

 6

 7          In West v. Atkins, 487 U.S. 42, 54-57 (1988), a contracted physician acted under color

 8   of law when providing care the State was obligated to furnish to a prisoner. Plaintiff relies on

 9   West as reinforcement, not as a categorical rule that every public contractor is a state actor.

10   The pleaded circumstances matter: Plaintiff could not opt out of the program; the State

11   designated the certified-provider/parole-agent process; HOPE allegedly occupied the only

12   certified-provider role available in the region; and HOPE's reports were used in the State's

13   exercise of supervisory authority. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 17-19, 31-37. Taken together, those

14   allegations plausibly show power made possible by state law and joint administration.

15

16   4. The Complaint alleges Smith's personal participation and HOPE's policy.

17

18          The motion treats Smith as though liability rested only on her title. It does not. The

19   Complaint alleges that Smith alone could modify company policy, personally approved and

20   maintained the blanket rule, directed the manual embodying it, personally assigned work to

21   Plaintiff, received notice of the controversy, and failed to correct the policy. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 12,

22   42-44, 54-55. Those are allegations of personal participation and deliberate indifference, not

23   respondeat superior.

24

25          The claim against HOPE likewise identifies a policy and causal mechanism: the

26   blanket retention policy, allegedly false treatment-status reporting, and retaliatory use of that

27   reporting process. Id. ¶¶ 56-59. At the pleading stage, that is sufficient to identify the

28   challenged policy and its alleged role in the injury.

 2   C. The First Amendment Claim Alleges More Than Offensive Responsive Speech.

 3

 4          Defendants isolate Palacios's words and characterize the claim as criticism answered

 5   by criticism. The Complaint alleges a different sequence. Plaintiff complained about HOPE's

 6   retention policy, missing homework, clinician turnover, phase progression, and treatment

 7   completion. ECF No. 1 ¶ 38. At a containment-team meeting that affected parole conditions,

 8   Palacios allegedly called him "disruptive," a "ringleader," and "abhorrent," and expressly

 9   linked those characterizations to his complaints: "when you start complaining then the other

10   guys chime in." Id. ¶¶ 39-40. The Complaint further alleges that HOPE's adverse reporting

11   was used in treatment-retention and discharge decisions. Id. ¶¶ 31-41.

12

13          Thus, the alleged adverse action is not insult alone. It is the use of derogatory and

14   allegedly false treatment characterizations within an official decision-making channel that

15   could prolong mandatory treatment and supervision. A First Amendment retaliation claim

16   requires protected activity, an adverse action that would chill a person of ordinary firmness,

17   and causation. Arizona Students' Association v. Arizona Board of Regents, 824 F.3d 858, 867

18   (9th Cir. 2016). The explicit reference to Plaintiff's complaining supplies a plausible

19   retaliatory nexus, and the alleged parole consequences supply injury beyond responsive

20   speech. Whether the statements actually affected the outcome is a factual question; dismissal

21   is not warranted merely because Defendants contest causation.

22

23   D. The Due-Process Claim Challenges a Systemic, Foreseeable Deprivation, Not Merely

24   a Mistake Correctable by Grievance.

25

26          Plaintiff does not challenge his original conviction, the initial imposition of

27   sex-offender treatment, or an abstract right to early discharge. He challenges the alleged use of

28   a predetermined corporate policy and fabricated treatment inputs in a process governing the

                                                  Page 17
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   duration of mandatory treatment and continued supervision. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 31-37, 50, 57-59.

 2

 3           Defendants' own cited provisions identify substantive and procedural constraints.

 4   Section 3008(d)(2) assigns the duration determination to an individualized consultation among

 5   the certified professional, parole officer, and court. Section 3574(f)(1) requires good cause, a

 6   written reason, and signatures from the provider and parole agent. The Complaint alleges

 7   those safeguards were hollowed out by a blanket rule and false factual inputs. It further alleges

 8   concrete restraints--GPS monitoring, curfew, mandatory attendance, and continued parole

 9   supervision--rather than stigma alone. Id. ¶¶ 37, 48-52. The Ninth Circuit recognizes a

10   protected liberty interest in avoiding the stigmatizing sex-offender label coupled with a

11   mandatory treatment program whose successful completion is a precondition to parole

12   eligibility. Neal v. Shimoda, 131 F.3d 818, 830 (9th Cir. 1997). Greenholtz v. Inmates of

13   Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), on which Defendants rely,

14   concerns only the discretionary decision whether to grant release on parole; it does not govern

15   the distinct liberty interest, recognized in Neal, that Plaintiff asserts here.

16

17           The availability of a grievance concerning mistakes in a discharge-review report does

18   not defeat the claim on the face of the Complaint. First, Defendants identify a remedy for

19   factual errors in the later Form 1502 report; the Complaint challenges an alleged corporate

20   policy and false inputs originating in HOPE's treatment and Form 3043 process. Second, the

21   random-and-unauthorized-act doctrine does not automatically make postdeprivation relief

22   sufficient where the deprivation is foreseeable, predeprivation safeguards are practicable, and

23   the challenged conduct occurs through an established process. Zinermon v. Burch, 494 U.S.

24   113, 132-39 (1990); see Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co., 455 U.S. 422, 436 (1982). The

25   alleged blanket policy and recurring reports fit that systemic theory.

26

27           Finally, Patsy means Plaintiff was not required to exhaust state administrative

28   remedies before bringing § 1983 claims. 457 U.S. at 516. That rule does not itself prove the

                                                    Page 18
              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   process inadequate; it forecloses Defendants' attempt to convert nonexhaustion into a

 2   jurisdictional or pleading bar. The adequacy of the specific procedures, and whether they

 3   could remedy the alleged policy-driven deprivation before it occurred, require a developed

 4   record.

 6   E. The Bane Act Claim Pleads Coercion Beyond the Underlying Constitutional

 9             The Bane Act applies whether or not the defendant acted under color of law. Cal. Civ.

14   parole officials to characterize Plaintiff adversely because he complained. ECF No. 1 ¶ 62.

15

16             Those allegations identify the means of coercion separately from the underlying rights:

17   comply with the allegedly predetermined treatment regime and scheduling demands or risk

18   adverse reports, continued restraints, or a parole violation. At minimum, whether that pressure

19   was sufficiently coercive is fact-bound and should not be resolved by recasting the allegations

20   as mere criticism.

21

22             Plaintiff does not rely on the $25,000 civil penalty in section 52.1(b), which is reserved

23   to specified public enforcement actions. If necessary, he can remove that remedy from the

24   prayer without dismissal of the cause of action.

 7   G. The RICO Claim Should Not Be Dismissed With Prejudice.

 8

 9          The motion challenges the enterprise allegations and Rule 9(b) particularity. The

10   Complaint alleges an association-in-fact with a common purpose, ongoing relationships, and

11   longevity, and it identifies recurring mail and electronic transmissions of allegedly false

12   treatment-status reports. ECF No. 1 ¶¶ 68-73. As to Smith, the required person-enterprise

13   distinction is at least plausible because a natural person is legally distinct from the corporation

14   and the broader association through which she allegedly acted. See Cedric Kushner

15   Promotions, Ltd. v. King, 533 U.S. 158, 163 (2001).

16

17          Plaintiff recognizes two pleading points that can be clarified if amendment is ordered.

18   First, 18 U.S.C. § 1519 is not among the predicate offenses listed in 18 U.S.C. § 1961(1);

19   Plaintiff therefore does not rely on paragraph 70(c) as a RICO predicate. The claim instead

20   depends on sufficiently pleaded mail and wire fraud. Second, an amended complaint can

21   identify each alleged mailing or wire by date, sender, recipient, document, false content, and

22   role in the alleged scheme, and can clarify which Defendant is the RICO "person" and which

23   persons and entities compose the enterprise.

24

25          Defendants' own authority demonstrates why prejudice is unwarranted. Schreiber

26   Distributing Co. v. Serv-Well Furniture Co., 806 F.2d 1393, 1400-01 (9th Cir. 1986)--not

27   2004, as the motion states--reversed a RICO dismissal and directed that leave to amend be

28   granted where the fraud allegations lacked particularity. If this Court finds the current mail-

              PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO MOTION TO DISMISS -- NEWANFORBI v. SMITH (N.D. Cal.)
 1   and wire-fraud allegations too general, the same remedy is appropriate here.

 3   H. Any Dismissal Should Be With Leave to Amend.

 4

 5          Dismissal without leave is proper only when amendment could not cure the defect.

 6   Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2); Lopez, 203 F.3d at 1130-31. Plaintiff can cure any pleading

 7   deficiency identified by the Court. He can add a transaction-by-transaction fraud and RICO

 8   schedule; identify each speaker and recipient; allege the precise state-provider interactions

14   therefore cannot justify the extraordinary request to terminate every claim with prejudice at

15   the first Rule 12 stage.
