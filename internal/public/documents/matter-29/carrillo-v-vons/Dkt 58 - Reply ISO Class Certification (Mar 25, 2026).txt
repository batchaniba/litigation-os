1                                              INTRODUCTION
2          Who wants to live in a world with no forgiveness, no redemption, and no second
3    chances? Apparently, Vons does. In its Opposition Vons does not deny that it has a common
4    practice of denying employment to applicants with SOR information in their background
5    reports. Instead, it argues that it relies on SOR information to “protect those at risk.” But this
6    is a merits argument, and it commonly applies to every Megan’s Law Class member. Vons’
7    acknowledged practice of relying on SOR information in employment decisions shows the
8    Megan’s Law Class is appropriate for class certification. Similarly, with respect to the Fair
9    Chance Class Vons offers zero evidence of any individualized assessment it conducted, for
10 Plaintiff or any of the other 901 Fair Chance Class members. As such, Vons fails to satisfy its

11 burden of proof to show any individualized issue bars certification of the Fair Chance Class.

12         Vons’ Opposition does not identify a genuine Rule 23 defect. Instead, it largely sets
13   forth merits defenses and ad hominem attacks on Plaintiff as purported class-certification
14   arguments. But Rule 23 asks whether Plaintiff’s claims under the Megan’s Law and Fair
15   Chance Act are cohesive enough to be tried through common proof—not whether Vons has
16   assembled inflammatory facts it hopes to use against Plaintiff. Vons has already identified
17   the members of the Classes, and Plaintiff accurately reflects the groups he seeks to represent
18   – those who were denied employment as a result of SOR information in their background
19   reports without any individualized assessment of their fitness for the position. Accordingly,
20   Plaintiff respectfully requests the Court grant class certification.

3 B.      Typicality Is Satisfied For The Megan’s Law Class
4         “Typicality is satisfied when the representative’s claims are reasonably coextensive
5 with those of absent class members, even if they are not substantially identical.” DZ Rsrv. v.

6 Meta Platforms, Inc., 96 F.4th 1223, 1238 (9th Cir. 2024), cert. denied, 145 S. Ct. 1051

7 (2025). A unique defense defeats typicality only when there is a danger that the named

8 plaintiff will become preoccupied with that defense to the detriment of the class. Id. at 1238-

9 39. Vons argues Plaintiff is atypical because he disclosed his conviction, and “Vons will
10 show that even if Plaintiff had never appeared on the MLW, the company would have

11 initiated the adverse action process based solely on his own disclosure of the underlying

12 conviction.” Opp. at 24:13-16 (bold in original). Vons’ argument is irrelevant here. Whether

13 or not Plaintiff disclosed his conviction does not free Vons from liability under the Megan’s

14 Law. The Megan’s Law does not require the SOR information be the “but-for cause of the

15 employment decision.” Opp. at 24:17-19; Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) (prohibiting “use

16 of any information that is disclosed” in Megan’s Law Website). The plain language of the

17 statute provides that the “use of any” SOR information in employment decisions is

18 prohibited, plain and simple. The record shows that Vons had a common practice of using

19 SOR information in denying employment to Plaintiff and more than 40 other persons.

20 Declaration of Raymond Y. Kim in support of Motion for Class Certification, Dkt. No. 55-1

21 (“Kim Decl.”), ¶ 14, Ex. 4. Vons does not dispute this. Indeed, all Megan’s Law Class

22 members received the Adverse Action Notices from Vons based on Vons’ use of SOR

23 information. Specifically, after receiving a conditional job offer from Vons members of the

24 Megan’s Law Class, including Plaintiff, received the Adverse Action Notice stating that

25 Vons’ decision to withdraw its conditional employment offer was “influenced in whole or in

26 part by” the SOR information in their background reports. Carrillo Decl., ¶ 16, Ex. I; Kim

27 Decl., ¶ 18. The Megan’s Law claim is not defeated merely because Plaintiff truthfully

28 disclosed his conviction history; the Adverse Action Notice itself states the decision was

5 C.     Plaintiff Is Typical Of The Fair Chance Class
6        Vons argues that Plaintiff is not typical of the Fair Chance Class on the grounds that
7 “he was not an applicant at the time Vons conducted its individualized assessment and

8 terminated his employment.” Opp. at 24:28-25:2. As a threshold matter, this is a merits issue

9 that has no bearing at this class certification stage. Olean Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v.
10 Bumble Bee Foods LLC, 31 F.4th 651, 667 (9th Cir. 2022) (“[A] district court cannot decline

11 certification merely because it considers plaintiffs’ evidence relating to the common question

12 to be unpersuasive and unlikely to succeed in carrying the plaintiffs’ burden of proof on that

13 issue.”) (citation omitted). Second, Vons’ “not an applicant” argument applies equally to

14 everyone in the Fair Chance Class, because they all, like Plaintiff, received a conditional job

15 offer pending the background investigation, pursuant to California law. See Cal. Gov. Code

16 12952 (“[I]t is an unlawful employment practice for an employer…: (2) To inquire into or

17 consider the conviction history of the applicant, including any inquiry about conviction

18 history on any employment application, until after the employer has made a conditional offer

19 of employment to the applicant.”). Thus, the “not an applicant” argument applies to every

20 member of the Fair Chance Class, and does not render Plaintiff atypical.

21       Third, this argument will ultimately fail on the merits, because as someone who
22 received a conditional offer of employment, Plaintiff and Fair Chance Class members are

23 “applicants” as that term is defined by the FEHA. 2 C.C.R. § 11017.1(j)(1) (“‘Applicant’

24 includes… individuals who have been conditionally offered employment[.]”); Carrillo Decl.

25 ¶¶ 7-8, 16, Ex. I (“[Y]our conditional offer of employment… has been withdrawn.”).

5 E.       Commonality And Predominance Is Satisfied for the Fair Chance Class
6          The common question predominating the Fair Chance Class is whether Vons
7 maintained and applied a common practice of denying applicants based on conviction history

8 without conducting any compliant individualized assessment. Here, there is no evidence that

9 Vons individually assessed Plaintiff or any members of the Fair Chance Class. Vons argues
10 that its SOP requires individualized assessments.1 Specifically, Ms. Jones, a “HR

11 Compliance Analyst,” states in her declaration that, “Each applicant with a criminal history is

12 adjudicated using Vons’ Standard Operating Procedures (‘SOP’).” Declaration of Courtney

13 Jones, Dkt. No. 56-12 (“Jones Decl.”), ¶ 5. Ms. Jones claims, “While ‘Albertsons’ appears at

14 the top of the document, the [sic] I use this SOP to adjudicate background checks for Vons

15 applicants.” Id. But a written SOP does not resolve the issue. The question is what Vons

16 actually did in carrying out individualized assessments, and whether that complied with the

17 Fair Chance Act. Other than an unsupported, self-serving declaration based on hearsay, Vons

18 proffers no evidence showing that it conducted an individualized assessment for Plaintiff or

19 anyone else.

20         In the Ninth Circuit, “a plaintiff must merely demonstrate by a preponderance of the
21 evidence that a common question of law or fact exists—an issue that is capable of class-wide

22 resolution.” Van v. LLR, Inc., 61 F.4th 1053, 1066–67 (9th Cir. 2023) (citing Olean, 31 F.4th

23 at 664–65 (en banc). “If the plaintiff demonstrates that class issues exist, the defendant must

24 invoke individualized issues and provide sufficient evidence that the individualized issues bar

25 recovery on at least some claims, thus raising the spectre of class-member-by-class-member

26 adjudication of the issue.” Id. (citation omitted) (italics added). In the Ninth Circuit, courts

27 “do not consider ... defenses that [the defendant] might advance or for which it has presented

20 F.    The Proposed Injunctive Classes Are Proper Under Rule 23(b)(2).
21       Vons does not oppose class certification under Rule 23(b)(2). Rule 23(b)(2) applies
22 when the defendant has acted on grounds generally applicable to the class, making final

23 injunctive relief appropriate for the class as a whole. Parsons, 754 F.3d at 686-89. That

24 standard is satisfied here. Plaintiff seeks uniform injunctive relief requiring Vons to cease

25 using Megan’s Law Website information for employment purposes and to implement and

26 carry out a compliant Fair Chance Act assessment process. Complaint, Prayer ¶¶ 6-8; Motion

27 at 25-26. Those injunctions would apply equally to all class members and future applicants.
