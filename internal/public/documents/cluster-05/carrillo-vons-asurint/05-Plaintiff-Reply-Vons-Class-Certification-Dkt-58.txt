INTRODUCTION
Who wants to live in a world with no forgiveness, no redemption, and no second 
chances? Apparently, Vons does. In its Opposition Vons does not deny that it has a common 
practice of denying employment to applicants with SOR information in their background 
reports. Instead, it argues that it relies on SOR information to “protect those at risk.” But this
is a merits argument, and it commonly applies to every Megan’s Law Class member. Vons’ 
acknowledged practice of relying on SOR information in employment decisions shows the 
Megan’s Law Class is appropriate for class certification. Similarly, with respect to the Fair 
Chance Class Vons offers zero evidence of any individualized assessment it conducted, for 
Plaintiff or any of the other 901 Fair Chance Class members. As such, Vons fails to satisfy its 
burden of proof to show any individualized issue bars certification of the Fair Chance Class.
Vons’ Opposition does not identify a genuine Rule 23 defect. Instead, it largely sets 
forth merits defenses and ad hominem attacks on Plaintiff as purported class-certification 
arguments. But Rule 23 asks whether Plaintiff’s claims under the Megan’s Law and Fair 
Chance Act are cohesive enough to be tried through common proof—not whether Vons has 
assembled inflammatory facts it hopes to use against Plaintiff. Vons has already identified 
the members of the Classes, and Plaintiff accurately reflects the groups he seeks to represent 
– those who were denied employment as a result of SOR information in their background 
reports without any individualized assessment of their fitness for the position. Accordingly, 
Plaintiff respectfully requests the Court grant class certification.

B. Typicality Is Satisfied For The Megan’s Law Class
“Typicality is satisfied when the representative’s claims are reasonably coextensive 
with those of absent class members, even if they are not substantially identical.” DZ Rsrv. v. 
Meta Platforms, Inc., 96 F.4th 1223, 1238 (9th Cir. 2024), cert. denied, 145 S. Ct. 1051 
(2025). A unique defense defeats typicality only when there is a danger that the named 
plaintiff will become preoccupied with that defense to the detriment of the class. Id. at 1238-
39. Vons argues Plaintiff is atypical because he disclosed his conviction, and “Vons will 
show that even if Plaintiff had never appeared on the MLW, the company would have 
initiated the adverse action process based solely on his own disclosure of the underlying 
conviction.” Opp. at 24:13-16 (bold in original). Vons’ argument is irrelevant here. Whether 
or not Plaintiff disclosed his conviction does not free Vons from liability under the Megan’s 
Law. The Megan’s Law does not require the SOR information be the “but-for cause of the 
employment decision.” Opp. at 24:17-19; Cal. Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)(E) (prohibiting “use 
of any information that is disclosed” in Megan’s Law Website). The plain language of the 
statute provides that the “use of any” SOR information in employment decisions is 
prohibited, plain and simple. The record shows that Vons had a common practice of using
SOR information in denying employment to Plaintiff and more than 40 other persons.
Declaration of Raymond Y. Kim in support of Motion for Class Certification, Dkt. No. 55-1

Vons’ decision to withdraw its conditional employment offer was “influenced in whole or in 
part by” the SOR information in their background reports. Carrillo Decl., ¶ 16, Ex. I; Kim 
Decl., ¶ 18. The Megan’s Law claim is not defeated merely because Plaintiff truthfully 
disclosed his conviction history; the Adverse Action Notice itself states the decision was

C. Plaintiff Is Typical Of The Fair Chance Class
Vons argues that Plaintiff is not typical of the Fair Chance Class on the grounds that 
“he was not an applicant at the time Vons conducted its individualized assessment and 
terminated his employment.” Opp. at 24:28-25:2. As a threshold matter, this is a merits issue
that has no bearing at this class certification stage. Olean Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v. 
Bumble Bee Foods LLC, 31 F.4th 651, 667 (9th Cir. 2022) (“[A] district court cannot decline 
certification merely because it considers plaintiffs’ evidence relating to the common question 
to be unpersuasive and unlikely to succeed in carrying the plaintiffs’ burden of proof on that 
issue.”) (citation omitted). Second, Vons’ “not an applicant” argument applies equally to 
everyone in the Fair Chance Class, because they all, like Plaintiff, received a conditional job 
offer pending the background investigation, pursuant to California law. See Cal. Gov. Code 
12952 (“[I]t is an unlawful employment practice for an employer…: (2) To inquire into or 
consider the conviction history of the applicant, including any inquiry about conviction 
history on any employment application, until after the employer has made a conditional offer 
of employment to the applicant.”). Thus, the “not an applicant” argument applies to every 
member of the Fair Chance Class, and does not render Plaintiff atypical. 
Third, this argument will ultimately fail on the merits, because as someone who 
received a conditional offer of employment, Plaintiff and Fair Chance Class members are
“applicants” as that term is defined by the FEHA. 2 C.C.R. § 11017.1(j)(1) (“‘Applicant’
includes… individuals who have been conditionally offered employment[.]”); Carrillo Decl. 
¶¶ 7-8, 16, Ex. I (“[Y]our conditional offer of employment… has been withdrawn.”).

F. The Proposed Injunctive Classes Are Proper Under Rule 23(b)(2).
Vons does not oppose class certification under Rule 23(b)(2). Rule 23(b)(2) applies 
when the defendant has acted on grounds generally applicable to the class, making final 
injunctive relief appropriate for the class as a whole. Parsons, 754 F.3d at 686-89. That
