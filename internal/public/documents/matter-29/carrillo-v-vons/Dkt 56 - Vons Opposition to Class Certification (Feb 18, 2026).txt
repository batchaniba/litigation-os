                               1 I.      INTRODUCTION
                               2         Plaintiff’s Motion for Class Certification (“Motion”) should be denied. Plaintiff
                               3 brings claims against Defendant The Vons Companies, Inc. (“Vons” or “Defendant”) for
                               4 alleged classwide violations of (1) the California Penal Code (“Megan’s Law”) and (2) the
                               5 California Fair Chance Act (“FCA”). Plaintiff fails to meet his burden under Rule 23 to
                               6 demonstrate these claims are suitable for class treatment.
                               7         Initially, Plaintiff, a former schoolteacher, is demonstrably unfit to serve as a class
                               8 representative (i.e., fiduciary). His federal conviction for possessing child-exploitation
                               9 material, his repeated violations of supervised release, his acknowledged inability to
                              10 exercise sound judgment while under the influence, and his ongoing struggles with
                              11 compliance and sobriety leave no doubt about his unfitness. No class action can proceed
                              12 with a representative whose own conduct, credibility, and continuing criminal issues
                              13 overwhelm the litigation.

                               5 IV.      LEGAL STANDARD FOR CLASS CERTIFICATION
                               6          Class actions are “an exception to the usual rule that litigation is conducted by and
                               7 on behalf of the individual named parties only.” Califano v. Yamasaki, 442 U.S. 682, 700-
                               8 701 (1979). Class actions for damages are considered the “most adventuresome” type of
                               9 class action. Amchem Prods. Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 614-615 (1997); see also Fed.
                              10 R. Civ. Proc. 23(b)(3)(D) (requiring consideration of “the likely difficulties in managing a
                              11 class action”). Issues are “individualized” when members of a proposed class must “present
                              12 evidence that varies from member to member.” Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S.
                              13 442, 453 (2016).
LITTLER MENDELSON, P.C.
501 W. Broadway, Suite 900
San Diego, California 92101




                              14          To obtain class certification under Rule 23(a), Plaintiff bears the burden of
       619.232.0441




                              15 demonstrating, by a preponderance of the evidence, each of the four threshold
                              16 requirements: (1) numerosity; (2) common questions of law or fact; (3) typicality; and (4)
                              17 adequacy of the class representatives and their counsel. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(a)(1)-(4); Olean
                              18 Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v. Bumble Bee Foods LLC, 31 F.4th 651, 664 (9th Cir.
                              19 2022). The Supreme Court has made clear: “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading
                              20 standard.” Wal-Mart v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). Rather, “[a] party seeking class
                              21 certification must affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule.” Id.
                              22          Even if Plaintiff could satisfy Rule 23(a)—and he cannot—it remains his burden to
                              23 show that the case also qualifies for class treatment under Rule 23(b). Dukes, 564 U.S. at
                              24 345-46. Under Rule 23(b)(3), Plaintiff must establish both predominance and superiority.
                              25 The predominance criterion is even more demanding than commonality under Rule 23(a).”
                              26 Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 133 S. Ct. 1426, 1432 (2013). It is not enough for Plaintiff to
                              27 simply point to questions he claims are common—the central question is whether those
                              28 issues are sufficiently cohesive and capable of classwide resolution. Class certification
