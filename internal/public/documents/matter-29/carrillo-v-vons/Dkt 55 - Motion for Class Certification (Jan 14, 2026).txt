 3                                        LEGAL STANDARD
 4         The primary objectives of Rule 23 is the efficient resolution of the claims or
 5 liabilities of many individuals in a single action as well as the elimination of repetitious

 6 litigation and possibly inconsistent adjudication. See Califano v. Yamaki, 442 U.S. 682,

 7 700-01 (1979); C. Wright, A.Miller & M. Kane, Federal Prac. & Proc. Civil 2d at § 1754

 8 (1986). District courts are afforded broad discretion in determining whether an action

 9 should be certified. Montgomery v. Rumsfield, 572 F.2d 250, 255 (9th Cir. 1978). Rule

10 23 outlines a two-step process for determining whether class certification is appropriate.

11 First, Rule 23(a) sets forth four conjunctive prerequisites that must be met for any class:

12 (1) the class is so numerous that joinder of all members is impracticable, (2) there are

13 questions of law or fact common to the class, (3) the claims or defenses of the

14 representative parties are typical of the claims or defenses of the class, and (4) the

15 representative parties will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the class. See Fed.

16 R. Civ. P. 23(a); Hanon v. Dataproducts Corp., 976 F.2d 497, 508 (9th Cir. 1992).

17         “In determining the propriety of a class action, the question is not whether the
18 plaintiff or plaintiffs have stated a cause of action or will prevail on the merits, but rather

19 whether the requirements of Rule 23 are met.” Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S.

20 156, 178 (U.S. 1974) (emphasis added). “[Plaintiff]t is well established that for purposes

21 of class certification, the moving party does not need to establish a likelihood of

22 prevailing on the merits.” McKenzie v. Fed. Express Corp., 275 F.R.D. 290, 297 (C.D.

23 Cal. 2011). “Sometimes the issues are plain enough from the pleadings to determine

24 whether the interests of the absent parties are fairly encompassed within the named

25 plaintiff’s claim…” General Telephone Co. of Sw. v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 160 (1982).

26 “[Plaintiff]t remains relatively clear an ultimate adjudication on the merits of plaintiffs’

27 claims is inappropriate, and any inquiry into the merits must be strictly limited to

28 evaluating plaintiffs’ allegations to determine whether they satisfy Rule 23.” Lee v.

13        1.     The Megan’s Law Class Satisfies Rule 23(a)’s Requirements
14               a.    Numerosity
15        Rule 23(a)(l) requires that the class be “so numerous that joinder of all members is
16 impracticable.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(a)(l). “‘Impracticability’ does not mean

17 ‘impossibility,’ but only the difficulty or inconvenience of joining all members of the

18 class.’” Jane Doe v. Nielsen, 357 F. Supp. 3d 972,988 (N.D. Cal. 2018) (quoting Harris

19 v. Palm Springs Alpine Estates, Inc., 329 F.2d 909, 913-914 (9th Cir. 1964)). “In

20 general, courts find the numerosity requirement satisfied when a class includes at least 40

21 members.” Holly v. Alta Newport Hosp., Inc., 612 F. Supp. 3d 1017, 1027 (C.D. Cal.

22 2020) (citing Rannis v. Recchia, 380 F. App'x 646, 650–51 (9th Cir. 2010)). Here, there

23 are over forty (40) persons who were denied employment as a result of Vons’ use of

24 information disclosed in the Megan’s Law Website. Kim Decl., ¶¶ 14-16. In addition,

25 Vons stipulated that “Vons shall not challenge numerosity with respect to the Megan’s

26 Law Class as defined in paragraph 28 of the Class Action Complaint.” Order on Second

27 Telephonic Discovery Conference, Dkt. No. 51, at 2. As a result, numerosity is satisfied

28 for the Megan’s Law Class.

 1              b.     Commonality
 2        The commonality “requirement is met if there are ‘questions of law and fact
 3 common to the class.’ ‘Where the circumstances of each particular class member vary but

 4 retain a common core of factual or legal issues with the rest of the class, commonality

 5 exists.’” Evon v. L. Offs. of Sidney Mickell, 688 F.3d 1015, 1029 (9th Cir. 2012) (citing

 6 Parra v. Bashas', Inc., 536 F.3d 975, 978–79 (9th Cir.2008)). “So long as there is ‘even

 7 a single common question,’ a would-be class can satisfy the commonality requirement of

 8 Rule 23(a)(2).” Wang v. Chinese Daily News, Inc., 737 F.3d 538, 544 (9th Cir. 2013);

 9 see also Alcantar v. Hobart Service, 800 F.3d 1047, 1052 (9th Cir. 2015); Ellis v. Costco

10 Wholesale Corp., 657 F.3d 970, 981 (9th Cir. 2011) (noting that not all questions of fact

11 and law in the litigation need to be common).

12        In this case, common questions of law and fact exist regarding the Megan’s Law
13 Class. The Megan’s Law mandates the California Department of Justice (“CA DOJ”) to

14 notify the public about specified registered sex offenders. CA Megan’s Law Website,

15 (https://www.meganslaw.ca.gov/AboutMegansLaw/SummaryOfMegansLaw). The

16 Megan’s Law site contains information about persons convicted of specified sex crimes,

17 including their picture, address, physical description and date of birth. Cal. Pen. Code

18 290.46(b). “Although sex offender registration is not considered a form of punishment

19 under the state or federal Constitution, it imposes a ‘substantial’ and ‘onerous’ burden…

20 When it becomes publicly known that a person is a registered sex offender, the person

21 may be at risk of losing employment, and may have difficulty finding a place to live.”

22 People v. Hofsheier, 37 Cal. 4th 1185, 1197 (2006), overruled on other grounds by

23 Johnson v. Dep't of Just., 60 Cal. 4th 871 (2015). Consequently, the Megan’s Law

24 expressly prohibits employers from using SOR information for employment purposes:

25        Except as authorized under paragraph (1) or any other provision of law,
          use of any information that is disclosed pursuant to this section for
26
          purposes relating to any of the following is prohibited:… Employment.

22 absent class members; they need not be substantially identical.” Rodriguez v. Hayes, 591

23 F.3d 1105, 1124 (9th Cir. 2010) (quotation marks omitted). The requirement is “satisfied

24 when each class member’s claim arises from the same course of events, and each class

25 member makes similar legal arguments to prove the defendant’s liability.” Id. (quotation

26 marks omitted). The purpose of the typicality requirement is to assure that the interests of

27 the class representatives align with the interests of the class. Wolin v. Jaguar Land Rover

28 N. Am., LLC, 617 F.3d 1168, 1175 (9th Cir. 2010). The interests and claims of named

16         California’s UCL and FEHA provide for injunctive relief. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code
17   § 17203; Cal. Gov Code § 12965. Rule 23(b)(2) “inquiry does not require an
18   examination of the viability or bases of the class members’ claims for relief, does not
19   require that the issues common to the class satisfy a Rule 23(b)(3)-like predominance
20   test, and does not require a finding that all members of the class have suffered identical
21   injuries.” Parsons v. Ryan, 754 F.3d 657, 688 (9th Cir. 2014). Instead, class certification
22   under Rule 23(b)(2) is appropriate when “the party opposing the class has acted or
23   refused to act on grounds that apply generally to the class, so that final injunctive relief
24   or corresponding declaratory relief is appropriate respecting the class as a whole.” Fed.
25   R. Civ. P. 23 (b)(2). “It is sufficient if class members complain of a pattern or practice
26   that is generally applicable to the class as a whole” and a class may be certified “[e]ven if
27   some class members have not been injured by the challenged practice.” Walters v. Reno,
28   145 F.3d 1032, 1047 (9th Cir. 1998); see Hart v. Colvin, 310 F.R.D. 427, 438 (N.D. Cal.
