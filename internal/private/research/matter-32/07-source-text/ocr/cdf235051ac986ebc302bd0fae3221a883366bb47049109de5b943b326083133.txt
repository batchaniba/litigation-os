Il. LEGAL STANDARD

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests the legal sufficiency of the claims in the
complaint. In deciding a motion to dismiss, the Court must accept all well-pled
factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's
favor. The Court may not resolve factual disputes or weigh evidence at this stage.
The question is simply whether the complaint states a plausible claim for relief.
When government officials assert qualified immunity on a motion to dismiss, the
same standard applies: the court must accept the plaintiff's facts as true and

determine whether those facts, if proven, show a violation of a clearly established

4. Defendants’ Reliance on Competing Facts or “Totality of Circumstances”
Arguments is Premature.

In their motion, Defendants attempt to excuse their actions by asserting a

only as a result of the unlawful search (not in plain view). Plaintiff further alleges
he did not exhibit signs of intoxication and did not fail the field sobriety tests.

true). Determining whose “story” is more credible, or whether the officers”

summary judgment or trial — not a motion to dismiss.

IV. CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs Complaint, read in the light most favorable to him, adequately
pleads that Defendants violated his clearly established constitutional rights. The
factual allegations detail a situation in which officers escalated a welfare check
into an investigatory stop without cause, performed a warrantless search after
their only suspicion had been dispelled, and effectuated an arrest without probable
cause. These facts state a plausible claim for relief under the Fourth Amendment
(unreasonable search and seizure) and the Fourteenth Amendment (abusive

executive conduct), which must be allowed to proceed to discovery.

Defendants’ motion, by contrast, improperly seeks to have the Court resolve

factual disputes and credit the officers’ version of events over the Complaint's
well-pled allegations. That is not the role of a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. At this stage,
the Court must accept Plaintiff's allegations as true and cannot weigh them against
Defendants’ contrary assertions.

Qualified immunity also does not warrant dismissal here. The rights at
issue were clearly established, and no reasonable officer could have believed the
alleged conduct was lawful. The law has long made clear that warrantless
searches require probable cause, that an officer cannot prolong or expand a stop
without new justification, and that an arrest requires at least probable cause of a
crime. Defendants are alleged to have flouted all of these well-known rules.

Accordingly, they are not entitled to qualified immunity at the pleading stage.
