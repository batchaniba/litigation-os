Newanforbi v. California Highway Patrol, et al. — Traditional mandate (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1085, 1086) to obtain a Form I-918, Supplement B certification, or to vacate Captain Serrato’s April 20, 2026 denial and remand under court-defined legal rules.

Petitioner: Brendan Ngehsi Newanforbi Date researched: October 24, 2026 Status: Advocacy memorandum — research synthesis, not a filing
Bottom line — read this first

Lead with controlled remand. Plead direct certification in the alternative.

Penal Code § 679.10(g)(1) says a certifying official “shall certify … when the victim was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity and has been helpful…” The signature is therefore conditionally mandatory — ministerial once the two predicates are found — but the statute assigns the official the job of finding those predicates. No published California decision found in this research has ordered an agency to sign a Supplement B, or held it could not. Direct certification is a first-impression prayer.

The viable writ theory is two-track, in this order of strength:

Track 1 — Strongest · Primary prayer

Vacatur + legally controlled remand

The denial applied the wrong legal standard, ignored the (g)/(h)/(k) rules, relied on a prohibited ground, or rested on materially inaccurate facts — so the court vacates and orders reconsideration under specified legal rules without dictating the outcome. Anchors: Common Cause, Saleeby, CCSO.

Track 2 — High-risk · Alternative prayer

Direct certification

Available only if the court can lawfully find both predicates itself on undisputed or properly found facts and that only one reasonable exercise of discretion remains (CCSO at p. 827; Common Cause’s “no discretion to exercise” exception at p. 446). It collides with federal guidance treating signing as discretionary and with the Supplement B attestation problem (see §5).

Framing rule: treat the two tracks as two levels of the same relief. First identify the findings that would compel certification. Then identify the minimum findings that make the existing denial legally unsustainable and require reconsideration under a court-defined framework. Build the record so the remand order does the work even if direct relief is denied — and invite that fallback expressly, so denial of direct relief does not become denial of the petition.

Deliverable 1

Recommended remedial theory

Theory in one sentence: CHP’s April 20, 2026 denial is reviewable traditional-mandate error — wrong legal standard, prohibited or irrelevant grounds, disregard of the helpfulness presumption, and/or dependence on materially inaccurate facts — and the court should therefore either (a) vacate and remand under binding legal directions, or (b) if it finds both § 679.10 predicates on a proper record and that no reasonable official could deny, order the designated official to sign.

Why § 1085, not § 1094.5

A § 679.10 certification denial involves no statutorily required evidentiary hearing, so the petition is properly under Code of Civil Procedure § 1085 (traditional mandate), not § 1094.5 (administrative mandate). Say so expressly to pre-empt a misclassification attack. Section 1094.5(b), (e), (f) language remains useful by analogy for abuse-of-discretion and remand framing — including its limiting proviso, which the agency will quote: on set-aside the court “may order the reconsideration of the case in light of the court’s opinion and judgment … but the judgment shall not limit or control in any way the discretion legally vested in the respondent” (§ 1094.5(f)). The controlled remand must therefore be drafted as legal rules + issues + findings to be made, not as a disguised direction to certify.

The statute: conditionally mandatory, in two stages

  • Predicate stage (discretionary): the official determines (i) victim of qualifying activity and (ii) helpfulness — past, present, or likely future. The statute does not say “in the official’s sole discretion,” but it assigns the determination to the official, and the federal scheme treats it as discretionary. A court may not simply substitute its view of a genuinely debatable predicate.
  • Consequence stage (ministerial): once the predicates are established — by the agency’s own findings, by undisputed facts, or by a lawful court finding on review — “shall certify” leaves no residual discretion to refuse for other reasons, and § 679.10(k)(3) bars refusal on the listed grounds.
  • What is reviewable: whether the predicate determination was made under the correct legal standard, on relevant evidence, without prohibited grounds or materially inaccurate facts, and with the (h) presumption applied. That is the traditional-mandate attack — not a claim that “shall” alone entitles petitioner to a signature on demand.

The Commission on State Mandates reads the statute the same way in substance: the test-claim statute “mandates the certifying entity to complete a U Visa certification when it has determined in the normal course of existing law enforcement duties that the qualifying criminal activity occurred … and that the victim … has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested.” Index

Binding vs. persuasive — keep the tiers straight

California Supreme Court cases (Common Cause, Saleeby, Western States, Mercer, Sanders) are Binding on the superior court. California Court of Appeal cases (CCSO, Jackson, Espinoza) are Binding on a superior court absent contrary Supreme Court authority. Fifth Circuit / federal district cases (Ordonez Orosco, Yoni) and USCIS guidance are Persuasive only — and critically, they construe federal law, which does not displace a state statutory duty under § 679.10. They are the agency’s best authority, not a jurisdictional bar. USCIS Administrative Appeals Office decisions are non-precedential agency adjudications.

Deliverable 2

Best route to direct certification

Direct certification requires all of the following. The absence of any one defeats it and collapses the remedy to remand.

  1. Both predicates found by the court — qualifying activity (false imprisonment, Pen. Code § 236, or a substantially similar offence under § 679.10(d), including § 146(a) pleaded that way) and helpfulness with the (h) presumption applied — on undisputed facts or facts found after a focused hearing under Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1090–1091.
  2. The “only one choice” showing: “Where only one choice can be a reasonable exercise of discretion, a court may compel an official to make that choice.” California Correctional Supervisors Organization v. Department of Corrections (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 824, 827, citing Bank of Italy v. Johnson (1926) 200 Cal. 1, 31. Verified live This must be proved, not asserted: if any reasonable view of the probable-cause or helpfulness facts supports denial, the test fails.
  3. The Common Cause branch: the entity “has no discretion to exercise” (duty made ministerial by statute), or it “fails to exercise that discretion after being given a reasonable opportunity to do so” (the Reinecke branch). Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 446. Verified live Note the posture candidly: Common Cause reversed an order requiring a particular discretionary programme — it is authority against direct relief where discretion remains.
  4. A truthful attestation is possible: the designated official can truthfully sign the Supplement B, including that the agency investigated the incident / qualifying activity (see the attestation problem in §5 of this memo / Q5 of the research). A court cannot order a false attestation.
  5. Narrow scope: the order directs only completion, signing, and forwarding of the Supplement B with the § 679.10(i)(1) specific details, by a date certain. It does not direct USCIS to grant status and does not decide substantial abuse, admissibility, or any other USCIS element. USCIS has sole jurisdiction over U petitions (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(1)).
The central vulnerability — plead it openly, do not hide it. CHP investigated a DUI by the victim, not a false imprisonment by its own officer. A Supplement B signed by CHP would attest the agency is investigating or prosecuting false imprisonment (8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(2)(i)). Petitioner’s best answer: § 679.10(k)(1) (no current investigation required) + the agency did investigate the underlying incident in the normal course, and the qualifying activity arises from that incident. The agency’s best answer: that reads “investigating or prosecuting [the qualifying activity]” out of the form. The research found this tension unresolved in the authorities. Do not present either reading as settled.

Narrowest findings that could justify direct relief (avoid everything unnecessary): intentional restraint against will + absence of lawful authority (no probable cause, or no Pen. Code § 836 presence and no Veh. Code § 40300.5 trigger) + direct, proximate harm + unrebutted helpfulness presumption + no (k)(3) ground + only-one-reasonable-choice. See §13 for the full findings list.

Do not ask the writ judge to sign the Supplement B personally as an alternative. A judge is a certifying official under 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(2)–(3) and § 679.10(a)(3), (b)(3) — but the writ judge did not investigate or prosecute the officer’s conduct, and a judge-signed certification where the judge is also adjudicator raises attestation and impartiality problems no authority found addresses.

Deliverable 3

Best route to vacatur and mandatory reconsideration

This is the Saleeby remedy, and the safest. In Saleeby v. State Bar (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547, the procedures were deficient because they denied an opportunity to be heard and “sufficient findings to afford review”; findings must be “sufficient to apprise interested parties and the courts of the bases for the administrative action” (id. at pp. 566–567, quoting Topanga Assn. v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 515–517 — findings “conduce the administrative body to draw legally relevant sub-conclusions” and “minimize the likelihood that the agency will randomly leap from evidence to conclusions”). The Court declined to decide the merits — “we have no basis on which to evaluate the bar’s determination” — remanded for further consideration, and stated: “We express no view on the merits of petitioner’s claim” (id. at p. 568). The peremptory writ commanded rule reformulation, a reasonable opportunity to be heard, and sufficient findings (id. at p. 575). Verified live

What the remand should require CHP to do

  • Vacate the April 20, 2026 denial and reconsider the original request;
  • Apply the correct definitions of qualifying criminal activity, including § 679.10(c)(18)–(19) and the (d) substantially-similar test (nature and elements);
  • Apply § 679.10(c), (d), (g), (h), (i), and (k) — including the (h) presumption and the (k)(1)–(3) prohibitions;
  • Consider the authenticated recording and other properly admitted evidence that was before the agency or properly before the court;
  • Correct any material factual error the court identifies with particularity;
  • Not treat absence of prosecution, conviction, or officer discipline as dispositive — § 679.10(k)(1)–(2) forecloses that;
  • Not rely on any § 679.10(k)(3) ground: (A) criminal history, (B) immigration history, (C) gang membership/affiliation, (D) belief USCIS will not approve, (E) open case with another entity, (F) extent of harm, (G) inability to produce a crime report, (H) cooperation/refusal in a separate case;
  • Consider helpfulness separately from qualifying victimhood;
  • If certification is again denied, provide the written explanation § 679.10(i)(2) requires (see the scope caution in §4); and
  • File a verified statement of compliance by a date certain (see the drafting caution in §15 — no CCP section verified in this research itself authorises a post-judgment compliance return; build it into the judgment).
Why this does not “dictate the result.” Forbidding reliance on a prohibited ground or a legally erroneous probable-cause theory is not controlling discretion — it is stating the law the discretion must be exercised under, which Common Cause expressly permits: mandamus lies “to compel an official both to exercise his discretion … and to exercise it under a proper interpretation of the applicable law” (49 Cal.3d at pp. 442–443). Requiring a factual finding (“you must find no probable cause”) is controlling discretion — unless the direct-certification test in §2 is met. Keep the remand on the lawful side of that line.

Federal framework — what certification is and is not

  • Statute/regulation: 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(U); certification under 8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(1); 8 C.F.R. § 214.14 (Cornell LII text Verified live). “Victim” = direct and proximate harm (§ 214.14(a)(14)); “any similar activity” = nature and elements substantially similar (§ 214.14(a)(9)).
  • No conviction, prosecution, or ongoing investigation is required under either federal or California law. USCIS guidance: certification may issue at various stages, including before charges.
  • Both sides of the determination: the Supplement B attests to both victimhood and helpfulness. USCIS guidance states certifying agencies “may be in the best position to determine if a qualifying crime has taken place” and that “[w]hether a certifying agency or official signs the certification is under the authority of the agency or official” (USCIS U Visa Law Enforcement Resource Guide Index). USCIS then “determines, in its sole discretion, the credibility of and weight given to all of the evidence, including the Supplement B” (8 U.S.C. § 1184(p)(4)). The agency’s determination is necessary but not sufficient — a point to state expressly to defuse pre-emption objections: ordering certification is not ordering USCIS to grant status.
  • State court’s role, in two senses: (1) Mandate review — the superior court necessarily decides, as a predicate to review, whether the agency’s qualifying-activity determination was legally erroneous, unsupported, or based on prohibited grounds. Ordinary judicial review; does not bind USCIS. (2) Judge as certifier — theoretically available, but not recommended here for the attestation reasons in §2.
Deliverable 5

Element-by-element false-imprisonment analysis

The offences

Pen. Code § 236: “False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another.” (Legislative Information Index) CALCRIM No. 1240: the prosecution must prove (1) the defendant intentionally and unlawfully restrained, detained, or confined a person; and (2) the act made that person stay or go somewhere against that person’s will. Felony false imprisonment adds violence, menace, fraud, or deceit (Pen. Code § 237(a)). No movement is required (that distinguishes § 236 from kidnapping, § 207) and no minimum duration — even a brief unlawful, non-consensual detention suffices.

Pen. Code § 146(a): “Every public officer, or person pretending to be a public officer, who, under the pretense or color of any process or other legal authority, does any of the following, without a regular process or other lawful authority, is guilty of a misdemeanor: (a) Arrests any person or detains that person against his or her will…” (Legislative Information Index) Elements: (i) officer (or pretended-officer) status; (ii) under pretense/colour of process or legal authority; (iii) arrest/detention against will; (iv) absence of regular process or other lawful authority. § 146 is a misdemeanour; § 236 is a wobbler.

Can an officer’s unlawful arrest be false imprisonment? Yes — in principle

  • “A police officer who makes an arrest without a warrant and without justification may be held civilly liable for false arrest and imprisonment” (Dragna v. White (1955) 45 Cal.2d 469, 471 Index); “false arrest is but one way of committing a false imprisonment” (Asgari v. City of Los Angeles (1997) 15 Cal.4th 744, 752 fn. 3 Index).
  • The converse immunity confirms the dividing line: Pen. Code § 847(b)(1) gives no civil liability only if “[t]he arrest was lawful, or the peace officer, at the time of the arrest, had reasonable cause to believe the arrest was lawful.” The scheme treats lawfulness / reasonable cause to believe lawfulness as the line — an arrest that is neither is the paradigm false imprisonment. This negative implication is petitioner’s best doctrinal anchor.

The concession that must be made — probable cause defeats the offence

Probable cause — even if later shown debatable — defeats the § 236 / § 146 predicate, if it was objectively reasonable at the time. Probable cause “depends upon the reasonable conclusion to be drawn from the facts known to the arresting officer at the time of the arrest” (Devenpeck v. Alford (2004) 543 U.S. 146, 152–153 Index); the officer’s subjective motive is irrelevant; the question is whether a reasonable officer in the same position would conclude probable cause existed (Espinoza v. Shiomoto Index). Probable cause is “a probability or substantial chance of criminal activity, not an actual showing” (District of Columbia v. Wesby (2018) 583 U.S. 48, 57). Petitioner must therefore show no reasonable officer would have found probable cause — or, for § 146 / Mercer, no regular process and no other lawful authority (no Pen. Code § 836 basis and no Veh. Code § 40300.5 exception). Proof the DUI could not be proved beyond a reasonable doubt, or that charges were dropped, does not establish false imprisonment.

Do not conflate a Fourth Amendment violation with the statutory crime

  • A Fourth Amendment violation turns on objective reasonableness / probable cause at seizure. A § 236 conviction additionally requires intentional, unlawful restraint proved beyond a reasonable doubt; § 146 requires the colour-of-authority + absence-of-lawful-authority elements.
  • A seizure can be unconstitutional without necessarily being a crime by the officer (good-faith mistaken arrest; § 847(b)(1)’s “reasonable cause to believe lawful” zone). Conversely, a seizure can be criminally unlawful in the § 146 sense even if Fourth Amendment analysis would focus on a different defect.
  • Certification standard: petitioner does not need to prove the officer’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. § 679.10 asks whether the victim “was a victim of a qualifying criminal activity” and prescribes no burden of proof; no case found sets one. Argue the standard is the agency’s normal-course determination on a preponderance / reasonable-grounds showing — while candidly flagging the agency will demand more and the court has not ruled. Plead: “on the facts found, the elements are made out, and no lawful authority existed” — not “the officer committed § 236 beyond a reasonable doubt.”

Strongest factual theory: the arrest itself as the crime — intentional custody without probable cause and without an in-presence / § 40300.5 basis. Weakest / distracting facts: the urine cup (no verified authority links it to DUI or impairment — see §6), the initial vehicle-door opening as a lead theory, and any framing that the DUI investigation alone is a qualifying crime. Concede the DUI investigation alone is not; the petition stands or falls on the arrest’s lawfulness.

Deliverable 6

Material-factual-error analysis — probable cause, disciplined

Scope discipline: this is not a DUI suppression memorandum. Every factor below is included only because it advances (a) unlawful restraint, (b) materiality of CHP’s factual error, (c) invalidity of the denial, (d) need for reconsideration, or (e) direct certification. The standard is totality, judged objectively on facts known at arrest. Petitioner must win on both prongs: (1) no probable cause to believe DUI, or (2) probable cause but no § 40300.5 trigger (misdemeanour DUI + no § 836(a)(1) presence).

The legal frame

  • Veh. Code § 40300.5 (Legislative Information Index): in addition to Pen. Code § 836(a)(1), an officer may arrest without a warrant with reasonable cause to believe the person had been driving under the influence when: (a) traffic crash; (b) person observed in or about a vehicle obstructing a roadway; (c) person will not be apprehended unless immediately arrested; (d) person may cause injury to self or damage property unless immediately arrested; (e) person may destroy or conceal evidence unless immediately arrested. § 40300.5 excuses presence, not probable cause — it still requires reasonable cause to believe the person had been driving under the influence. § 40300.6 requires liberal interpretation for arrests within a reasonable time/distance from a traffic accident.
  • Mercer v. DMV (1991) 53 Cal.3d 753 Binding: § 23152 “requires proof of volitional movement of a vehicle”; because Pen. Code § 836 permits a warrantless misdemeanour arrest only if the offence occurs in the officer’s presence, and the officer did not see Mercer’s vehicle move, Mercer was not “lawfully arrested” for licence-revocation purposes (id. at pp. 769–770 Index — re-check before filing). Mercer also states observed movement is not necessary to support a conviction — conviction may rest on circumstantial proof of past movement. That distinction is the battleground: the agency conflates “could a jury find driving circumstantially?” with “was this a lawful in-presence arrest?” Different questions, different answers.
  • Agency’s better § 40300.5 arguments are (e) — alcohol dissipates with time (People v. Schofield (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 968 Index) — and, if petitioner was asleep at the wheel positioned to resume driving, (d). (c) is weak if petitioner was identified, stationary, and asleep — but the agency will argue flight risk once awakened. Each trigger must be proved with pre-arrest facts, not post hoc rationalisation.

Later-acquired evidence cannot retroactively justify the restraint

People v. Sanders (2003) 31 Cal.4th 318 Binding: “an otherwise unlawful search … may not be justified by the circumstance that the suspect was subject to a search condition of which the law enforcement officers were unaware when the search was conducted” (id. at p. 335 Index); “such a search cannot be justified as a parole search, because the officer is not acting pursuant to the conditions of parole” (id. at p. 333 Index). Scope limit — state it openly: Sanders is a search case about an unknown parole search condition, not an arrest/probable-cause case. Its value is analogical + timing: justification is judged by what the officer knew and acted pursuant to at the time.

  • Post-arrest blood result: cannot create pre-arrest probable cause. It can be circumstantial evidence of intoxication around arrest if bridged by expert retrograde extrapolation (cf. Jackson, 189 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1465, 1468). A bare BAC number — no timing, no rising/falling analysis, no test foundation — proves little about PC at arrest and nothing about driving. Conversely, a low/zero later BAC does not alone prove no PC existed. A high BAC helps the agency on “was this person in fact under the influence?” — not on the legal question that matters for the § 236/§ 146 predicate: probable cause and a § 40300.5 trigger before arrest.
  • Warrant obtained after arrest: cannot retroactively make the warrantless arrest lawful (general Fourth Amendment timing principle, Devenpeck). No California case on a post-arrest warrant curing a DUI arrest was verified — plead the principle, not an invented holding.
  • Dispatch parole information learned after arrest: the Sanders analogy is strongest here — unknown status cannot justify the seizure/search after the fact. Caveat: information received before arrest and communicated counts. And parole status alone is not probable cause of DUI; it is at most a search-condition justification that must have been known and acted on.

PAS refusal — Jackson’s precise, narrow use

Veh. Code § 23612(h)–(i) Index: a PAS test “may be used by an officer as a further investigative tool” to establish reasonable cause; the officer must advise of the right to refuse, and that the post-arrest chemical-test obligation is not satisfied by a PAS test. People v. Jackson (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1461, 1466–1469 Verified live reproduction: because § 23612 confers a right to refuse with required advisement, “it makes little sense to grant a right of refusal yet allow the prosecution to admit evidence of that refusal to establish consciousness of guilt” — admission at trial as consciousness of guilt was error (harmless on Jackson’s strong facts). Jackson distinguishes standard FST refusal, which is routinely admitted (Marvin v. DMV (1984) 161 Cal.App.3d 717, 719–720).

Scope — do not overcite Jackson. Jackson is a trial-evidence holding. It does not hold an officer may not consider PAS refusal in the pre-arrest totality. Use it only for: (1) scepticism where PAS refusal is the central or sole inculpatory fact — treating exercise of the right as inculpatory defeats the right; (2) PAS refusal must not be conflated with FST refusal — a PC narrative citing undifferentiated “refusal to cooperate with testing” is legally inaccurate; (3) if no § 23612(i) advisement was given, the refusal was not a knowing exercise of the right and its weight falls further. The agency’s response — objective totality, refusal as one factor, harmless-error affirmance — is credible where other PC facts exist.

Detention certificate (Pen. Code §§ 849.5, 851.6) — what it does not prove

When a person is arrested and released without an accusatory pleading, the event “shall not be deemed an arrest, but a detention only” (§ 849.5), and the agency must issue a certificate describing it as a detention (§ 851.6) Index — verify exact text on Legislative Information before filing. It proves no charges were filed and governs record/disclosure treatment. It does not adjudicate probable cause, prove the arrest unlawful, prove false imprisonment, or erase the detention. Use it only to show the DUI investigation ended without prosecution (consistent with § 679.10(k)(1)) and to rebut “charges prove a crime occurred.” Do not plead it as a finding of unlawful arrest.

The conflict as framed in the brief: CHP Form 202 reportedly states petitioner chose not to perform the walk-and-turn because it was difficult and later relies on poor field-test performance; the recording indicates petitioner expressed willingness to attempt the test and Officer Melano decided not to administer it because the freeway shoulder was uneven. If other PC facts remain independently sufficient, concede the discrepancy alone is immaterial — do not let it taint the petition’s credibility.
  • Join it, don’t rest on it alone. Joined with (a) no observed movement / failed § 40300.5 trigger, (b) the PAS/FST conflation, and (c) any uncommunicated-observation gap, the discrepancy shows a pattern: the stated PC basis was materially inaccurate in a respect the denial relied on. Alone, it supports remand at most; joined, it supports the material-error finding in §14.
  • Can the court resolve it? Yes — if the recording is authenticated, the court can compare Form 202 against the recording and documents without a swearing contest. If authentication or audibility is genuinely disputed, the route is a focused hearing (§7), not denial.
  • Remedy supported: primarily remand (correct the material error and reconsider). It supports direct certification only as one link in the full only-one-choice chain in §2.
  • Precise finding to request: “The April 20, 2026 denial relied on [the specified Form 202 characterisation of the walk-and-turn]. The authenticated recording shows [the specified contrary fact]. That reliance was material because [the specified role the fact played in the stated basis for arrest/custody and in the denial].” Fill the brackets from the authenticated record only — never from assertion.

What counts as “materially inaccurate” in mandate law

No single California mandate case found states a freestanding “materially inaccurate fact = abuse of discretion” rule in those words. Build it from: a finding with no substantial evidentiary support, or a decision resting on a fact the record affirmatively disproves, is arbitrary / “beyond the bounds of reason” (CCSO, p. 827); a decision that ignores considerable relevant evidence before the agency is an abuse (Citizens Assn. for Sensible Development of Bishop Area v. County of Inyo (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 151 Index, citing No Oil and Brentwood Assn. — “an agency may not refuse to consider uncontradicted testimony based upon objective data”); and a decision without findings showing its mode of analysis is unreviewable (Saleeby/Topanga). Tie every alleged inaccuracy to a predicate (probable cause, helpfulness, qualifying crime) and show the denial depends on it.

Abuse of discretion — the four pleadable forms

  • Legal error / wrong standard: denial applies a federal “wholly discretionary” standard instead of § 679.10(g)/(h)/(k), or treats a (k)(3) ground as a reason — “in derogation of the applicable legal standards” (CCSO p. 827) on its face. Failure to apply the correct standard is itself a ground for the writ, without proving the ultimate outcome (Common Cause pp. 442–443).
  • Failure to consider relevant evidence: denial ignores considerable evidence before the agency, or refuses to consider uncontradicted testimony on objective data (Bishop Area, 172 Cal.App.3d 151 Index). Provided the ignored evidence was actually before the agency or properly before the court under (v).
  • No findings / unreviewable reasoning: conclusory denial with no mode of analysis (Saleeby/Topanga).
  • Failure to exercise discretion at all: “While mandamus will not lie to compel governmental officials to exercise their discretionary powers in a particular manner, it will lie to compel them to exercise them in some manner” (Ballard v. Anderson (1971) 4 Cal.3d 873, 884; Sego v. Santa Monica Rent Control Bd. (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 250, 255 Index).

Western States — extra-record evidence, stated precisely

Western States Petroleum Assn. v. Superior Court (1995) 9 Cal.4th 559 Binding Verified live: for quasi-legislative decisions, courts “generally may not consider evidence not contained in the administrative record” (p. 565) and extra-record evidence is “generally not admissible” to show failure to proceed as required by law (pp. 565, 575–576). Preserved exception: “we will continue to allow admission of extra-record evidence in traditional mandamus actions challenging ministerial or informal administrative actions if the facts are in dispute” (p. 576). Narrow quasi-legislative exception: evidence (1) existed before the decision and (2) could not with reasonable diligence have been presented before decision (p. 578). Extra-record evidence “can never be admitted merely to contradict the evidence the administrative agency relied on… or to raise a question regarding the wisdom of that decision” for quasi-legislative decisions (p. 579). Do not cite Western States as if it generally permits extra-record evidence — the agency will quote pp. 565, 575–576 back. Frame the argument as: (a) informal/ministerial action within p. 576; and (b) in the alternative, the evidence existed before the denial and could not have been presented if the agency gave no opportunity to be heard / never disclosed its basis — a Saleeby defect that itself justifies remand for consideration of the evidence.

Credibility and standard of review

  • A writ court can resolve credibility where it takes evidence and makes findings (§§ 1090–1091 presuppose it). It may not disbelieve the return on papers alone and substitute a credibility finding without an evidentiary basis. The agency’s “writ courts cannot resolve credibility” argument is overbroad as stated, correct only in its narrow paper-record form. Ask for the focused hearing; fallback is remand for agency fact-finding on specified issues — not denial.
  • On appeal: trial-court findings reviewed for substantial evidence; the appellate court “may make its own determination when the case involves resolution of questions of law where the facts are undisputed” (CCSO p. 827, quoting Saathoff v. City of San Diego (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 697, 700). Statutory interpretation and application of law to undisputed facts are reviewed de novo (cf. CV Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 279–280 Index). Hearing mechanics: § 1094 (no return → heard on applicant’s papers; return raising only law → court proceeds; § 1088.5 no-triable-issue petitions may be determined by noticed motion) Index.
Five things to keep distinct in briefing: (1) compelling a mandatory duty; (2) ordering reconsideration; (3) directing the agency to apply specified legal rules; (4) the court resolving disputed facts (only via §§ 1090–1091 process); (5) improperly substituting judgment for agency discretion. Every paragraph of the motion should be assignable to (1)–(4). If it reads as (5), rewrite it.

Deliverable 13

Proposed findings for direct certification — all required

  1. Standing / duty / no adequate remedy: petitioner is the victim (or authorised requester under (g)(1)), made a proper request to the correct entity/official, and has no plain, speedy, adequate remedy (§ 1086) — e.g., no other certifying entity with responsibility for the incident; USCIS cannot act without a Supplement B.
  2. Qualifying activity — found, not alleged: on facts found after a focused hearing or on undisputed facts, the elements of § 236 (or a (d) substantially similar offence, including § 146(a) if pleaded that way) are made out — intentional restraint against will and absence of lawful authority (no probable cause, or no § 836 presence + no § 40300.5 trigger, each analysed separately).
  3. Victimhood: direct and proximate harm from that activity (§ 679.10(o)(2); 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(a)(14)) — not merely harm from the DUI investigation or from being a DUI suspect.
  4. Helpfulness: has been / is being / is likely to be helpful to detection/investigation/prosecution of that activity; (h) presumption applied; any claimed rebuttal (specific reasonable request + knowing refusal/failure) found not proved.
  5. No prohibited ground: the denial did not — and the new decision will not — rest on any (k)(3) ground, on a prosecution/conviction/current-investigation requirement ((k)(1)–(2)), or on a belief about USCIS approval ((k)(3)(D)).
  6. Only-one-reasonable-choice: on the found facts, no reasonable official applying the correct (g)/(h)/(k) standards could deny (CCSO p. 827) — and the agency already had a reasonable opportunity to exercise its discretion (Common Cause p. 446, Reinecke branch).
  7. Truthful attestation possible: the designated official can truthfully sign the Supplement B as the form requires, including the agency’s investigation of the incident/qualifying activity. If it cannot be truthfully signed, direct relief against the agency is unavailable — do not order a false attestation.
  8. Scope limit: order directs only completion, signing, and forwarding (with (i)(1) details) by a date certain; it does not direct USCIS, decide substantial abuse/admissibility, or bind future certifications.
Deliverable 14

Proposed findings for remand — the minimum set

  1. Reviewability: the denial is reviewable under § 1085 (not § 1094.5 — no statutorily required hearing); petitioner is beneficially interested with no adequate alternative remedy.
  2. Legal error, with particularity: e.g., denial applied a “wholly discretionary / requires prosecution or ongoing investigation” standard contrary to (g)/(h)/(k)(1); treated a (k)(3) ground as a reason; required a crime report contrary to (k)(3)(G); or failed to apply the (h) presumption / treated absence of a request as non-helpfulness. (Plead only the errors the actual denial letter commits — read it against this list line by line.)
  3. Factual/evidentiary defect, with particularity: denial rests on a materially inaccurate fact on which it depends (specify fact, record cite, outcome-dependence); or ignores considerable relevant evidence before the agency (specify it — Bishop Area); or contains no findings sufficient to show its mode of analysis (Saleeby/Topanga).
  4. Procedural defect, if any: (i)(2) specifics missing where non-cooperation was invoked; no reasonable opportunity to be heard/respond (Saleeby); (f) report or (j) deadline breached.
  5. Remand directions (law, issues, findings — not outcome): vacate; reconsider (i) under the correct (g)/(h)/(k) standards as stated in the judgment, (ii) on the specified issues (qualifying activity incl. substantially-similar analysis; victimhood; helpfulness with presumption; PC/§ 40300.5 analysis where the theory depends on it), (iii) with written findings sufficient to apprise the parties and the court, including (i)(2) specifics if denial is again on non-cooperation, (iv) by a date certain (apply (j) periods by analogy).
  6. Compliance mechanism: verified statement of compliance filed/served by the date certain (new decision + signed Supplement B or new written denial + record relied on); jurisdiction retained solely to determine compliance / enforce the writ; express statement that the court expresses no view on the remand outcome except as stated in the legal directions (Saleeby p. 568 model).
Overreach flags — do not include: directing a future discretionary outcome while claiming discretion remains (contradictory; reversible under Common Cause p. 446 / § 1094.5(f) proviso) · deciding or directing immigration status (USCIS sole jurisdiction, 8 C.F.R. § 214.14(c)(1)) · adjudicating the officer’s criminal guilt, civil false-arrest liability, or § 1983 liability in the writ judgment · ordering an investigation or prosecution of the officer ((k)(1) creates no such duty) · ordering a false Supplement B attestation · binding future certifications for other victims/incidents · retaining jurisdiction to re-decide the remand outcome de novo (retention is for compliance only).

Alternative 1 — Judgment ordering certification

The Court finds, on [the undisputed facts / the facts found after hearing on ___], that: (1) petitioner was intentionally restrained against his will by respondents’ officers through [commands / handcuffing / transportation / custody]; (2) the restraint lacked lawful authority because [no probable cause existed on the facts known to the arresting officer before arrest / no offence was committed in the officer’s presence within Penal Code section 836 and no Vehicle Code section 40300.5 circumstance was established on pre-arrest facts]; (3) the conduct therefore constitutes false imprisonment within Penal Code sections 236 and 679.10(c)(19) [and/or unlawful criminal restraint within section 679.10(c)(18) / conduct substantially similar thereto within section 679.10(d)]; (4) petitioner suffered direct and proximate harm from that activity; (5) petitioner has been, is being, or is likely to be helpful within section 679.10(g)(1), the presumption of section 679.10(h) applies and has not been rebutted by proof of a specific reasonable request for cooperation that petitioner refused or failed to meet; and (6) on these findings, no reasonable certifying official applying section 679.10(g), (h), and (k) could deny certification.

Respondent shall file and serve a verified statement of compliance, attaching the completed certification as forwarded, no later than [date].
