## 1. Executive judgment

The opposition should not try to prove that every claim in the FAC is correct. Its highest-value objective is to prevent a pleading-stage dismissal **with prejudice**, preserve the strongest Fourth Amendment theories, stop Defendants from converting disputed police narratives into established facts, and present a concrete, credible amendment proffer for defects that the existing record now makes undeniable.

The case is not strongest as a global “the officers invented everything” case. The produced warrant affidavit, the plaintiff-created transcript, and the 0.073 g% ethanol result create substantial adverse evidence. The case is stronger when broken into time-specific intrusions:

1. opening the passenger door;
2. reaching into the vehicle and handling the cup;
3. continuing the detention and conducting tests after the cup was identified as urine;
4. making the custodial arrest;
5. seeking a blood warrant through a separate affidavit; and
6. using the resulting documents in later administrative and custodial processes.

### The single most important strategic choice

Lead with the distinction between **what the Court may assume at Rule 12** and **what Plaintiff can responsibly prove or amend under Rule 11**:

- At Rule 12, the Court generally accepts specific factual allegations and cannot adopt contested factual recitations in an incorporated police document merely because Defendants prefer them. *Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.*, 899 F.3d 988, 998–1003 (9th Cir. 2018); *Grier v. Finjan Holdings, Inc.*, 58 F.4th 1048, 1051 n.1 (9th Cir. 2023).
- For amendment and ultimate proof, Plaintiff cannot ignore the actual warrant affidavit, transcript, form labels, toxicology, or prior litigation. The opposition should concede clear mistakes and explain exactly what can be corrected.

That two-layer approach is stronger than either extreme: it avoids allowing the defense to win factual disputes on a motion to dismiss, and it avoids asking the Court to credit assertions the preserved sources no longer support.

### Material that should affirmatively drive the opposition

The following analysis advances Plaintiff's burden without supplying a new defense:

1. **Rule 12 document discipline:** ECF 22 cannot convert challenged police observations into established facts.
2. **Temporal segmentation:** Entry, cup handling, continued detention, arrest, and warrant procurement require separate justifications based on knowledge at each time.
3. **No retroactivity:** Facts learned after an intrusion cannot justify it retroactively; post-arrest conduct and later toxicology cannot establish the earlier arrest.
4. **Motion-specific deficiencies:** Defendants must identify the exception, authority, defendant, and accepted allegation supporting each requested dismissal.
5. **Rule 12(f):** Whittlestone defeats use of Rule 12(f) merely to strike a damages demand as unavailable.
6. **Specific leave to amend:** Correcting document identity, labels, duration, actor attribution, and search particularity demonstrates that amendment is not categorically futile.
7. **No dismissal through adverse inference:** Emergency aid, probable cause, materiality, causation, and qualified immunity cannot be resolved by selecting Defendants' version of disputed facts.

**Tier 1 — Use affirmatively:** Khoja/Grier, separate-intrusion analysis, Ngumezi's search holding, disputed probable-cause facts, the officer's statement, the one-second refusal-to-arrest-command sequence when properly in the record, Whittlestone, and a focused amendment proffer.

**Tier 2 — Use only to answer ECF 22:** emergency aid, qualified immunity, no-charge fabrication, punitive damages, pro se fees, and prior litigation as relevant to futility.

**Tier 3 — Keep internal unless triggered:** toxicology, adverse transcript passages, the warrant affidavit's full adverse remainder, likely materiality outcome, alternative automobile-search exceptions, unraised protected-activity/Nieves defects, Rule 41/preclusion, downstream damages causation, and voluntary claim abandonment.

## 5. The governing Rule 12 framework

The defense motion repeatedly converts its account of the incident into fact—for example, “Plaintiff unconscious,” “strong smell of alcohol,” failed sobriety tests, and probable cause—and then asks the Court to dismiss because those facts justify every intrusion. The response should force the Court to separate four questions:

1. Is the matter actually alleged in the FAC?
2. Is the matter contained only in a challenged police document?
3. Is the document properly considered, and for what purpose?
4. Is the asserted content legally operative, undisputed, or merely a contested factual recital?

In *Khoja*, the Ninth Circuit warned that judicial notice and incorporation cannot be used to resolve disputed facts against well-pleaded allegations. A document may be considered because the complaint necessarily relies on it without making every disputed assertion in it true. The Ninth Circuit reiterated the point in *Grier*: a non-legally-operative document's competing factual recitation does not automatically trump a specific complaint allegation.

This doctrine is not a license to disregard exhibits. A court may reject allegations contradicted by properly considered material, and conclusory allegations receive no presumption. The opposition therefore should use a precise comparison:

| Issue | FAC allegation to be accepted if well pleaded | Document proposition Defendants may not automatically establish | Correction Plaintiff must make |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wake-up / entry | FAC says Plaintiff remained asleep; later firsthand correction says knocks woke him before the 00:56:40 window command | Defense may use the FAC's existing allegation to strengthen emergency aid | Do not silently rewrite the FAC; request leave to plead the corrected three-second compliance sequence |
| Speech | Extended coherent speech; ability to spell name and discuss details | DS 367 characterization of slurred speech | Quote and cite only after original-video verification |
| Orientation | Later correct identification of Livermore/signage | Report's global “very confused and disoriented” characterization | Acknowledge any initial “parking lot” or groggy response |
| Cup | Officer identified handled liquid as urine | Precontact visual suspicion of alcohol | Do not say urine identification retroactively erased all suspicion |
| Odor | Plaintiff disputes odor | Officer's claimed odor as established truth | Do not say recording silence proves absence of odor |
| FSTs | Some tests affected or omitted because of terrain | General report conclusion that tests were failed | Plead each test, instruction, response, physical condition, and camera limitation |
| Warrant | Plaintiff alleges misleading warrant procurement | DS 367/report treated as the warrant affidavit | Replace with the actual affidavit and exact corrections |

### Proposed Rule 12 thesis

> The Court may consider what the officers wrote without accepting the truth of the observations the FAC specifically challenges. The motion's probable-cause theory depends on resolving contested observations—odor, speech, orientation, and test performance—in Defendants' favor. That is not a permissible Rule 12 inference. Plaintiff separately acknowledges discrete pleading errors and requests leave to correct them; those corrections do not authorize the Court to adjudicate every remaining factual dispute from police paperwork.

### Limits of this thesis

The Court can still dismiss a claim if the FAC's own facts establish a defense, the contrary allegation is conclusory or implausible, or qualified immunity applies even under Plaintiff's version. The opposition must therefore pair *Khoja* with claim-specific analysis, not present it as a universal answer.

## 6. Event-stage Fourth Amendment analysis

### Stage A — Approach and welfare check

Officers could lawfully approach a vehicle on the freeway shoulder, look through windows from a lawful vantage point, knock, speak, and attempt a welfare check without converting every observation into a search. Plaintiff should not challenge the approach itself.

### Stage B — Opening the passenger door

Opening a vehicle door and intruding into the interior is a Fourth Amendment search. *United States v. Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d 1285, 1288–89 (9th Cir. 2020), held that reasonable suspicion for a traffic stop did not itself authorize an officer to open a car door and lean inside absent probable cause or another particularized justification.

The defense's strongest answer is emergency aid. The current Ninth Circuit model instruction states two requirements: objectively reasonable grounds at the time to believe an emergency created an immediate need to protect against serious harm, and a scope and manner reasonable to meet that need. The exception is narrow and evaluated under the totality. See *Bonivert v. City of Clarkston*, 883 F.3d 865, 877 (9th Cir. 2018); *Sheehan v. City & County of San Francisco*, 743 F.3d 1211, 1221 (9th Cir. 2014), rev'd in part on other grounds, 575 U.S. 600 (2015).

**Plaintiff's best position under the present FAC:** The pleading alleges a normal resting posture, intact vehicle, no collision/fire/medical indicator, and pre-entry words directed at a rental car and suspected cup rather than a medical emergency. At Rule 12, Defendants cannot automatically convert “asleep” into “unconscious and medically endangered.”

**Corrected merits account and amendment need:** The Matter 20 reconciliation records that knocking woke Plaintiff, he heard “Open the window” at 00:56:40, and Melano opened the door at 00:56:43 before Plaintiff had a meaningful opportunity to comply. The transcript proves the three-second interval; Plaintiff's recollection supplies the wake-up fact. FAC paragraph 16 says he remained asleep, so the opposition cannot present the correction as though already pleaded. The high-value move is a specific alternative amendment proffer, not defense of a known factual conflict.

**Defense and QI risk:** Officers knocked for approximately 36 seconds, and an apparently unresponsive person in the driver's seat on an interstate shoulder after midnight presents a plausible safety concern. Defendants will argue they need not wait after repeated knocking. Plaintiff's answer is narrower: once the officer issued a window command to an occupant who had awakened, only three seconds elapsed before physical entry, and the first express welfare question came afterward. *Ngumezi* involved an awake driver at a gas station and no emergency, so qualified immunity remains substantial.

**Facts that decide the issue:** exact wake-up moment; visible movement or response before 00:56:43; whether Plaintiff moved toward the window control; number and force of knocks; breathing; window position; traffic danger; posture; officer statements; whether Melano only opened the door or leaned/reached in; and whether the scope remained tied to aid after Plaintiff responded.

### Stage C — Handling and smelling the cup

The FAC alleges Melano reached into the vehicle, seized the cup, smelled it, and examined its contents. Seeing an ambiguous cup from outside is not itself authority for a physical interior search. Defendants must identify the doctrine they rely on: emergency aid, plain view, automobile exception, consent, protective search, or some combination.

**Plaintiff's best position:** The motion improperly collapses visual suspicion into blanket authority to enter and manipulate property. If the cup's incriminating nature was not immediately apparent, plain view alone would not justify seizure. The alleged pre-entry statement “I think it's booze” expresses uncertainty. Once the handled cup was identified as urine, that particular cup suspicion ended.

**Defense risk:** A cup appearing to contain alcohol beside a person in the driver's seat on a freeway shoulder may establish probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of an open-container or DUI offense, particularly when combined with the person's condition and any odor. The automobile exception may therefore justify the cup seizure if the underlying observations are credited. The opposition must not imply that a clear cup of urine was obviously identifiable as urine from outside.

### Stage D — Continued detention and field sobriety investigation

Reasonable suspicion can justify a temporary DUI investigation even if probable cause is not yet present. The cup's identification as urine addresses the cup hypothesis; it does not automatically eliminate suspicion arising from the freeway location, sleep, possible recent driving, answers, party wristband/travel, claimed odor, or physical performance.

The key question is whether reasonable suspicion persisted and whether the duration and methods remained reasonably related to the investigation. The FAC should not use a global “suspicion dissipated” conclusion. It should plead what each officer knew at each point and exactly which fact allegedly dissipated which suspicion.

### Stage E — Arrest

Probable cause exists when reasonably trustworthy facts known at the time create a fair probability that the person committed a crime. It is a totality inquiry and “not a high bar.” *District of Columbia v. Wesby*, 583 U.S. 48, 57–61 (2018). Innocent explanations need not be ruled out, and the officer's subjective offense theory does not control if objective probable cause existed for an offense. *Devenpeck v. Alford*, 543 U.S. 146, 152–55 (2004).

**Pleading-stage response:** The motion's probable-cause case depends heavily on contested factual recitations: alcohol odor, slurred speech, disorientation, and unsuccessful tests. The FAC pleads concrete contrary facts about recorded speech, cup identification, later orientation, terrain, and the absence of new evidence between PAS refusal and arrest. Under *Khoja*, the Court should not decide those disputes by accepting the police narratives.

**Merits and QI assessment:** The surviving totality remains significant: Plaintiff was asleep in the driver's seat on an interstate shoulder after driving from an Oakland party; reportedly wore a 21+ party wristband; initially gave a confused location answer; allegedly performed tests contrary to instructions; and allegedly refused post-arrest chemical testing. The 0.073 result, though later and not part of the arrest knowledge, weakens an inference that the odor account was invented. Even if actual probable cause is disputed, a court may find arguable probable cause and qualified immunity.

**PAS refusal:** Refusal of consent cannot itself establish probable cause or reasonable suspicion. See *United States v. Prescott*, 581 F.2d 1343, 1351 (9th Cir. 1978); *United States v. Krupa*, 658 F.3d 1174, 1177–78 (9th Cir. 2011). But an officer may decide to arrest after a refusal if independent facts already establish probable cause. The transcript places the arrest command one second after the final refusal and handcuffing 18 seconds after it, with no new spoken observation in between. That sequence and the officer's statement are evidence of decision sequence and possible motive; they are not a substitute for the objective probable-cause analysis.

### Stage F — Vehicle searches after the cup

The transcript materially changes the prior issue framing. A bracketed entry depicts Melano opening the rear/back-seat passenger door and searching at 00:59:46 while Meza-Gonzalez escorts Plaintiff away. At 01:00:06 the transcript depicts Melano approaching with an empty can; at 01:00:09 he says, “Mike's Hard Lemonade. Source alcohol.” The original video must verify the search, the can's location and visibility, whether it was empty, what was moved, and how it was obtained.

This sequence cuts both ways. A lawfully observed can can strengthen suspicion from discovery forward. But the can cannot bootstrap the legality of the search that produced it, and the transcript does not report parole status until dispatch at 01:11:42—11:56 after the alleged rear-seat search and after the arrest. Later statements at 01:18:34 and 01:23:20 invoke parole and gun-safety rationales for continued searching; those rationales must be confined to conduct occurring after the officers learned the status.

The existing FAC's generalized “continuing warrantless search” remains too vague. An amendment should separate at least:

1. the 00:56:43 door opening;
2. the 00:58:49–00:59:07 cup handling;
3. the alleged 00:59:46 rear-seat search and can discovery;
4. any post-01:11:42 parole-based search; and

For each act, plead the area, object, actor, time, then-known facts, asserted exception, and injury. Do not allege that the urine cup was the only alcohol-related object. Do not concede that later parole knowledge authorized the earlier search.

## 7. Count One — Judicial deception

### Elements

A civil judicial-deception claim requires a deliberate or reckless false statement or omission, material to the probable-cause determination, in a warrant submission. Materiality asks whether the judge would have issued the warrant after false statements are removed and material omissions restored. *Chism v. Washington State*, 661 F.3d 380, 386–89 (9th Cir. 2011); Ninth Circuit Model Civil Jury Instruction 9.19 (2026).

### Why the current claim is vulnerable

1. The FAC identifies the DS 367 and arrest report as the warrant basis, while the produced packet contains a separate warrant affidavit.
2. The “Beverage Present” premise is a form-label error.
3. Recording silence is not nonconclusory proof that odor was fabricated.
4. The affidavit already includes Plaintiff's denial of drinking, so that fact is not an omission.
5. The FAC's corrected-affidavit paragraph removes adverse allegations without actually addressing them.
6. Melano did not sign the affidavit; any liability requires his own deliberate/reckless contribution and causation, not group pleading.

### Actual affidavit: required corrected-affidavit model

|---|---|---|
| Tesla on I-580 shoulder; Plaintiff asleep in driver's seat | Retain | Supports welfare concern and recent vehicle operation |
| Orange 21+ wristband | Retain unless video/source disproves | Supports party/alcohol context, though weak alone |
| Officers knocked/woke Plaintiff | Retain knocking; clarify that Plaintiff says the knocks woke him before the window command, while the transcript does not fix the exact wake second; verify visible conduct from video | Relevant to emergency entry, alertness, and the FAC inconsistency |
| Confused/disoriented; said he was in a parking lot | Separate exact words from officer characterization; retain any initial mistaken response | Significant support for impairment unless context neutralizes it |
| Odor from vehicle | Disputed; cup urine does not necessarily eliminate simultaneous alcohol odor | Significant if retained |
| Drove from Oakland party | Retain if admitted | Supports recent driving and alcohol context |
| Denied drinking | Retain; already included | Supports Plaintiff but does not eliminate probable cause |
| Odor from breath/person | Dispute only with a nonconclusory basis; video silence alone is insufficient | Significant if retained |

### Evidence-stage conclusion

On the present preserved record, the corrected affidavit likely still contains enough to create a substantial probable-cause basis: recent driving from a party, sleep on an interstate shoulder, an initial mistaken-location answer, claimed odor from breath/person, reported test-performance issues, and alleged chemical-test refusal. The urine omission weakens the vehicle-odor/cup story but does not erase the independent assertions. That makes materiality the central barrier.

### Best opposition use

- Argue that ECF 22 cannot obtain dismissal by treating challenged warrant assertions as true.
- Acknowledge that the present claim identifies the wrong documents and needs amendment.
- Provide a proposed amendment based on the actual affidavit—not an unverified global assertion that the corrected affidavit lacks probable cause.
- Preserve an intentional-deception theory only if original-video and source review provides direct, particular facts showing one or more material affidavit statements were knowingly or recklessly false.
- Do not allow weakness in Count 1 to consume the independent entry, cup-search, or pre-warrant arrest theories.

## 8. Count Two — Deliberate fabrication

The Ninth Circuit states that a plaintiff must prove deliberate fabrication and that the fabrication caused a deprivation of liberty. *Spencer v. Peters*, 857 F.3d 789, 798 (9th Cir. 2017). Carelessness, tone disputes, minor inaccuracies, and fabrication that caused no injury are insufficient. *Caldwell v. City & County of San Francisco*, 889 F.3d 1105, 1115–16 (9th Cir. 2018), explains that conviction is unnecessary and being criminally charged can be enough. The current Ninth Circuit model instruction includes an “other deprivation of liberty” bracket but emphasizes the established core right against being subjected to criminal charges through fabricated evidence.

### Defense overstatement and Plaintiff's real problem

Defendants overstate the law if they contend that no conviction always defeats a fabrication claim. But the FAC alleges **no criminal charge**, and its asserted injuries require their own causation analysis:

- The arrest occurred before the challenged post-arrest reports, so those reports could not have caused the initial arrest.
- The blood draw is governed directly by the Fourth Amendment warrant/deception framework.
- Any Santa Rita detention under a parole hold may turn on an independent parole decision, creating an intervening-cause issue.
- A DMV license suspension is an administrative/property injury; its relationship to a Fourteenth Amendment fabrication claim must be researched and tied to the exact DS 367 process.
- A district attorney referral without a filed charge may be insufficient by itself.

### Recommended treatment

Do not spend opposition pages defending an abstract right to call the same conduct both judicial deception and fabrication. State that the categorical “no conviction” argument is too broad, but request leave to allege the exact fabrication, decisionmaker, use, deprivation, and causal chain. If the only supportable injury is the warranted blood draw, Count 1 is the more specific and coherent vehicle. If no distinct caused deprivation can be pleaded, dismissing Count 2 without prejudice may improve the opposition's credibility and focus.

## 9. Count Six — Retaliation

### Governing barriers

A retaliatory-arrest plaintiff ordinarily must plead and prove the absence of probable cause. *Nieves v. Bartlett*, 587 U.S. 391, 402–07 (2019). The narrow exception uses objective evidence that otherwise similarly situated persons not engaged in the protected conduct were not arrested. *Gonzalez v. Trevino*, 602 U.S. 653 (2024), rejects a demand for virtually identical comparators, but it does not eliminate the objective-evidence requirement.

### Protected activity defect

The current theory equates exercise of a statutory option not to take a voluntary PAS with First Amendment activity. *Camara*, *Prescott*, and related consent cases protect against treating refusal as evidence of guilt or authorize withholding consent; they do not clearly establish that every refusal is expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment. *Ballentine* does not establish this proposition on materially similar facts.

### Causation evidence

The statement—“If you choose to not consent to it, then it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not”—plus the arrest command one second after refusal and handcuffing 18 seconds after refusal supports a motive inference. But it can also be understood as an explanation that the officer would decide based on existing evidence if Plaintiff declined an additional test. If independent probable cause existed, *Nieves* is likely dispositive unless the exception is adequately pleaded.

The FAC's assertion that compliant drivers are not arrested “solely” for compliance is not objective differential-treatment evidence. It is circular and does not identify data, policy, examples, or a relevant class.

### Recommended treatment

Do not lead with Count 6. Either:

1. request leave to identify actual protected speech—criticism, a request for a supervisor, or another verbal protest—plus defendant-specific retaliatory conduct, absence of probable cause, and objective exception evidence; or
2. narrow or dismiss the claim while using the officer's statement as context for the timing and arrest decision under the Fourth Amendment.

Qualified immunity is especially likely because no located authority clearly established by November 1, 2024 that declining a voluntary PAS, without more, was protected First Amendment conduct supporting a retaliatory-arrest action under these circumstances.

## 11. Qualified immunity map

Qualified immunity must be analyzed by particular conduct, not by stating the general right to be free from unreasonable searches and arrests. The relevant legal cutoff is November 1, 2024.

| Theory | Constitutional merits | Clearly established problem | Likely motion-stage result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Door opening | *Ngumezi* establishes that opening/leaning into a vehicle is a search | *Ngumezi* had an awake driver at a gas station and no emergency; sleeping on an interstate shoulder is materially different | Merits may be pleadable; QI is substantial and may be dispositive |
| Cup seizure | Physical handling requires an exception | Apparent alcohol, location, sleep, and possible odor may create arguable automobile-exception PC | Fact-sensitive, but QI risk high |
| Continued vehicle search | Requires specific act and exception | FAC does not clearly identify the post-cup search | Dismissal with leave likely unless particularized |
| Arrest | Actual PC depends on contested observations | General no-PC law is too abstract; officers may have arguable PC from the totality | Best chance is to postpone factual resolution, but QI remains serious |
| Judicial deception | Deliberate/reckless material deception violates the Fourth Amendment | The right is clearly established; *Chism* rejects QI when supported | QI does not save deliberate material deception, but falsity/materiality pleading may fail first |
| Fabrication | Deliberate fabrication causing a protected deprivation is established in core criminal-process settings | No-charge, pretrial/administrative injury and more-specific-Fourth-Amendment boundaries are uncertain | Dismissal/leave likely; QI strong on the exact asserted right |
| Retaliation | Requires protected conduct, adverse action, and but-for causation; *Nieves* applies | PAS refusal as First Amendment conduct is not clearly established on analogous facts | QI likely |

The opposition should argue that qualified immunity cannot be granted by first resolving pleaded factual disputes in Defendants' favor. But it should not claim that all QI questions require discovery. Courts routinely decide QI on pleadings when Plaintiff's accepted version still does not show violation of a clearly established right.

## 12. Damages, remedies, and causation

### Punitive damages

Under *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983), § 1983 punitive damages may be available for evil motive/intent or reckless/callous indifference to federally protected rights. *Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co.*, 618 F.3d 970, 974–75 (9th Cir. 2010), holds that Rule 12(f) is not the proper mechanism to strike a damages demand merely because the relief is legally unavailable.

The clean position is dual:

- procedurally, deny the Rule 12(f) request under *Whittlestone*;
- substantively, preserve punitive damages only against an officer for whom deliberate deception, deliberate fabrication, or intentional retaliation remains plausibly alleged.

If only negligence, reasonable mistake, or arguable-probable-cause theories remain, the punitive request should be withdrawn or amended.

### Attorney's fees

Concede that Plaintiff cannot recover compensation for his own pro se work. *Kay v. Ehrler*, 499 U.S. 432, 435–38 (1991). Preserve:

- taxable costs;
- ordinary compensatory relief;
- any attorney's fees later incurred by qualifying, actually retained counsel if the statute and circumstances permit.

### Damages causation audit

Every large damages category needs a decision chain:

| Injury | Immediate decisionmaker/cause to verify | Causation risk |
|---|---|---|
| Roadside detention/arrest | Meza and any integral participant | Depends on entry/search/arrest merits and QI |

The opposition need not prove damages, but it should avoid using downstream harms as if causation were self-evident.

## 13. Prior litigation, Rule 41, preclusion, and futility

Defendants rely on prior cases primarily to argue that further amendment is futile and that Plaintiff is an experienced serial filer. The opposition should answer the exact procedural record without defensiveness.

### Known sequence

- The present N.D. Cal. action was filed August 10, 2026.
- Plaintiff filed a Rule 41(a)(1)(A)(i) notice dismissing the E.D. Cal. action without prejudice on August 11, 2026. The notice expressly called it the first voluntary dismissal of an action based on the claims.
- Plaintiff submitted a California CIV-110 request on August 14, 2026 to dismiss the Alameda action without prejudice; the current room includes a copy labeled as entered August 21, but the extracted text does not reliably show the clerk's entry block.

### Two-dismissal rule

Rule 41(a)(1)(B) provides that a federal Rule 41 notice operates as an adjudication on the merits when the plaintiff previously dismissed a federal or state action based on or including the same claim. On the presently known chronology, the August 11 federal notice preceded the August 14 state request and represented the first dismissal. A later California state-court request is governed by California procedure; it does not retroactively convert the earlier federal notice into a second Rule 41 notice. The current federal action has not been voluntarily dismissed.

**Working conclusion:** The two-dismissal rule does not presently appear to bar this action. This is not a final preclusion opinion. Verify filing/entry dates, every earlier action, claim identity, parties/privity, and whether any other qualifying dismissal preceded August 11.

### Claim and issue preclusion

A voluntary dismissal without prejudice ordinarily does not supply a final merits judgment for claim preclusion. A magistrate judge's recommendation does not itself become a final merits judgment without the district judge's disposition. Issue preclusion also requires an issue actually litigated and necessarily decided in a final determination. The original Kim recommendation, district-judge order, and closure entries must be obtained before making categorical statements.

### Best response to futility

Do not say the earlier proceedings are irrelevant. Say:

- the prior recommendation is serious adverse history;
- the current opposition does not ask the Court to ignore it;
- the actual produced warrant and forms now permit a more precise pleading;
- Plaintiff identifies corrections that narrow rather than multiply allegations; and
- futility must be evaluated against the proposed amendment, not against errors Plaintiff offers to remove.

Rule 15's policy favors amendment, and prejudice is the most important *Foman* factor. *Eminence Capital, LLC v. Aspeon, Inc.*, 316 F.3d 1048, 1051–52 (9th Cir. 2003). A pro se civil-rights complaint generally should not be dismissed without leave unless it is clear the deficiency cannot be cured. *Lopez v. Smith*, 203 F.3d 1122, 1130–31 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc). Prior opportunities to amend enlarge the court's discretion, so the opposition must present exact cures rather than rely on the general rule.

## 20. Bottom line

The maximum-value opposition is a **credibility-first preservation brief**. It should:

1. prevent Defendants from winning disputed facts through police paperwork at Rule 12;
2. divide the encounter into separate intrusions and require a separate justification at each stage;
3. candidly correct the checkbox, warrant-document, duration, and refusal errors;
4. concentrate on the entry, cup search, arrest, and leave-to-amend issues;
5. treat judicial deception as an actual-affidavit/materiality problem, not a slogan;
6. narrow fabrication and retaliation unless the missing causation and authority can be supplied;
7. oppose Rule 12(f), concede only Plaintiff's own pro se fees, and condition punitive damages on a surviving intentional/reckless theory; and

It is to preserve the maximum amount of viable constitutional litigation and to earn the Court's confidence for the factual disputes that genuinely require a record.
