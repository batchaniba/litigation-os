                     6   III.   PLAINTIFF’S FIFTH CLAIM FOR INVASION OF PRIVACY SHOULD BE
                                DISMISSED WITH PREJUDICE.
                     7
                                Plaintiff’s Opposition conflates several of the factors necessary to show an invasion of
                     8
                         privacy and asserts that one factor should override the rest. This is legally incorrect.
                     9
                                Throughout his Opposition, Plaintiff continually references California Penal Code Section
                    10
                         290.46 as creating a constitutional right to privacy over public information. See Opp. 10:13-17
                    11
                         (stating that the Complaint “alleges an interest in the information ‘insofar as that information is
                    12
                         used for purposes expressly prohibited by Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)’”); Opp. 10:28-11:9. He
                    13
                         argues that his claim is premised on the use of the information rather than the information itself.
                    14
                         Opp. 11:21–28. This misstates the reach of a claim for invasion of privacy.
                    15
                                A claim for invasion of privacy under the California Constitution has three parts: (1) a
                    16
                         legally protected privacy interest (the interest); (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy (the
                    17
                         expectation); and (3) a serious invasion of that privacy interest (the defendant’s conduct). Hill v.
                    18
                         Nat’l Collegiate Ath. Ass’n, 7 Cal.4th 1, 35-37 (Cal. 1994). Plaintiff asks the Court to find that
                    19
                         Amazon’s purported conduct effectively satisfies the interest factor as constitutionally protected.
                    20
                         This argument is backwards. Indeed, informational privacy only applies when there is “sensitive
                    21
                         and confidential information” that underlies the claim of invasion of privacy. Id. at 35 (emphasis
                    22
                         added). When the allegedly confidential information is already publicly disclosed, there cannot
                    23
                         be a valid right to privacy claim. See Fredenburg v. City of Fremont, 119 Cal.App.4th 408, 422
                    24
                         (Cal. Ct. App. 2004) (noting that “essentially public information is not surrounded by a legally
                    25
                         protected privacy interest”). No quantum of misuse alleged can backfill an alleged privacy
                    26
                         interest that the underlying information lacks. Without any protected interest in the public
                    27
                         information regarding his claim, Plaintiff cannot state a claim for invasion of privacy.

                     1          Even if an interest exists (it does not), Plaintiff’s argument regarding consent is similarly

                     2   flawed. He claims that Amazon argues for unfettered consent to “every possible source and use.”

                     3   Opp. 12:7-8. But even his Complaint shows that this alleged use was limited to any employment
                     4   decision; there are no allegations that it was disseminated, broadcast, or sent to any third party.

                     5   Compl.¶¶ 7-8. He consented to the use of the public information by means of a background

                     6   check as part of routine employment procedures. That is precisely the type of use he consented to

                     7   when signing up for a background check and tied directly to the harms he alleges. See Compl.
                     8   ¶ 75 (“The invasion is serious because it resulted in the denial of employment . . . .”). There

                     9   cannot be a sufficiently serious invasion when the information collected is used in alignment with

                    10   a party’s expectations and consent. See Saleh v. Nike, Inc., 562 F. Supp. 3d 503, 525 (C.D. Cal.
                    11   2021) (discussing that “routine collection” of information and a plaintiff’s “privacy choices”

                    12   factor into seriousness of invasion).

                    13          Plaintiff’s Opposition showcases his attempt to take a statutory right and morph it into a

                    14   constitutional one. His claim still fails. Throughout his Opposition, Plaintiff continually
                    15   references section 290.46 as creating a constitutional right to privacy over public information.

                    16   E.g., Opp. 10:13-17 (stating that the Complaint “alleges an interest in the information ‘insofar as

                    17   that information is used for purposes expressly prohibited by Penal Code § 290.46(j)(2)”). That
                    18   cannot be the case. Indeed, Plaintiff has already brought a claim under Megan’s Law, the statute

                    19   providing the specific rights and remedies allegedly applicable for Count One, and he cannot now

                    20   recast the same violations as a constitutional issue simply because the Legislature mandated it.

                    21   See Hill, 7 Cal.4th at 35 (discussing that privacy violations are dependent on “well-established
                    22   social norms”).

                    23          In any event, additional factual pleading would be futile given that Plaintiff cannot allege

                    24   a protected interest nor change the fact he consented to a background check. Plaintiff’s claim

                    25   should be dismissed with prejudice.
