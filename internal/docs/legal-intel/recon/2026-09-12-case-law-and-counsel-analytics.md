## 1. Penal Code § 290.46 — the employment-use restriction

### 1.1 Statutory framework (binding)
Current Penal Code § 290.46(j) (older decisions use historical subsection (l)):
- **(j)(1):** A person is authorized to use information disclosed pursuant to the section **only to protect a "person at risk."**
- **(j)(2)(E):** Except as authorized under paragraph (1) **or any other provision of law**, use of any information disclosed pursuant to this section for purposes relating to **employment** is prohibited (alongside health insurance, insurance, loans, credit, education, housing, etc.).
- **(j)(3):** Carve-outs preserving authorized access under other provisions (Pen. Code §§ 11105, 11105.3; Fam. Code § 8808; Fin. Code §§ 777.5, 14409.2; Health & Safety Code §§ 1522.01, 1596.871; Lab. Code § 432.7).
- **(j)(4)(A) (remedies):** A user who violates the section is liable for **actual damages**, plus an amount determined by the trier of fact **not exceeding three times actual damages and not less than $250**, plus **attorney's fees**, plus **exemplary damages or a civil penalty not exceeding $25,000**. The parallel criminal-registry provision, § 290.4(d)(4)(A), carries the same schedule.
- "Person at risk" is **not defined in the current §290.46 text**, and no located employment decision definitively defines it. *Mendoza* contains defense-favorable dictum but does not decide the exception's scope.

### 1.2 The merits and comparator decisions
The § 290.46 employment-use field is nearly empty. The direct applications now verified are *Pastrana*, *Donaleski*, *Mendoza*, and *Lerma*, but they resolve different questions and different procedural postures. No located final judgment imposes §290.46 damages on an employer. *Pastrana* is a defense summary-judgment win based on a specific statutory authorization; *Donaleski* is a defense summary-judgment win based on independent lawful sourcing and decisionmaking; *Mendoza* is a published anti-SLAPP decision distinguishing a CRA that merely republishes from the employer that acts; and *Lerma* is a pleading-stage source-attribution dismissal.

**a. *Donaleski v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, No. 2:08-cv-00219-JAM-JFM (E.D. Cal. May 8, 2009) (Mendez, J.) — the lead case.** An assistant manager claimed Wal-Mart used his California Megan's Law Website listing to fire him, seeking the civil penalty, statutory, general, economic, and special damages. Summary judgment for Wal-Mart:
- **Rule:** "The statutory language of the CPC **does not prevent an employer from engaging in an independent investigation and obtaining personal information from the ex-offender even if the employer is aware that the ex-offender's information is displayed on the Website**" (citing *Pastrana*).
- **Causation is the crux:** the termination committee never consulted the Website, did not know plaintiff was listed, and acted on his self-disclosed conviction history. Mere awareness of the listing by lower-level managers, and the fact that the listing first surfaced the issue, did not make the decision a prohibited "use."
- **Independent-decisionmaker insulation:** citing *Lakeside-Scott v. Multnomah County*, 556 F.3d 797, 799 (9th Cir. 2009), a final decisionmaker's wholly independent, legitimate decision breaks the causal link to a subordinate's tainted knowledge.
- **Negligence count** premised on the same facts failed with the statutory claim (no "use" = no breach of duty).
- The court **declined to reach** Wal-Mart's as-applied constitutionality challenge — so no holding narrows the statute.
- **Plaintiff-side lesson from *Donaleski*:** liability requires proving the *decisionmaker* used the Website information. The plaintiff lost because the paper trail showed the committee used only the employee's own statements.

**b. *Pastrana v. Local 9509, Communications Workers of America*, 2008 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 94612 (S.D. Cal. Nov. 19, 2008).** The full order has now been verified. Pastrana pleaded §290.46 and a public-policy termination claim. PacBell prevailed on those claims because §290.46's savings provision preserved a specific independently authorized use under Penal Code §11105(c)(10)(A) for covered public-utility employees entering private residences. The key proposition is therefore **specific statutory authorization**, not a generalized independent-investigation safe harbor.

**c. *Lerma v. Amazon.com Servs.*, No. 8:22-cv-01727-FWS (C.D. Cal., Slaughter, J.)** — the closest factual comparator, and its full docket is in this portfolio's documents branch (`internal/public/documents/matter-29/lerma-v-amazon/`, 70 filings). Miguel Lerma Jr. sued Amazon and Accurate Background in a putative class action alleging the companies used California Megan's Law website data to revoke a conditional job offer (seasonal hire Nov. 2021; offer revoked after Accurate reported 2011/2013 felony rape convictions); the FAC also invoked the Fair Chance Act (Gov. Code § 12952), ICRAA's seven-year rule (Civ. Code § 1786.18(a)(7)), FCRA, and the UCL. On May 26, 2023 (Dkt. 36), Judge Slaughter granted both MTDs:
- **Megan's Law claim (Claim 1) — DISMISSED without prejudice, with leave to amend.** The killing fact: Accurate's background report, judicially noticed under the incorporation-by-reference doctrine, showed the criminal history came from **Orange County criminal records** (the only "record found" among twelve databases searched) — not the Megan's Law website. The court refused to accept the "competing theory" of MLW sourcing where the complaint's own incorporated report contradicted it (*Sprewell*). This is the perfect *negative* comparator for Matter 26: Lerma lost on sourcing because his report showed a non-MLW source; Matter 26's Exhibit A affirmatively states "SOURCE OF RECORD: CALIFORNIA SEX OFFENDER REGISTRY." The two cases are mirror images on the dispositive fact.
- **FCRA claim (Claim 4) — DISMISSED** as predicated entirely on the underlying Megan's Law violation; fell with it.
- **UCL claim (Claim 5) — DISMISSED** for failure to plead lack of an adequate remedy at law (*Sonner v. Premier Nutrition*, 971 F.3d 834; *Barranco v. 3D Sys. Corp.*, 952 F.3d 1122); plaintiff effectively conceded the point. **Practice note for Matter 26:** a federal UCL claim must plead the absence of an adequate legal remedy — check the UPS complaint for a *Sonner* allegation.
- **ICRAA claim (Claim 3) — dismissed** as to Accurate (unopposed).
- **Fair Chance Act claim (Claim 2) — SURVIVED** (not challenged by either MTD). The individualized-assessment theory was never tested at the pleading stage.
- Leave to amend granted (30 days); plaintiff filed no SAC, an OSC re lack of prosecution issued, Amazon answered the surviving claims, and the case settled — Notice of Settlement filed **September 21, 2023** (Dkt. 46), action dismissed September 29, 2023 (Dkt. 47), stipulated dismissal October 26, 2023 (Dkt. 48). **Plaintiff-side lesson:** the § 290.46 claim's life or death turns on *source attribution in the report itself* — which is precisely what Exhibit A supplies in Matter 26 and what Lerma's report withheld.

**d. *Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Servs., Inc.*, 182 Cal.App.4th 1644, 1657–1658 (2010) (Cal. Ct. App., 2d Dist., Div. 8) (binding on state-law issues)** — the only published California appellate decision construing § 290.46's "use" prohibition. A background screener that accessed the Megan's Law website and republished the information to client employers was held **not** to have "used" the information: liability "should be limited to employers who 'use' information disclosed on the MLW as a basis for an employment decision" — not to businesses that "merely reproduce or republish such information, without actually acting on the information"; "His cause of action, if any, lies against his prospective employer, and not [the screener]." **Plaintiff-side lesson:** UPS — the employer whose Centralized Adjudication Team decided on the report — is exactly the party *Mendoza* says bears § 290.46(l) liability; UPS cannot deflect onto its vendor under a *Mendoza* theory. **Defense-side warning:** *Mendoza* contains dictum that "a strong argument might be made that employees are persons at risk whom an employer has a duty to protect from a sexually violent predator" — the best judicial language UPS can cite for a broad "person at risk" reading. It was offered about a screener, not applied to an employer's hiring decision; no court has held that a customer base qualifies (see § 1.3(3)).

**No published or unpublished decision was found** holding an employer liable under § 290.46(l) for an employment action, and **no decision defines "person at risk"** in this context (multiple practitioner analyses, e.g. Littler 2004; Mondaq 2005/2015; Lexology 2015, agree the term is judicially undefined — these are secondary sources, not authority).

### 1.3 Open issues decisive for the UPS case (no binding authority)
1. **Source tracing — California website vs. national registry.** The statute restricts "information disclosed pursuant to this section" (the California DOJ website). UPS's brief disclaims reliance on the California registry while admitting national-registry use. **But Exhibit A says "SOURCE OF RECORD: CALIFORNIA SEX OFFENDER REGISTRY."** At the pleading stage the court must accept the complaint's well-pleaded facts and may consider the exhibit; the contradiction is pleaded against UPS, not for it. *Donaleski* never addressed a vendor report naming the California registry as its source — its "independent investigation" safe harbor involved self-disclosed history, not a registry-sourced dossier. **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — the exhibit is the case; if the trier of fact finds the California registry was the source, the statutory text squarely covers it. Defense — will argue the data was national-registry data merely labeled with the California source, and that a vendor's mislabeling is not UPS's "use" of website-disclosed information.**
2. **"Use" and the Centralized Adjudication Team.** Exhibit A shows "Decisional," "INELIGIBLE," and "Adjudicated by the Centralized Adjudication Team." Unlike *Donaleski*'s committee that never saw the Website, UPS's adjudicators rendered a decision on a report whose stated source of record is the California registry. That is pleaded "use," and *Lakeside-Scott* insulation is unavailable where the decisionmaker itself acted on the tainted report.
3. **"Person at risk."** The current statute uses the phrase but does not define it, and no located employment decision definitively supplies a definition. UPS can rely on *Mendoza*'s dictum that "a strong argument might be made that employees are persons at risk whom an employer has a duty to protect from a sexually violent predator." The response is that this was dictum in a CRA anti-SLAPP appeal, was never applied to an employer's actual hiring decision, and does not establish that ordinary coworkers, customers, or the public automatically qualify.
4. **"Or any other provision of law."** The § 290.46(l)(2) savings clause plus the (l)(3) carve-out list (including Lab. Code § 432.7) is the defense's other escape hatch. Note UPS has not identified such a provision in the briefing described; bare invocation without a statute will not survive *Twombly/Iqbal*.

### 1.4 § 290.46 bottom line
The statute plainly prohibits what is pleaded; the only merits precedents are defense summary-judgment wins turning on proof that the decisionmaker never used the Website information — a showing UPS cannot make on the pleadings given Exhibit A. **Pleading-stage survival: strong. Summary-judgment risk: the *Donaleski* causation fight (source tracing, who knew what, whether the national registry is "information disclosed pursuant to this section").**

## 2. ICRAA — disclosure, authorization, adverse action, and FCRA interplay

### 2.1 Core rules (binding)
- **Investigative consumer reports in employment are covered.** *Connor v. First Student, Inc.*, 5 Cal. 5th 1026 (2018): ICRAA is not unconstitutionally vague merely because it overlaps with the CCRAA; reports containing character information (including criminal/registry history) are investigative consumer reports "regardless of how [the information] was obtained," and an employer obtaining them **must comply with ICRAA's disclosure and written-authorization requirements**.
- **Disclosure + authorization duties.** Civ. Code § 1786.16(a)(2): before procuring a report for employment purposes the user must make a **clear and conspicuous written disclosure** — in a document consisting **solely** of the disclosure — stating the report's purpose, that it may include character/reputation information, identifying the agency by name/address/phone, and defining the scope; and must obtain the consumer's **written authorization**.
- **Remedies.** Civ. Code § 1786.50: the greater of actual damages or **$10,000**, plus punitive damages, attorney's fees, and costs.
- **FCRA parallel.** 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(A): employer's pre-procurement disclosure must be "clear and conspicuous" and standalone ("in writing... in a document that consists solely of the disclosure"). 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A): pre-adverse-action notice with a copy of the report and summary of rights.

### 2.2 Pleading-stage case law (binding/persuasive)
- ***Gilberg v. California Check Cashing Stores*, 913 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 2019) (binding):** An employer that combined FCRA and state-law (including ICRAA) disclosures into one form violated **both** statutes' standalone requirements; "each required disclosure should only contain the information explicitly required by that statute." ICRAA disclosure duties are "identical" to FCRA's in this respect. Reversed a 12(b)(6) dismissal. **Direct support that defective-disclosure ICRAA claims survive pleading and that the Ninth Circuit reads ICRAA in parallel with FCRA.**
- ***Walker v. Fred Meyer, Inc.*, 953 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 2020) (binding):** Paragraph-by-paragraph review; disclosures may briefly explain what a consumer report is, how it is obtained, and the employment purposes, and may identify the agency and the categories searched — but extraneous material (e.g., file-inspection rights better placed elsewhere) violates the standalone rule. Sets the "concise explanation" standard. **Plaintiff-side use: measures UPS's forms; defense-side use: UPS's forms may pass if lean.**
- ***Connor*, 5 Cal. 5th 1026 (binding):** Reversed summary judgment for the employer; constitutional challenge rejected; background checks = investigative consumer reports. Note *Connor* was decided at summary judgment, but its statutory-construction holdings control at any stage.

### 2.3 FCRA interplay
ICRAA and FCRA apply concurrently to the same report; compliance with one does not excuse the other (*Connor*; *Gilberg*). The practical consequence is pleading leverage: an ICRAA disclosure defect claim can ride alongside an FCRA § 1681b(b)(2) claim on the same form. ICRAA's $10,000-or-actual-damages floor (Civ. Code § 1786.50) is more generous than FCRA's willful-violation tier, and ICRAA has no FCRA-style "willfulness" gate for the statutory minimum.

### 2.4 Standing for technical violations — the defense's best ICRAA authority
- ***Ramirez v. TransUnion LLC*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021) (binding):** Article III concrete harm exists where misleading information is **disseminated to a third party** (there, creditors) — the class members whose files were merely held internally lacked standing. **Applied here this cuts for the plaintiff:** the report was disseminated to UPS's Centralized Adjudication Team and used to render an "INELIGIBLE" decision — the paradigmatic concrete adverse action.
- ***Limon v. Circle K Stores, Inc.*, 84 Cal. App. 5th 671 (2023), review denied Jan. 2024 (binding on state-law standing):** "An informational injury that causes no adverse effect is insufficient" — a plaintiff hired with no issue from his background check lacked standing for a purely technical FCRA disclosure violation. **Distinguishable here:** the UPS applicant was *rejected* on the report — concrete harm, not a bare informational injury.
- **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — adverse action (rejection) supplies the concrete injury *Limon* found missing and satisfies *Ramirez*'s dissemination-plus-use test; a pure failure-to-give-a-copy or defective-form claim without adverse action would be the vulnerable tail. Defense — will press *Limon*/*TransUnion* on any technical-violation-only sub-theory and argue no willfulness/actual injury.**

### 2.5 ICRAA bottom line
*Connor* + *Gilberg* make the disclosure/authorization claim pleading-viable; *Ramirez* + the rejection distinguish *Limon* on standing. **Survival: strong on disclosure/authorization and any adverse-action-notice defect tied to the rejection.**

## 3. Does *Tameny* extend to failure to hire? — No, under current California law

### 3.1 Rules (binding)
- ***Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 27 Cal. 3d 167 (1980):** An **employee** discharged in violation of a fundamental public policy stated in a constitutional or statutory provision may sue in tort.
- ***Miklosy v. Regents of University of California*, 44 Cal. 4th 876, 900 (2008) (binding):** Breach of **an employment relationship** is "indispensable to the maintenance of" a *Tameny* claim.
- ***Williams v. Sacramento River Cats Baseball Club, LLC*, 40 Cal. App. 5th 280, 288 (2019) (binding):** California **does not recognize a common-law wrongful-failure-to-hire claim** — with no employment relationship there is no duty whose breach the tort can remedy.
- ***Daly v. Exxon Corp.*, 55 Cal. App. 4th 39, 45–46 (1997) (binding):** No *Tameny* tort where the employment relationship had ended (nonrenewal after a fixed term expired).
- ***Gantt v. Sentry Insurance*, 1 Cal. 4th 1083 (1992) (binding):** The public policy must be "fundamental," "substantial," "tethered to" a constitutional or statutory provision — § 290.46(l)'s express employment-use prohibition would satisfy the *policy* prong, but the *relationship* prong still fails.
- ***Stevenson v. Superior Court*, 16 Cal. 4th 880 (1997) (binding):** Where the Legislature has built a comprehensive remedial scheme (there, FEHA), common-law duplication is disfavored — an additional reason courts resist inventing a failure-to-hire tort.

### 3.2 Recent reinforcement
- ***Hill*, No. D084500 (Cal. Ct. App. 2024) (unpublished; persuasive only, and uncitable in California state court):** Applied *Williams* to dismiss a deterred applicant's failure-to-hire theory without leave. Signals the rule is stable and recently re-applied.

### 3.3 Bottom line
Even treating § 290.46(l) as the requisite fundamental policy, *Miklosy* + *Williams* close the door: **no employment relationship, no *Tameny* duty, no tort.** The standalone failure-to-hire count is the complaint's most vulnerable. **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — argue for extension as a matter of first impression (the policy is unusually explicit), but expect dismissal; preserve the issue and fold the § 290.46 policy into the UCL and negligence theories. Defense — *Williams* is a clean, recent, on-point kill shot; will seek dismissal with prejudice and cite *Hill* (2024) as confirmation.**

## 4. UCL unlawful-prong claim predicated on § 290.46

### 4.1 Rules (binding)
- The UCL's **unlawful prong** "borrows" violations of other laws — including criminal statutes with civil remedies — and makes them independently actionable as unfair competition. *Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co.*, 20 Cal. 4th 163 (1999); *Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court*, 2 Cal. 4th 377 (1992). A pleaded § 290.46(l) violation is a valid predicate; the UCL adds a four-year limitations period (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17208) and representative remedies.
- **Standing (Prop. 64):** plaintiff must have "lost money or property" as a result of the violation. *Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court*, 51 Cal. 4th 310 (2011): economic injury in the ordinary sense suffices — including being "deprived of money or property to which he or she has a cognizable claim"; the injury need not also be restitutionary. *Zhang v. Superior Court*, 57 Cal. 4th 364 (2013) (same).
- **Remedies are limited:** restitution is confined to money or property **acquired by the defendant** by means of the violation. *Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 29 Cal. 4th 1134 (2003): nonrestitutionary disgorgement — e.g., lost profits or wages the defendant never received from the plaintiff — is **not** recoverable. Injunctive relief remains available even to a plaintiff ineligible for restitution (*Kwikset*).

### 4.2 Application
- **Standing:** lost employment income/opportunity from the rejection is economic injury under *Kwikset*'s ordinary-meaning test — but UPS will argue the wages were never the applicant's (no offer, no cognizable claim to *UPS's* money), testing the outer edge of *Kwikset*. Pleading that the applicant was qualified and the rejection flowed from the unlawful report use is the load-bearing allegation.
- **Restitution:** likely **unavailable** — UPS acquired nothing *from* the applicant; the lost wages are *Korea Supply* nonrestitutionary damages. Expect this remedy to be struck or limited.
- **Injunction:** available in principle, but federal Article III requires a real and immediate threat of future harm. *Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.*, 889 F.3d 956 (9th Cir. 2017) (binding): injunctive standing requires plausibly alleging the plaintiff will again encounter the conduct (there, would purchase again but cannot rely on labeling). A **one-time rejected applicant** who does not allege intent to reapply faces a serious standing attack on the injunction; alleging intent to reapply to UPS (a mass hirer) plus UPS's ongoing use of the same vendor/adjudication pipeline is the *Davidson*-style cure.
- **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — the unlawful prong survives wherever the § 290.46 claim survives, adding a 4-year tail and fee leverage; plead intent to reapply to hold the injunction. Defense — concede nothing on the predicate, but kill restitution under *Korea Supply* and the injunction under Article III future-harm standing.**

## 5. Negligence per se as a standalone count

### 5.1 Rule (binding)
- **Negligence per se is an evidentiary presumption, not an independent cause of action.** *Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center*, 140 Cal. App. 4th 1256, 1285–86 (2006); *Millard v. Biosources, Inc.*, 156 Cal. App. 4th 1338, 1353 (2007): the doctrine (Evid. Code § 669; *Elsner v. Uveges*, 34 Cal. 4th 915 (2004)) supplies the *standard of care* for a **negligence** claim — it requires a viable underlying duty of reasonable care, which a statute's violation can then help establish.
- A separately numbered "negligence per se" count is therefore routinely dismissed, with courts treating the allegations as part of the negligence claim or striking the duplicative count.

### 5.2 Application
- Expect the standalone count to be **dismissed** (likely with leave to replead as negligence). The underlying negligence theory — duty of reasonable care in procuring/using background reports for hiring, breached by using registry information the Legislature forbade employers to use — is where the fight actually lives.
- Note *Donaleski* again: the negligence claim there fell **with** the statutory claim for lack of "use" — i.e., no breach. Here, Exhibit A pleads the breach, so the negligence theory's survival tracks the § 290.46 claim's survival, not the label on the count.
- **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — concede the label, defend the substance: replead as negligence with § 290.46 supplying the standard of care under Evid. Code § 669. Defense — *Quiroz*/*Millard* make dismissal of the standalone count nearly automatic; attack any freestanding negligence theory for lack of duty to a non-employee applicant.**

## 6. Privacy theories — constitutional and common-law

### 6.1 Rules (binding)
- **California Constitution, art. I, § 1** creates an enforceable privacy right against private actors. *Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn.*, 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994) (three elements): (1) a legally protected privacy interest; (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy under the circumstances; (3) a serious invasion of privacy — balanced against competing interests. *Chico Feminist Women's Health Center v. Scully*, 208 Cal. App. 3d 230 (1989) (same framework).
- **Common-law intrusion upon seclusion:** intentional intrusion into private affairs where the plaintiff has a reasonable expectation of privacy, in a manner highly offensive to a reasonable person. *Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc.*, 18 Cal. 4th 200 (1998).
- **Public disclosure of private facts:** truthful information lawfully obtained from **public records** generally cannot support liability. *Gates v. Discovery Communications, Inc.*, 34 Cal. 4th 679 (2004); *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469 (1975) (First Amendment).

### 6.2 Application — the theories cut in opposite directions
- **Plaintiff-side:** The Legislature itself classified Megan's Law website information as privacy-sensitive by restricting its use (*§ 290.46(l)*) — that legislative judgment feeds *Hill*'s first two elements (legally protected interest + reasonable expectation that employers will not use it). The Riverside criminal-index litigation (*All of Us or None v. Hamrick* — E.D. Cal. 2018; Cal. Ct. App. reversed a demurrer, holding court-rule limits on DOB-searchable criminal indexes stated privacy/UCL claims) shows California courts treat **aggregated criminal-history data** as privacy-sensitive even when the underlying records are public. A vendor-assembled dossier used to brand an applicant "INELIGIBLE" is a serious, consequential invasion.
- **Defense-side:** *Gates*/*Cox Broadcasting* — registry information is public-record truth; UPS will argue there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in it and no "intrusion" where the applicant **authorized** a background check (*Shulman*'s offensiveness prong). The intrusion theory is the weaker of the two; the constitutional theory tethered to the § 290.46 legislative judgment is the stronger.
- **One-line assessment: Plaintiff — anchor privacy in the constitutional right + the Legislature's own use restriction (not in common-law intrusion, which the signed authorization undercuts); *Hamrick* shows courts take criminal-data aggregation seriously. Defense — *Gates* and the signed authorization: public-record information voluntarily submitted to a background check cannot be a "serious invasion" of a "reasonable expectation."**
