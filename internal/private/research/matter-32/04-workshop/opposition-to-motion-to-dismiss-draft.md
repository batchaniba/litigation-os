## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendants’ motion depends on a chronology the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) does not plead and on factual choices a court cannot make under Rule 12(b)(6). The motion combines three distinct events—the opening of a closed passenger door, the physical removal and examination of a cup, and an arrest approximately fourteen minutes later—and treats facts allegedly learned during or after those events as if they necessarily justified everything that came before.

The FAC alleges a different sequence. Defendant Melano formed an investigative hypothesis about a visible cup before speaking with Plaintiff. FAC ¶¶ 14–15. He then opened the passenger door without a warrant or consent. Id. ¶¶ 16, 54–58. He later reached into the vehicle, removed the cup, smelled it, and identified its contents as urine. Id. ¶¶ 17–18, 59–63. The officers continued the investigation, administered field exercises on ground alleged to be unsuitable for some exercises, and arrested Plaintiff shortly after he refused a test Defendant Meza-Gonzalez had expressly described as voluntary. Id. ¶¶ 19–24, 48–53, 64–70. The FAC further alleges that Meza-Gonzalez later made materially false or misleading sworn statements that caused a blood-draw warrant, confinement, and an administrative suspension of Plaintiff’s commercial driving privilege. Id. ¶¶ 25–30, 36–47.

Defendants do not attach the warrant affidavit, reports, video, or any request for judicial notice. Yet their motion supplies benign explanations for the challenged statements, assumes that contested signs of intoxication were true, treats silence concerning field-exercise “results” as an admission of inculpatory results, and labels Plaintiff “unconscious” even though the FAC alleges that he had pulled over to rest. Those are factual arguments for a later record, not grounds to dismiss a complaint.

The motion also makes legal errors. Opening a vehicle door to obtain information is a Fourth Amendment search. *Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d at 1289. Probable cause to suspect a parking violation does not automatically authorize an evidence-gathering search of the passenger compartment, and Vehicle Code section 21718 itself contains exceptions the motion omits. Facts learned after a search cannot retroactively justify its inception. The absence of a criminal charge is not a categorical bar to a fabrication claim alleging a distinct liberty or property deprivation; *Costanich* recognized a due-process fabrication claim arising from civil proceedings. And Rule 12(f) is not a substitute for adjudicating whether damages are recoverable. *Whittlestone*, 618 F.3d at 974–75.

The Court should deny the motion. At minimum, any dismissal must be claim- and defendant-specific and with leave to amend. Plaintiff provides a concrete amendment proffer below, including corrections that eliminate any plausible claim of futility.

## II. ISSUES PRESENTED

1. Whether Defendants may obtain Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal by accepting contested police observations as true, selecting innocent explanations for alleged discrepancies, and using facts allegedly learned later to justify an earlier vehicle intrusion.

2. Whether the allegations that Melano opened a closed passenger door without a warrant or consent, reached inside, and seized and examined an object state Fourth Amendment claims under *Ngumezi*.

3. Whether allegations of coherent orientation, dissipation of the officers’ initial cup hypothesis, unsuitable ground conditions for some field exercises, and an arrest immediately following refusal of a voluntary test plausibly allege absence of probable cause.

4. Whether a deliberate-fabrication claim necessarily fails when no criminal charge was filed, notwithstanding alleged use of fabricated evidence to cause other liberty and property deprivations.

5. Whether the alleged statement conditioning arrest discretion on PAS consent, followed by refusal and arrest, plausibly supports but-for causation at the pleading stage.

6. Whether qualified immunity, futility, or Rule 12(f) supports blanket dismissal with prejudice and removal of Plaintiff’s punitive-damages request.

## IV. LEGAL STANDARD

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests the complaint’s legal sufficiency. The Court accepts well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draws reasonable inferences in Plaintiff’s favor. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678; *Twombly*, 550 U.S. at 555–56. A court may disregard a legal conclusion, but it may not discard a specific factual allegation merely because the defendant offers another explanation.

This distinction matters where a complaint challenges the truth of government records. Incorporation by reference and judicial notice cannot be used “to resolve factual disputes against the plaintiff’s well-pled complaint.” *Khoja*, 899 F.3d at 1014. Even where an external document may properly be considered, it is not automatically assumed to establish the truth of contested statements within it. See id. at 999–1003. Here Defendants submitted no evidentiary material and sought no judicial notice. The Court therefore evaluates the pleaded facts—not Defendants’ preferred reconstruction.

Because Plaintiff proceeds pro se, his pleading is construed liberally, and leave to amend should be granted unless amendment would be futile. *Garity*, 828 F.3d at 854; *Lopez*, 203 F.3d at 1130–31. Rule 15 embodies an “extreme liberality” policy. *Lacey*, 693 F.3d at 926.

## V. ARGUMENT

### A. The Motion Improperly Resolves Disputed Facts and Draws Inferences Against Plaintiff

The motion’s principal method is to rename disputed allegations as conclusions and then replace them with Defendants’ account.

For the deception claim, Defendants propose that there was “no need” to verbalize an odor and that an odor statement may not have been captured. Mot. 6. Those are competing factual possibilities, not pleaded facts. Defendants similarly argue that coherent conversation is compatible with slurred speech and that Plaintiff’s demonstrated orientation is “irrelevant.” Id. Neither proposition allows the Court to treat the challenged police descriptions as true on a motion to dismiss. The FAC identifies the speaker, documents, challenged descriptions, contradictory conduct, omissions, uses of the statements, and resulting injuries. FAC ¶¶ 25–30, 38–47. Whether the contradictions reflect deliberateness, recklessness, mistake, or no contradiction at all is a factual question.

For the search and arrest claims, Defendants rely on “signs of intoxication” that the FAC specifically disputes. Mot. 9–10. They also treat the absence of pleaded field-exercise results as though it established adverse results. Id. at 6, 10. Rule 8 does not require Plaintiff to plead evidence assisting Defendants, and silence about results is not a factual admission.

Most importantly, the motion compresses the encounter. The legality of each intrusion turns on what was known when it occurred. Facts learned by removing and examining the cup cannot retroactively justify opening the door to obtain those facts. Later observations likewise cannot retroactively validate an earlier search. The Court should evaluate the door opening, the cup seizure and examination, any continued search, and the arrest at their respective times.

### B. Count IV Plausibly Alleges an Unlawful Warrantless Entry

#### 1. Opening the passenger door was a search.

The Ninth Circuit has held that an officer conducted a Fourth Amendment search by opening a vehicle’s passenger door and leaning inside to ask questions. *Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d at 1289. The “modest” magnitude of the physical intrusion did not remove it from the Fourth Amendment. Id. The FAC alleges the same legally significant act: Melano opened Plaintiff’s closed passenger door to investigate, without a warrant or consent. FAC ¶¶ 16, 55–56. That states a search.

Defendants cite *Devenpeck*, an arrest case, for the general proposition that probable cause makes a warrantless search reasonable. Mot. 8–9. But probable cause alone does not erase the warrant requirement; Defendants must identify an applicable exception and facts supporting it at the search’s inception. Their motion does not establish such an exception from the face of the FAC.

#### 2. Emergency aid cannot be decided by converting “resting” into “unconscious.”

Emergency aid permits a warrantless entry when officers have an objectively reasonable basis to believe a person inside needs immediate assistance. *Mincey*, 437 U.S. at 392. The FAC does not allege an accident, injury, smoke, fire, medical distress, unresponsiveness after attempted contact, or any other objective sign of an emergency. It alleges that Plaintiff became fatigued, pulled out of active travel lanes, and rested in the driver’s seat. FAC ¶¶ 11–12, 56.

The motion repeatedly calls Plaintiff “unconscious.” Mot. 2, 7. That is Defendants’ characterization, not an allegation the Court must accept. Nor may the Court infer that every sleeping or resting occupant of an intact vehicle requires immediate medical intervention. The reasonableness of any welfare concern may be litigated on evidence, but the pleaded absence of objective emergency facts prevents dismissal as a matter of law.

The motion also argues that Defendants could not know no emergency existed until after they opened the door. Mot. 8. That assumes the very conclusion the emergency-aid exception requires. The relevant question is whether objective facts known before entry created a reasonable basis to believe immediate aid was needed—not whether an entry could eliminate uncertainty. On the FAC, that question cannot be resolved for Defendants.

#### 3. The motion’s parking-offense theory omits the pleaded facts and the statute’s exception.

Defendants posit a binary: if Plaintiff faced no medical emergency, his shoulder stop necessarily violated Vehicle Code section 21718, and that suspected violation justified opening the door. Mot. 8. The statute is not so categorical. Section 21718(a)(1) excepts a stop made when necessary to avoid injury or damage. The FAC alleges Plaintiff felt fatigued and left active lanes to rest rather than continue driving. FAC ¶¶ 11–12. At minimum, those allegations preclude the motion’s assertion that unlawfulness is established on the pleadings.

Even suspected unlawful parking would not by itself authorize an evidence-gathering search of the vehicle’s interior. The motion identifies no authority holding that probable cause for a shoulder-parking infraction permits opening a closed passenger door to investigate the occupant or contents.

#### 4. The cup hypothesis does not establish lawful entry as a matter of law.

The FAC alleges that, from outside the vehicle, Melano said, “I think it’s booze.” FAC ¶ 15. That tentative observation may support inquiry, but the motion treats it as conclusive proof both that alcohol was present and that Plaintiff had driven under the influence. Mot. 8–10. The pleaded facts include no officer observation of driving. The cup’s nature was unknown before entry. And the motion itself states that Defendants did not learn what the cup contained until after searching the vehicle. Mot. 8. Defendants cannot bootstrap the fruits of the intrusion into justification for its inception.

At minimum, the circumstances and reasonable inferences are disputed. Count IV should proceed.

### C. Count V Plausibly Alleges an Unlawful Vehicle Search

Count V concerns conduct beyond merely opening the door. It alleges that Melano reached into the vehicle, seized the cup from the console area, smelled it, examined it, and continued searching after identifying the liquid as urine, while Meza-Gonzalez directed Plaintiff’s attention and movements. FAC ¶¶ 59–63.

Defendants largely merge this conduct with the door opening and the later arrest. But each search must be justified in scope and duration. A belief that the cup might contain alcohol before it was examined does not automatically authorize an unbounded vehicle search. Once the officer allegedly handled the cup and identified the suspected substance as urine, the factual basis supplied by that particular observation had changed. See *Sialoi*, 823 F.3d at 1233 (officers must account for information that dissipates the basis for a seizure). The FAC alleges no independent exception for continued warrantless searching at that moment.

Plaintiff does not argue that an unlawful initial search automatically invalidates every later action or every item of damages. See *Lingo*, 832 F.3d at 959–60. The point is narrower: the physical vehicle intrusions caused a completed privacy injury and must be evaluated on their own facts and timing. Count V therefore survives even if later-developed facts ultimately bear on the arrest.

The motion also cannot establish probable cause by treating the disputed odor, speech, and orientation descriptions as true. Nor can it use unknown field-exercise results or later-acquired information to support earlier search conduct. Count V plausibly alleges a warrantless search without consent, probable cause, or another exception.

### D. Count III Plausibly Alleges Arrest Without Probable Cause

Probable cause depends on the totality of facts known to the officers at the time of arrest. *Devenpeck*, 543 U.S. at 152. The Court must consider inculpatory and exculpatory circumstances rather than divide the facts or ignore information that weakens the asserted basis. See *Sialoi*, 823 F.3d at 1232–33.

The motion’s probable-cause conclusion depends on resolving factual disputes in Defendants’ favor. It assumes that Plaintiff displayed signs of intoxication, including odor and slurred speech. Mot. 9–10. The FAC specifically alleges that the sworn descriptions were false or materially contradicted by Plaintiff’s coherent speech and orientation. FAC ¶¶ 20, 25–28, 50. Whether an odor existed cannot be determined from an audio transcript alone, but neither may Defendants’ challenged report be accepted as true merely because Defendants offer an innocent explanation.

The remaining pleaded facts do not compel probable cause. Plaintiff was resting in a stationary vehicle after pulling off the roadway because he was fatigued; no officer saw him drive. FAC ¶¶ 11–12. A cup initially suspected to contain alcohol was identified by the investigating officer as urine. Id. ¶¶ 17–18. Some field exercises were not administered because of ground conditions, and the FAC does not admit inculpatory results from those that were performed. Id. ¶ 19. Plaintiff spoke coherently and identified his location. Id. ¶ 20. And refusal of consent to an investigative procedure cannot itself create the suspicion or probable cause otherwise absent. See *Thomas*, 818 F.3d at 884–85.

Defendants respond that “a reasonable person could determine” probable cause existed. Mot. 10. But the Rule 12 question is whether the FAC plausibly alleges that it did not. It does. At most, Defendants identify competing inferences for a developed record.

The later detention certificate and declination of charges do not, by themselves, prove a Fourth Amendment violation, as the FAC acknowledges. FAC ¶¶ 32–34, 51. They are pleaded as corroborative, not as substitutes for the facts known at arrest. The claim rests on the officers’ alleged knowledge at the time.

Finally, Count III alleges each officer’s role. Meza-Gonzalez effected the arrest. Melano performed the entry, cup examination, and field exercises, communicated with Meza-Gonzalez, and remained actively engaged through the arrest. FAC ¶ 53. Whether that conduct satisfies integral-participation principles is fact dependent; it is more than mere presence.

### E. Count I Should Not Be Dismissed With Prejudice

A judicial-deception plaintiff must allege that an officer deliberately or recklessly made false statements or omissions in a warrant application and that the deception was material to probable cause. *Chism*, 661 F.3d at 386. Intent may be alleged through particular facts and reasonable inference; no extra heightened motive pleading applies merely because the claim is brought under section 1983. See *Galbraith*, 307 F.3d at 1126; *Benavidez*, 993 F.3d at 1146–48.

The FAC identifies Meza-Gonzalez, the incident, the documents he allegedly executed, the challenged descriptions, the omitted circumstances, the blood-draw warrant, and the resulting bodily intrusion. FAC ¶¶ 25–30, 38–42. Defendants’ response is factual: perhaps the odor existed without being spoken; perhaps coherent conversation was still slurred; perhaps Plaintiff’s demonstrated orientation did not contradict “very confused and disoriented.” Mot. 5–6. Those possibilities may be litigated, but they do not entitle Defendants to have their inferences selected at the pleading stage.

Materiality likewise cannot be defeated by treating every challenged observation as true and using unpleaded field-exercise results. A corrected-affidavit inquiry removes alleged falsehoods, adds material omissions, and then evaluates what remains. *Chism*, 661 F.3d at 389. The motion instead preserves the disputed observations and fills a pleading silence against Plaintiff.

Plaintiff recognizes that ECF 5 imprecisely identifies which post-arrest documents were submitted to the issuing judge and misdescribes one CHP form label. Plaintiff does not ask the Court to treat that wording as accurate or to amend the FAC through this brief. Those errors do not support dismissal with prejudice. If the Court finds the present allegations insufficiently particular, Plaintiff requests leave to identify the actual warrant affidavit and challenge the precise statements and omissions within it, as detailed in Section VI. That targeted correction is the opposite of futility.

### F. Count II Is Not Barred Merely Because No Criminal Charge Was Filed

Defendants’ categorical premise—that deliberate fabrication is actionable only if it results in a criminal charge, prosecution, or conviction—is incorrect. Mot. 7. *Caldwell* held that being charged was sufficient on the facts there; it did not hold that a criminal charge is invariably necessary. And a model jury instruction is not binding authority restricting the Constitution to criminal cases.

The Ninth Circuit has recognized that deliberate fabrication in civil proceedings can violate due process when it causes deprivation of a protected liberty or property interest. *Costanich*, 627 F.3d at 1108. The decisive questions are deliberate fabrication, use, a protected deprivation, and causation—not whether a prosecutor filed a criminal complaint.

The FAC alleges more than a failed prosecution. It alleges that Meza-Gonzalez knowingly made false or misleading sworn statements based on an encounter in which he personally participated; that the statements were used for a compelled blood draw, continued confinement, and an administrative-per-se proceeding; and that they caused or materially contributed to a one-year suspension of Plaintiff’s commercial driving privilege and resulting economic injury. FAC ¶¶ 36–37, 43–47. Those allegations identify distinct liberty and property consequences.

Defendants call Count II duplicative but do not analyze those separately alleged uses and injuries. To the extent one use overlaps Count I’s warrant theory, that does not eliminate the alleged administrative deprivation at the pleading stage. Rule 8 permits alternative theories, and duplicative recovery can be prevented later without dismissing a potentially valid claim now.

At minimum, *Costanich* forecloses dismissal with prejudice based solely on the absence of criminal charges. If the Court requires greater detail concerning the precise statement used in the DMV process or the causal chain to the suspension, Plaintiff should receive leave to plead it.

### G. Count VI Plausibly Alleges But-For Causation

Defendants challenge Count VI on causation. Mot. 11–12. The FAC alleges more than temporal proximity. It alleges that Meza-Gonzalez advised that the PAS was voluntary, then said that refusal would leave it to his discretion whether to arrest Plaintiff for DUI. FAC ¶¶ 21–22, 66–68. Plaintiff refused, and the arrest followed immediately, without an intervening articulated observation. Id. ¶¶ 23–24, 67–68.

That combination—a statement expressly linking the arrest decision to consent, followed by refusal and immediate arrest—plausibly supports an inference that refusal made a difference to the decision. Defendants’ contrary explanation, that Meza-Gonzalez was merely explaining lawful discretion and would have arrested Plaintiff anyway, is a competing factual inference. Mot. 11–12. It is not an admitted fact and cannot eliminate causation at Rule 12.

Plaintiff need not prove the ultimate motive in the complaint. The question is whether the pleaded statement and sequence make causation plausible. They do. The motion should therefore be denied on the causation ground actually presented. Alternatively, any timing imprecision can be corrected through the specific proffer in Section VI.

### H. Qualified Immunity Does Not Support Blanket Dismissal

Qualified immunity must be analyzed claim by claim and on the facts alleged in Plaintiff’s favor. See *al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. at 735; *Rosenbaum*, 663 F.3d at 1075–76. The motion instead repeats its merits conclusion that probable cause existed and then asserts, at a high level, that no clearly established law prohibited “the circumstances as pled.” Mot. 12–13.

For the door opening, *Ngumezi* clearly established before November 2024 that opening a passenger door and leaning into a vehicle to obtain information constitutes a search. 980 F.3d at 1289. The warrant requirement and recognized-exception framework were likewise settled. Whether emergency aid or another exception objectively applied depends on the disputed facts; Defendants cannot obtain immunity by substituting “unconscious” for the FAC’s resting-driver allegations.

For the cup seizure and continued search, the clearly established inquiry likewise depends on what Defendants knew and on the scope of each intrusion. The motion does not address the completed privacy injury separately from the later arrest.

For the arrest, no one disputes that an arrest requires probable cause. Whether officers could reasonably—but mistakenly—believe probable cause existed depends on which contested observations are credited. A Rule 12 dismissal is inappropriate where immunity turns on disputed facts concerning what the officers saw, heard, knew, or fabricated. See *Rosenbaum*, 663 F.3d at 1078–79.

For judicial deception, the prohibition on deliberately or recklessly misleading a judicial officer through material warrant statements was clearly established long before this incident. *Chism*, 661 F.3d at 393; *Benavidez*, 993 F.3d at 1151–52. An officer is not immunized at the pleading stage merely by denying falsity or materiality.

And deliberate fabrication cannot be converted into protected reasonable error by accepting Defendants’ disputed factual account. See *Costanich*, 627 F.3d at 1114–16. If the Court concludes that any claim presents a distinct clearly-established-law defect, the proper ruling must identify that claim and conduct rather than dismiss all six claims wholesale.

### I. Litigation History Does Not Establish Futility

Defendants devote their futility argument to other lawsuits and characterize Plaintiff’s filing decisions as a strategy to evade adverse rulings. Mot. 13–14. That rhetoric does not show that the amendments proposed here would fail as a matter of law.

The motion identifies a report and recommendation in a voluntarily dismissed Eastern District action, a dismissed state action, and a separate pending matter. It does not establish that a final judgment in another action adjudicated the particular proposed amendments or that ordinary claim-preclusion requirements are satisfied. Nor does experience as a pro se litigant remove the liberal-amendment rule.

The relevant question is whether amendment could cure an identified pleading defect. *Garity*, 828 F.3d at 854; *Lopez*, 203 F.3d at 1130–31. Section VI supplies concrete, evidence-based corrections to the door sequence, document identity, form wording, search chronology, PAS-to-arrest timing, causation allegations, and individual roles. Defendants’ litigation-history narrative does not establish futility as to those corrections.

### J. Rule 12(f) Does Not Authorize Defendants’ Requested Damages Adjudication

Rule 12(f) permits striking an insufficient defense or redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter. It is not a vehicle for dismissing a damages request on the ground that the plaintiff supposedly cannot prove entitlement. *Whittlestone*, 618 F.3d at 974–75. Defendants’ cases allowing damages allegations to be stricken cannot override controlling Ninth Circuit law.

The punitive-damages request is also tied to alleged intentional or reckless conduct. Punitive damages may be available under section 1983 when a defendant acts with evil motive or intent or with reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights. *Smith*, 461 U.S. at 56. The FAC alleges deliberate or reckless sworn deception, deliberate fabrication, a knowingly warrantless intrusion, and an arrest expressly tied to refusal of a voluntary procedure. FAC ¶¶ 38–47, 54–70. Whether Plaintiff can prove the required mental state is not properly decided by striking the prayer.

Plaintiff clarifies that he does not seek attorney’s fees for his own pro se work. See *Kay v. Ehrler*, 499 U.S. 432, 435–38 (1991). The FAC already requests fees only “to the extent recoverable.” FAC Prayer ¶ C. That clarification narrows any dispute without requiring dismissal of a claim or foreclosing fees for compensable work by counsel should counsel later appear.

## VI. ALTERNATIVE REQUEST FOR TARGETED LEAVE TO AMEND

Dismissal should be denied. If the Court finds any claim insufficient, Plaintiff requests leave to amend under Rule 15(a)(2). The request is concrete and does not depend on speculative future discovery. Plaintiff can amend as follows:

1. **Door-opening sequence.** Correct FAC paragraph 16’s statement that Plaintiff remained asleep when the door opened. Plaintiff can allege, based on the reconciled transcript and subject to confirmation against the original video, that knocks woke him before the command to open the window; Melano opened the passenger door approximately three seconds after that command, before Plaintiff had a meaningful opportunity to lower the window or consent. The constitutional theory remains a nonconsensual physical vehicle intrusion under *Ngumezi*; it does not depend on Plaintiff being asleep at the instant of entry.

2. **Actual warrant submission.** Identify the actual blood-draw warrant affidavit as a document separate from the CHP 202 report and DS-367; quote or paraphrase the exact challenged affidavit statements; identify the material omissions; allege who submitted or caused the affidavit to be submitted; and apply the corrected-affidavit test to that document rather than treating the post-arrest forms as interchangeable.

3. **CHP form wording.** Remove the inaccurate allegation that the produced CHP 202 contains a checkbox stating “Beverage Present: Yes.” The produced form instead states “BREATH ODOR OF ALCOHOLIC BEVERAGE PRESENT.” Plaintiff will not equate an odor field with a physical beverage. This correction does not concede that an odor existed, that any statement about odor was truthful, or that any search was lawful.

4. **Search chronology and scope.** Plead each physical intrusion separately, including the door opening, the cup removal and examination, and any later vehicle-area search supported by the original recording; state the information known before each act; specify when the cup was identified as urine; and identify each Defendant’s personal role without relying on collective labels.

5. **Arrest chronology.** Correct the shorthand that arrest occurred “eighteen seconds” after PAS refusal.
