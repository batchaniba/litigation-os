ABOUT THE HOPE PREPARATORY TREATMENT PROGRAM

The HOPE Preparatory Sexual Offender Treatment Program (Prep Program) is designed
for individuals who have been convicted of a sexual offense or have engaged in
inappropriate or compulsive sexual behaviors and who have realized that they need help

in addressing their behavior(s).

The Prep Program is a 24 session (12-week) open/rolling program for adult males who
have been convicted of a sexual offense. It is a cognitive-behavioral treatment program
that is primarily based on the Risk Need Responsivity (RNR) model (Andrews & Bonta,
2003). As this is a preparatory treatment program, considerable emphasis is placed on
motivation and engagement as the primary means with which to engage offenders and

prepare them for success in the core treatment program as well as discharge.

In the Prep Program, it is essential that participants be introduced to the goals of treatment,
what they should expect from treatment, the theoretical orientation of treatment, and that
they understand and build on motivational aspects of the assessments conducted with
them. In addition, as research indicates, at the outset of treatment, group facilitators must
build a strong therapeutic/working alliance with the group members. A number of studies
have demonstrated that the therapeutic alliance predicts outcome and is a fundamental
tool for achieving client change (Martin et al., 2000; Marshall et al., 2011; Norcross, 2002),
and that this is essential to ultimate outcome.

In general, the group facilitator should adopt a reinforcing and encouraging approach,
while also being firm and setting limits (see appendix for effective group and therapist
characteristics). This style should be evident throughout the treatment program but is
particularly important at the beginning stages of therapy when clients may have low self-
efficacy and some resistance to change. Motivational enhancement strategies are
essential at this stage of treatment. It is also important that participants invest in the
treatment and group process in order to encourage openness and disclosure and to allow
participants to respectfully challenge each other. Lastly, it is essential that participants
understand how this treatment program can benefit them and help them to achieve their
goals in life.

It is essential that the facilitator convey the Good Lives approach as well as other relevant
modalities utilized in this program. An essential message to convey is what participants
will gain in their lives by participating in treatment; that is, how will they benefit from
treatment, beyond managing their risk to re-offend. In brief, this program is designed to
help participants to both not to commit a new sexual offence and to help them discover
how to have a better life in areas outside sexual offending.

The Prep Program is conceptualized as having two phases. Phase 1 is directed at
providing individuals with the knowledge of HOPE Program policy and procedures as well
as relevant treatment concepts that will prepare them for success in the subsequent
program and following discharge. Phase 2 is primarily directed at motivating and engaging
clients, establishing appropriate group climate, and beginning the development of an
effective therapeutic relationship. Initial issues discussed should be non-threatening
although a gradual degree of supportive challenging is recommended.

The initial issues to be addressed include a description of the rules of the group, an outline
of the program (both the Prep and Core treatment curriculum), and an indication of the
general findings of the effectiveness of sexual offender treatment. The subsequent targets
of the Prep program include the Good Lives Model, a cost-benefit analysis, an
autobiography, and recognition of immediate offense triggers. Relevant topics are

included in this manual for discussion amongst the group.

** As this is an open/rolling group, whenever a new client starts the Prep Program,
the facilitator should spend a sufficient proportion of the session introducing the
new group member to the other clients, the curriculum, and the relevant policies
and procedures. After introductions, an overview of the prep and core programs
should be presented, as well as the group rules and how to give and receive
feedback. It is strongly recommended that the facilitator have the group members
explain these various concepts to the new member and then missing/relevant
information can be supplemented by the facilitator.

Phase 1 represents the introductory steps in treatment. Although it is primarily
psychoeducational, it is important to note that the initial phase of any treatment program
should be focused on winning the confidence and trust of the client and establishing a
strong therapeutic alliance. The content of these initial sessions needs to be presented

every time a new group member stats the program.

During the initial session, the facilitator welcomes everyone to the HOPE Program and
introduces the overall HOPE approach toward treatment. It is essential to note that while
this program targets risk for sexual offending, it is much broader in scope and is designed
to help participants to not commit a sexual offense and help them have a better, more
fulfilling life in areas outside of sexual offending. Evidence of the effectiveness of treatment
should be presented. There have been several large-scale meta-analyses of treatment
outcome with sexual offenders with virtually identical results. Over a 5-year follow-up
period, official recidivism data revealed re-offense rates of approximately 9% for the
treated group versus 18% for the untreated group. Some specific programs have even
shown better rates. Other programs that emphasize motivational and process, such as the

current program have shown even better results, such as 3.2% for treated sexual

MODULE 2: Types of Groups and Feedback

Preparatory Treatment Program

Key Learning Points/Module Goals
e Assist individuals in being able to understand the importance of the group process
and group dynamics
e Familiarize individuals with the various types of group dynamics that can form
during the group process
e To orient clients on how to give and receive feedback

Rationale

The purpose of this module is for group members to gain an understanding of the group
process and how different types of group dynamics may form amongst members, and the
pros and cons of each group dynamic. The Barn Raising group is the most beneficial for
individual and group growth as each member is supportive but constructively challenging
to other members of the group. In this group dynamic, members are held accountable for
their cognitive distortions and high-risk behaviors. Thus, it is important for individuals to
understand what behaviors support growth among members.

It is also fundamental to a good group on how best to provide and receive feedback. This
will serve as the foundation of the two core assignments presented later in this manual
and is the primary mechanism of change.

Types of Groups

1. Free-For-All Group

In the Free-For-All Group, everyone is out for themselves. They feel scared to talk or to
be vulnerable, and they do not trust one another or the facilitators. They do not want to
participate or give the facilitators what they want, so they remain quiet or give meaningless
answers. Everyone is competing to be the best, the coolest, the least involved. Anyone
who participates is mocked or shunned.

Pros: Ego boosting when a person feels like a “winner,” people get to protect themselves

from examination or criticism, safety from vulnerability.

Cons: Hostility or lack of participation can be obstacles to getting help from the group.
The group feels unsafe, there is no group cohesion therefore no one works together or

2. Beauty Contest Group

In the Beauty Contest Group, everyone understands what the facilitators want and try to
give it to them. People compete to be the “favorite” of the facilitators. People try to give
“correct” answers to questions and show off their knowledge. People who do not give

correct answers are mocked. People who do not participate are shunned.

Pros: Good flow of conversation; people want to talk instead of remaining silent; people
care what others in the group think about them and their answers; people understand what
the point of the group is.

Cons: Answers are superficial and given to get praise rather than to express honesty;
people avoid negative experiences and try to focus only on positive statements; problems
are not examined in depth or solved effectively.

3. Club Group

In the Club Group, everyone wants to give positive feedback and get positive feedback in
return. People praise one another and point out each other's strengths and successes.
Each person gets a turn in the spotlight and gets praised by the group. People who bring
up negatives or who ruin the good mood are dismissed and the group quickly shifts the
focus back to “positive” feelings. People who express anger or dislike towards peers are
shunned. People who don't participate are defended by peers.

Pros: Acceptance from the entire group towards each member; feeling of being a part of
group; offering of support to everyone; plenty of socialization and friendliness; support
network provided.

Cons: People are nonchalant about actual problems; each member gives praise selfishly
in order to receive it back; people are afraid to go deeper into issues; group culture
prevents bringing up or examining problems; peer pressure may keep people from
speaking up about negative issues; people do not challenge one another or hold one
another accountable.

4. Barn Raising Group

In the Barn Raising Group, everyone works together towards a common goal. Each
person takes a part of the task of helping each other person, and no one carries the entire
group alone. Each person understands what is being asked of them and takes a turn
being examined by the others. Each person is held accountable by the group and their
distortions or blind spots are pointed out. When people feel unhappy or badly treated,
they bring it up and everyone gets to understand why they feel that way and what can be
done about it. Solutions to problems are discussed. The facilitators are seen as allies
rather than enemies. People who don't participate are questioned and held accountable.

Pros: Genuine support is offered to each member; alternatives to problematic behaviors
are explored; help from everyone increases chances of accomplishing goals; people are
encouraged to be genuine and honest.

Cons: Each group member must take more risks, everyone must do more work, each
person must allow themselves to be vulnerable. People must face up to negative facts
rather than ignoring or sugar-coating them; honesty creates opportunity for conflict which
must be resolved; each person bears responsibility for their own well-being as well as
participating in the group.

Types of Risk Assessments and Risk Factors

Clinicians should introduce the concept of risk assessment and present information related
to risk factors (static, stable, and acute). The reason that we use these tools is because
no one is very good at predicting what another person is going to do just based on
our impressions or knowledge of that person. No risk assessment tool is perfect, but
the ones we use are designed to be combined so that we get a better picture of someone’s
risk for re-offense. Identifying risks factors will also identify protective factors (strengths)
that can help decrease a client's risk. This information will help clients and therapists in
understanding the best way to address specific treatment needs and to create a focused
treatment plan.

Risk Assessments

The following static and dynamic risk assessments are used by the HOPE Program to
identify a client's treatment needs. An agency called SARATSO (State Authorized Risk
Assessment Tools for Sexual Offenders) has identified some tools that we have to use
with every client. We use additional risk assessment tools to get a clearer picture of each

client's needs and treatment goals.

STATIC-99R

The Static-99R is a tool designed to assist in predicting the risk of sexual and violent re-
offending. It’s referred to as ‘static’ because it scores people in areas where little, if any
change is possible. This tool is scored using paperwork (rap sheets, probation reports,
court documents, etc.) rather than from gathering information from clients through
interviews. The instrument is used all over the world and has been tested over and over
again to make sure that it is an accurate predictor of risk for re-offense.

In California, the Static-99R is scored by trained employees in prisons and jails. Everyone
who has been convicted of a sexual offense is scored on the Static-99R before they leave
custody, no matter how long ago the offense occurred or why the person was in prison
this time. A client's Static-99R risk score was calculated while in custody and given to
treatment providers to assist with risk assessment. The Static-99R has shown moderate
accuracy (better than clinical judgment but not perfect) in ranking clients according to their
risk for sexual re-offense. It's accuracy in assessing risk has been consistent across a
wide variety of groups of people, in many different countries and in many different settings.

The STABLE-2007 is another risk assessment tool that can help us predict the level of

estimate risk of sexual re-offense. Treatment providers use the STABLE-2007 as one way
to identify treatment goals.

There are 13 areas that get scored on the STABLE-2007:

Significant Social Influences - Who is the client close to? Are those relationships
positive (healthy, non-criminal), negative (unhealthy, potentially pro-criminal), or neutral
(neither positive nor negative)?

Capacity for Relationship Stability — Has the person lived with a romantic partner
continuously for two or more years? Is the person currently in a relationship? If so, is the
relationship healthy or unhealthy? If the person is not in a relationship, is he lonely?

Emotional Identification with Children —This is scored only for clients who had at least
one victim who was 13 years of age or under. Does this client prefer the company of
children to that of adults? Is s/he more comfortable talking to kids because they are less
judgmental and more open? Does this client lack adult friends?

Hostility Towards Women — Does the client have female friends who he is not sexually
involved with? What does the client think about women in positions of power? Does the
client believe that women in general are not to be trusted and go out of their ways to get
men in trouble? This question is looking for generalized hostility towards women, not just

anger at a specific woman.

General Social Rejection and Loneliness — Is the client isolated and lonely? Does s/he
feel rejected by his or her family or community? Does this client have difficulty making and
keeping healthy friendships and relationships?

Lack of Concern for Others — Does the client express concern about the needs and
feelings of others? Does he take those needs and feelings into account when making
decisions and acting? Does the client have a group of people that he feels close to and
cares about, and whose needs have an influence on his choices?

Impulsive Acts — Is this client prone to being impulsive in multiple areas of his life? Does
he demonstrate difficulty with thinking something through before he acts? Does he seek
out high-risk/high-thrill activities? Does he have a history of quitting jobs without having
another one lined up? Does the client get into frequent fights? This item is looking for
generalized impulsivity, not just impulsive behavior in one area of his life.

Self-Regulation Model

The Self-Regulation Model is different from the traditional Relapse Prevention model in
two important ways. Instead of focusing completely on avoiding high-risk thoughts,
feelings, or behaviors, the Self-Regulation Model focuses on developing goals for living a
positive, crime-free life while also paying attention to risk factors. Also, the Self-Regulation
Model differs from Relapse Prevention by showing that there can be multiple pathways to
offending and different strategies used to meet negative goals. This differs from Relapse

Prevention, which only looks at one pathway.

The Self-Regulation Model uses avoidance, like Relapse Prevention, but it is also
approach-oriented, meaning that it helps people move TOWARD (approach) something
they want.

Good Lives Model

While both the Relapse Prevention and the Self-Regulation approaches to treatment for
people who have committed sexual offenses require that people look BACK at their
offenses and learn to understand the risk factors (thoughts, feelings, fantasies, areas of
personal challenge, and situations) that resulted in a sexual offense, the Good Lives Model
focuses more on looking FORWARD. The model is strength-based (meaning that it helps
people identify their strengths rather than just looking at challenges) and is based on the
idea that people who commit sexual offenses may be violators of human rights but also
have human rights themselves. The model suggests that humans require positive
things/experiences in a variety of areas of life in order to feel whole. Those areas include
Life/Healthy Living, Knowledge, Excellence in Work and Play, Excellence in Agency (self-
determination and self-agency), Peace of Mind, Relatedness (family/friends), Community,
Spirituality, Happiness, and Creativity. The model helps clients understand that their
needs in the areas above are normal but that the ways in which they tried to meet some
of those needs were harmful to others and themselves.

The HOPE Program uses the Good Lives model to help clients and therapists understand
and evaluate what risk factors might be getting in the way of clients’ abilities to have
positive things in the areas of Human Good. That understanding is used to help clients set
goals in those areas and develop prosocial (non-criminal) strategies for achieving those
goals in order to increase the good things in their lives. We expect that by teaching clients
how to acquire positive things (Good Lives Goals) in non-criminal, non-harmful ways, we
will encourage clients to attain their Good Lives Goals without harming or sexually
offending against others (Yates, Kingston, and Ward, 2009).

The combination of Relapse Prevention, Self-Regulation, and Good Lives Model in
treatment helps clients recognize, understand, and if necessary, change thoughts,
feelings, and behaviors that put them at risk for committing another sexual offense, while
at the same time, giving them tools to live a happier, healthier life. In other words, the
program addresses client's risk while helping them improve their lives.

The Containment Model and Laws Governing People Convicted of Committing a
Sexual Offense

The Containment Model was created to increase community safety by helping Parole
Agents and Probation Officers work together with treatment providers to provide the most
effective assessment, treatment, and supervision strategies for clients based on their level
of risk for committing another sexual offense. The model is based in research that helps
us figure out what works best in reducing recidivism. Basing what we do on research is
really important — we need to be able to prove that what we do works. We know that if we
can support each of you in getting through the treatment program, we can help you reduce
your risk for re-offense by up to 50%!

It is a collaborative approach to working with people convicted of sexual offenses after
they’ve been paroled into the community. On July 1, 2012, the Containment Model
became mandatory in California. This means that all Parole units and all County Probation
officers, along with all certified treatment programs, are required to use this model when
working with clients who have been convicted of a sexual offense. The Containment Model
requires programs to have standards; such as, training and experience in sex offender
specific treatment. Such standards ensure that treatment professionals are qualified and
use an effective, evidence-based approach to sex offender specific treatment. You'll hear
us use the term ‘evidence-based’ while you're in treatment with us. What this means is
that the material we are sharing with you has been shown to help people reduce their risk
for re-offense. We'll explain more about this when we talk about how we assess each
person’s risk for re-offense.

The Containment Model requires that all clients be scored on several different risk
assessment instruments when entering sex offender treatment. We'll explain each type of

risk assessment in the next module. The new law allows the length and frequency of
attendance to be tailored to each client's risk level.

The Containment Model uses a victim-centered approach, meaning that the primary
responsibility of all parts of the containment team is community safety and reduction in
clients’ risk for re-offense. The three main people or groups who must have regular

Pre-Contemplation: During this stage individuals have not yet identified that there is a
problem with their behavior. Typically, other people are aware that the person is having
problems. However, people with behavior problems are usually so rooted in their
Treatment Readiness Block Page 21 of 43 behavior that they easily tum a deaf ear to the
concerns of others. A person in the precontemplation stage is “uninformed” because he
has yet to be presented with a convincing reason to change. An example might be an
individual who believes that they can stop doing heroin anytime and will not experience
any negative consequences. Another, more immediate example is someone who gets into
his car drunk thinking “I can drive fine when | am drunk.” In both of these examples, the
individual is not even considering that there is a behavior problem.

Contemplation: Once the individual enters into a dialog (even a healthy debate) regarding
the merits of his behavior and considers the possibility of change, he has entered the
contemplation stage of change. At this stage of change the individual often vacillates
between the idea of changing and defending his old behavior. Despite the desire, some
clients feel that they are unable to change and that their old behavior is their only choice.
However, the ability to contemplate change allows the change process to be owned or
personalized as opposed to being imposed. If an individual is not convinced that changing
is better than staying the same any change that is made is not likely to be true or long
standing. Deciding whether to change or not is usually determined through an evaluation
of the pros and cons of changing. This is called DECISIONAL BALANCE. In other words,
which way do the scales tip, toward changing or not changing? There are two ways to
determine decisional balance. The first is doing a “cost —benefit analysis” regarding the
behavior.

Preparation (“Getting Ready”): In the preparation stage, the individual is no longer
ambivalent about making changes and makes serious efforts to develop strategies to
correct behavior. At this point, a client is seriously thinking about change and also
beginning to take some action. In effect, he is ready for change as he has resolved the
indecisiveness of the contemplation stage. The individual can visualize goals and success.
Individuals may express a desire for direction and insight from others. During the
preparation stage individuals may experiment with change processes such as “trying out”
a group or making mild disclosures. In some cases, individuals will report past attempts at
changing and explore the lessons learned from these past attempts (DiClemente et al.,
2001). This is a turning point at which the person decides to change or quit the behavior.

In order to approach this stage in a conscious and useful way, the individual must be able
to identify what it is about their lifestyle that they want to change. According to this model,
it is important to understand that the individual doesn’t need to change every aspect of his
life. It is important however, to identify strengths and problem areas. The individual must
view the goal as conducive to his capabilities, needs, and values. He also benefits best
when he sees the goal as reasonable. If the goal is unreasonable to the client, or beyond
his personal expectations he is likely to become discouraged and fail.

Action: Graduation to the action stage is measured by observable lifestyle changes. This
includes an obvious investment in the change program as well as concrete changes in
one’s everyday life. The action stage challenges individuals to abstain from past behavior
and adopt new ways of being. In the case of clients who have engaged in sexually abusive
behaviors, this would mean cessation of high-risk pre- Treatment Readiness Block Page
22 of 43 offense offence behaviors. Such changes would likely relate directly to the
variables that contributed to problematic behaviors.

at replacing undesirable behavior with healthy positive behavior. However, this stage also
acknowledges that there is a history of problematic behavior and steps need to be made
to foster ongoing beneficial behaviors. Like a good running car, maintenance is required
to assure both its current wellbeing and longevity. Relapse into problematic behavior

that you have intemalized what you have learned and integrate it into your life. This
treatment program cannot “cure” you, so therapy will not work for you unless you are
willing to make a commitment to put effort into it.

Remember that you can, if you want to, make the most of what is available here for
yourself. You can come out of treatment — as many men have before you — a changed
man, a man who feels a lot different inside than you do now, a man really in control of
himself and his life, a man who feels some pride in himself and what he has accomplished.
It is up to you.

Goal Setting/GLM

Facilitators should discuss the importance of goals with the group and link it back to the earlier
goal setting exercise.

Review the GLM good live goals and the goals identified on the Wheel of Life and discuss the
difference between short-term goals and long-term goals. Generate a discussion with group

the difference between avoidance goals and approach goals. Clients often frame their goals as
avoidance-based (i.e., things they should not do) such as avoid children and certain places. While
this is appropriate and deserves some attention, considerable discussion needs to be placed on
approach goals (i.e., things they will do). Examples of approach goals are a) establish a healthy
relationship and b) establish a group of prosocial friends.

immediately achievable, practicing achieving these smaller goals, and keeping track of successes
and difficulties so these can be discussed, and strategies and skills developed over time. These
should also be expanded throughout the program as participants generate new ways to achieve

Autobiography and Life Analysis Assignment
Each client is asked to describe their life, including who he is now and how he came
to be the person he is today. These assignments are completed between sessions.
They can talk/write about anything they want but try to get them to include some of
the positive things about their life as well as some of the negative things. Focus, in
particular, on thoughts and feelings that they experienced at the times they are writing about. This
exercise allows clients to identify with each other and become more aware of treatment targets
that may be important for them to address in treatment. In addition, it allows clients to share
personal details of their life history in a safe, supportive environment.

There really is no right way or wrong way to give an autobiography — it is their life. However, some
discussion on childhood, relationships, major life events, and work history can help for identifying
areas for development and planning for the future. If they do not mention all of these areas of life,
ask them about them. Often what people leave out is as, or more, important than what they
include. This exercise should be presented by the client in his own words and he should read this
from his prepared written work.

Once the client has read his autobiography to the group, he should be provided with positive
feedback from everyone, including the therapist. The therapist should focus on motivating and
supporting each client during these discussions, which will facilitate trust and treatment
engagement. These issues should be the primary focus, particularly in this early stage of
treatment.

Offense Disclosure Module/Assignment
In this assignment, participants provide a disclosure of the sexual offence(s) that
resulted in their incarceration and/or referral to this program. The purpose is to
identify the affective, behavioral, cognitive, and contextual factors that culminate
in the occurrence of a sexual offence and to assist clients in taking responsibility
for their actions in offending. These sessions also allow clients to receive
feedback from others in terms of their motivations and dynamics involved in their offence(s).

The purpose is to have each client identify the problems or other issues that occurred in the period
just prior to his offense and that appeared to precipitate the offense. Taken together, the two
assignments in this program (autobiography and offense disclosure) will begin to elucidate the

distal and immediate factors that precipitate a sexual offense for that individual.

Facilitators should display the offense progression on the wall to discuss the relationship between
distal and more immediate factors (see appendix).

Although some degree of supportive challenging is appropriate, it is fundamental that at this stage
in the program, individuals are reinforced for their participation and their willingness to present
any issue about the offense. It is also important to note that, as facilitators, we are most interested
in the circumstances surrounding the offense (i.e., background/immediate factors) rather than the
specific details of the offense itself.

** For individuals who deny their sexual offense, ask them to discuss what they were charged
with. A discussion can then ensue about the circumstances surrounding when they were accused
of the offense.

APPENDICES

Therapeutic Processes

Recent research examining therapeutic processes with sexual offenders has shown that several

features enhance the effectiveness of treatment.

|. Therapist features: empathy, warmth, rewardingness, directiveness, flexibility, and non-
confrontational challenging

e Empathy: An empathic therapist is viewed positively by clients who accept his/her advice.
Since we want our clients to develop empathy we must model it.

e Warmth: A warm and supportive therapist is viewed positively by clients who are then
more likely to listen to and follow the therapist's advice.

e Rewardingness: Any signs of movement by the client in the desired direction should be
encouraged as should reports of satisfactory between-sessions work.

e Directiveness: Clients should be encouraged to find their own solutions and to recognize
their own problems but there are times when some guidance is required.

e Flexibility: This allows the therapist to meet responsivity needs. Each client has a different
learning style as well as a different personal style, and the therapist needs to adjust his/her
style to meet these differences. Similarly, each client may present at each session in a
different mood or in a more or less cooperative mood, and again the therapist needs to
adapt to this.

e Nonconfrontation: An aggressively confrontational style is not only perceived as
denigrating by clients it also tends to make them respond in kind. A supportive but firm
challenging style generates change.

ll. Group climate: The two major features of the group climate that need to be achieved for
maximum effectiveness are cohesiveness and expressiveness.

e Cohesiveness: When clients are supportive of one another while at the same time
appropriately challenging, the goals of treatment will be met.
e Expressiveness: Having each client fully participate in all discussions and being free to

express their emotions significantly influences outcome.
