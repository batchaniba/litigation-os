 1   II.   A MOTION TO DISMISS IS APPROPRIATE

 2         A defendant may assert a defense that a complaint served upon him fails to state a claim

 3   upon which relief may be granted by way of a motion pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure

 4   12(b)(6). The purpose of a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) is to test the legal sufficiency of

 5   the claims asserted in the complaint. Navarro v. Black, 250 F.3d 729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001).

 6   Although a complaint does not need to state detailed factual allegations, “a plaintiff’s obligation

 7   to provide the grounds of his ‘entitlement to relief’ requires more than labels and conclusions, and

 8   a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v.

 9   Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). A complaint that alleges “naked assertions” that are “devoid

10   a further factual enhancement” will not suffice. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 667 (2009).

11   Although generally in reviewing a motion to dismiss the court must accept as true all allegations

12   contained in the subject Complaint, “threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action,

13   supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id. at 678.

14                                                  ARGUMENT

15   I.    THE FAC FAILS TO STATE A VALID CLAIM FOR JUDICIAL DECEPTION

16         To state a claim for judicial deception, a plaintiff must show that the investigator “made

17   deliberately false statements or recklessly disregarded the truth in the affidavit” and that the

18   falsifications were “material” to the finding of probable cause. Galbraith v. Cnty. of Santa Clara,

19   307 F.3d 1119, 1126 (9th Cir. 2002). A plaintiff must allege, and ultimately establish, that

20   “without the dishonestly included or omitted information, the magistrate would not have issued

21   the warrant. Thus, the plaintiff must establish that the remaining information in the affidavit is

22   insufficient to support probable cause. Hervey v. Estes, 65 F.3d 784, 789 (9th Cir. 1995), as

23   amended on denial of reh'g (Dec. 5, 1995).

24               Plaintiff alleges there are four inconsistencies between the video of the incident and

25   the contents of Defendant Meza-Gonzalez’s report as the basis for his claim of judicial deception.

26   Despite Plaintiff’s unsupported assertions to the contrary, none of the factual allegations within

27   the FAC plausibly allege that Defendant Meza-Gonzalez deliberately made false statements to the

28   magistrate judge.

 7   claim based upon implication or speculation alone, especially when equally or more likely

10   the line between possibility and plausibility” and will not survive a Motion to Dismiss. Id.

12         Similarly, Plaintiff denies that his speech was “slurred” because video footage shows him

13   engaging in “coherent conversation, spelling his name letter by letter and discussing the

14   mechanical details of his rental vehicle.” (ECF No. 5, FAC, ¶ 26, p.8:5-7). None of these

15   allegations are inconsistent with slurred speech. Plaintiff also denies that he was “confused and

16   disoriented” because Plaintiff claims that he was able to correctly identify his location and read

17   “distant” signage at the time of his arrest. (Id. at ¶ 26, p. 8:9.) These allegations are irrelevant.

18   Plaintiff’s subjective disagreement with Defendant Meza-Gonzalez’s determination that Plaintiff

22         Finally, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant Meza-Gonzalez incorrectly stated that there were

25   totality of the circumstances, whether beverages were present in Plaintiff’s vehicle is not material

26   to establishing probable cause for Plaintiff’s blood draw. The FAC admits that Defendants

27   administered field sobriety tests prior to placing Plaintiff under arrest. (Id. at ¶ 53 p.16:9-11).

28   Notably, the FAC is conspicuously silent as to the results of those tests.

 1   II.   THE FAC FAILS TO STATE A VALID CLAIM FOR FABRICATION OF EVIDENCE ON
           THE ADDITIONAL GROUND THAT PLAINTIFF WAS NEVER CHARGED WITH ANY
 2         CRIME.

 3         Plaintiff’s second cause of action for fabrication of evidence is duplicative of his first cause

 4   of action for judicial deception and fails for the same reasons stated above. However, Plaintiff’s

 5   fabrication of evidence claim also fails on the additional ground that Plaintiff cannot show that

 6   fabricated evidence was used to criminally charge, prosecute, or convict him because the FAC

 7   admits that no criminal charges were ever filed due to the incident. (ECF No. 5, FAC, ¶ 34 p.10.)

 8               The Ninth Circuit has held that a Section 1983 plaintiff need not be convicted based

 9   on fabricated evidence to have suffered a deprivation of liberty, and that being criminally charged

10   is enough. Caldwell v. City and County of San Francisco, 889 F.3d 1105 (2018). The Manual of

11   Model Civil Jury Instructions for the Ninth Circuit’s (Model Instructions) sets the standard jury

12   instructions in the Ninth Circuit. The model instruction for fabrication of evidence states, in

13   pertinent part, that “the Fourteenth Amendment protects against being subjected to criminal

14   charges on the basis of false evidence that was deliberately fabricated.” See Model Instructions

15   No.9.38 (emphasis added). Since Plaintiff was never subjected to criminal charges due to

16   evidence presented by Defendant Meza-Gonzalez, Plaintiff’s cause of action for fabrication of

17   evidence must be dismissed with prejudice.

18   III. PLAINTIFF’S CLAIM FOR WARRANTLESS ENTRY OF HIS VEHICLE FAILS AS
          DEFENDANTS HAD REASONABLE GROUND TO BELIEVE THAT EXIGENT
19        CIRCUMSTANCES EXISTED AND/OR THAT PLAINTIFF HAD COMMITTED A CRIME

20         Plaintiff alleges that Defendants unlawfully opened his vehicle’s door upon finding him

21   asleep in his vehicle on the side of the freeway. Plaintiff’s claim fails as Defendants had

22   reasonable grounds based upon the totality of the circumstances to believe that either Plaintiff had

23   committed a crime, or that Plaintiff may have required emergency medical assistance when

24   Defendants first encountered Plaintiff.

25         A.    It is reasonable to assume that Plaintiff needed emergency assistance upon
                 finding Plaintiff unconscious in the driver’s seat of his vehicle at the
26               beginning of the encounter.

27         The Ninth Circuit has held that law enforcement officers may enter a home or vehicle

28   without consent or a warrant if necessary during an emergency. See Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S.

 4         Here, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff was parked on the side of the freeway prior to

12         B.    It is reasonable to assume that Plaintiff committed a crime upon seeing a

13               was illegally parked on the side of the freeway
14         Assuming arguendo that exigent circumstances did not exist and the opening of the

15   passenger side door of Plaintiff’s vehicle constituted a warrantless search, Plaintiff’s claim of

16   illegal entry would fail nonetheless because Defendants had probable cause to believe that

17   Plaintiff had committed a crime. Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146, 152 (2004) (citations

18   omitted). California Vehicle Code section 21718 prohibits a person from parking their vehicle on

19   the side of the freeway except in emergency situations or when directed to do so by a law

20   enforcement officer. If Plaintiff was not suffering from a medical emergency, then

21   stopping/parking his vehicle on the side of the freeway would be a violation of Vehicle Code

22   section 21718. If the exigent circumstances exception does not apply, then Defendants would be

23   justified in opening the door of Plaintiff’s vehicle to investigate a crime.

25   upon seeing what appeared to be booze in Plaintiff’s vehicle. California Vehicle Code section

26   23152(a) prohibits anyone from driving a vehicle while under the influence of alcohol. (Cal. Veh.

27   Code § 23152(a).) Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant originally believed that Plaintiff had

28   booze in his vehicle upon first encountering Plaintiff. (Id. at ¶ 15, p.5:10.) According to the

 6   IV.   PLAINTIFF’S CLAIM RELATED TO THE SEARCH OF HIS VEHICLE AND HIS
           SUBSEQUENT ARREST FAIL AS THERE EXISTED PROBABLY CAUSE UNDER THE
 7         FACTS AS ALLEGED IN THE FAC
 8         To establish a claim of unlawful search and seizure and and/or false arrest, Plaintiff must

 9   assert Defendants did not have probable cause to effectuate a search of Plaintiff’s vehicle and/or

10   arrest Plaintiff. “[A] warrantless arrest [or search] by a law officer is reasonable under the Fourth

11   Amendment where there is probable cause to believe that a criminal offense has been or is being

12   committed.” Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146, 152 (2004) (citations omitted). Probable cause

13   exists when a prudent person would have concluded there was a fair probability that the suspect

14   had committed a crime, based on the totality of the circumstances known to the officer. Crowe v.

15   County of San Diego, 608 F.3d 406, 434 (9th Cir. 2010). However, fair probability does not

16   require “certainty or even a preponderance of the evidence.” United States v. Gourde, 440 F.3d

17   1065, 1069 (9th Cir.2006). Whether probable cause existed on the facts known to the defendant is

18   a question of law for the court. Roberts v. McAfee, Inc., 660 F.3d 1156, 1164 (9th Cir. 2011).

19         Stripped of Plaintiff’s unsupported and conclusionary allegations of Defendants’

20   impropriety, the factual allegations in the FAC regarding the search of Plaintiff’s vehicle and

21   Plaintiff’s subsequent arrest are as follows:

22         1. Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano encounter Plaintiff while he was asleep in his

23   vehicle on the side of Interstate 580 East. (ECF No. 5, FAC 4:13-16.)

24         2. Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano observe a cup in Plaintiff’s vehicle, and Officer

 1   V.    PLAINTIFF’S FAC FAILS TO ALLEGE SUFFICIENT FACTS TO SHOW PLAUSIBLE
           “BUT-FOR” CAUSATION NECESSARY FOR A VALID CLAIM OF RETALIATION.
 2

 3         Plaintiff alleges that he was arrested in retaliation for his refusal to perform a voluntary

 4   preliminary alcohol screening test. The facts within the FAC do not support Plaintiff’s allegation

 5   that his arrest was due to retaliatory animus.

 6         To establish a viable First Amendment retaliation claim, Plaintiff must (1) allege that the

 7   alleged conduct would chill a person of ordinary firmness from future First Amendment activity

 8   and (2) allege that the officer's desire to chill his speech was a “but-for” cause of the alleged

 9   unlawful conduct. Ford v. City of Yakima, 706 F.3d 1188, 1193 (9th Cir. 2013). The Supreme

10   Court has defined “but for” causation as being “established whenever a particular outcome would

11   not have happened ‘but for’ the purported cause.” Bostock v. Clayton Cnty., 590 U.S. 644, 656

12   (2020). “In other words, a but-for test directs [the Court] to change one thing at a time and see if

13   the outcome changes. If it does, [there exists] a but-for cause.” Id. Under this test, if the outcome

14   (the adverse action) would not have occurred without the government official's retaliatory

15   animus, then that animus was a but-for cause of the adverse action. See id. Conversely, if the

16   official would have taken the same adverse action even in the absence of their animus or

17   retaliatory motive arising from the plaintiff’s speech, then the official’s animus was not a but-for

18   cause of the adverse action, and there was no violation of the plaintiff's constitutional rights. See

19   id.

20         Here, Plaintiff alleges that his arrest soon after his refusal to take the preliminary alcohol

21   screening test and Defendant Meza-Gonzalez’s statement that “If you choose to not consent to it,

22   then it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not” proves retaliatory

23   animus. (ECF No. 5, FAC ¶ 68 p.19-19:21.) This is not so. Defendant Meza-Gonzalez informed

24   Plaintiff of the voluntary nature of the test prior to asking Plaintiff whether he consents to the test.

25   (Id. ¶ 66, p.19:7-9.) Defendant Meza-Gonzalez also correctly stated that he had the discretion to

26   arrest Plaintiff for DUI even if Plaintiff refuses the test. As is the pattern in the FAC, there is no

27   indication that Defendant Meza-Gonzalez did anything improper on the facts, but Plaintiff

28   nevertheless alleges that Defendant Meza-Gonzalez acted unlawfully. As the Court held in

 7   VI.   DEFENDANTS ARE ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY ON EACH OF PLAINTIFF’S
           CAUSES OF ACTION.
 8

 9         Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants fail on the independent ground that qualified

10   immunity applies. “Qualified immunity shields federal and state officials from money damages

11   unless a plaintiff pleads facts showing (1) that the official violated a statutory or constitutional

12   right, and (2) that the right was clearly established at the time of the challenged conduct.”

13   Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S. 731, 735 (2011); accord Chavez v. Robinson, 12 F.4th 978, 995

14   (9th Cir. 2021). Qualified immunity is afforded to officers if they had probable cause to make the

15   arrest or search, or if the officers reasonably believed they had probable cause to make the arrest

16   or search. Rosenbaum v. Washoe County, 663, F.3d 1071, 1076 (9th Cir. 2011). This applies to

17   knowledge at the time, and not whether or not an independent prosecutor declined to press

18   charges, or the plaintiff was found innocent. Id. An officer’s decision to search or arrest is

19   protected by qualified immunity unless “all reasonable officers would agree that there was no

20   probable cause in this instance.” Johnson v. Barr 79 F.4th 996, 1005 (9th Cir. 2023).

21         Regarding the first prong of the qualified immunity analysis, Plaintiff has not shown any

22   violation of his constitutional rights, as explained above. The qualified immunity analysis should

23   end here.

24               Regarding the second prong of the qualified immunity analysis, assuming, arguendo,

25   that no probable cause existed to support the search of Plaintiff’s vehicle and/or for Plaintiff’s

26   arrest, Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano would still be entitled to qualified immunity as it

27   was not “clearly established” that probable cause to search and arrest did not exist in the

28   circumstances as pled in the FAC.

 1         While there does not have to be a case directly on point, existing precedent must place the

 4   clearly established at the time of the alleged misconduct.” Romero v. Kitsap Cty., 931 F.2d 624,

 5   627 (9th Cir. 1991). “Clearly established” law means precedent existing at the time of the

 6   conduct that “squarely governs the specific facts at issue.” Kisela v. Hughes, 584 U.S.100, 104

 7   (2018). “[C]ourts must not define clearly established law at a high level of generality[.]” Wesby,

 8   supra, 583 U.S. at 63.

 9         Here, there is no “clearly established” law that should have put Defendants on notice that

10   all reasonable officers would know that probable cause to arrest and/or search Plaintiff did not

11   exist in the circumstances as pled in the FAC. To the contrary, it is clearly established that

12   probable cause exists when “a prudent person would have concluded there was a fair probability

13   that the suspect had committed a crime, based on the totality of the circumstances known to the

14   officer.” Crowe v. County of San Diego, 608 F.3d 406, 434 (9th Cir. 2010). Here, even if

15   Defendants were factually mistaken and had arrested Plaintiff for DUI and searched Plaintiff’s
