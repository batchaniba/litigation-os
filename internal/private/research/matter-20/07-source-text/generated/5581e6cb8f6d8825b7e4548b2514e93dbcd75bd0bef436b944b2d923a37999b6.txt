 1                                           INTRODUCTION

 2         Petitioner’s opposition fails to squarely address California Highway Patrol’s (“CHP”)

 3   demurrer arguments. First, Petitioner appears to concede that mandamus cannot lie to compel

 4   CHP to issue a Form B certification to him. (See Petitioner’s Opposition to CHP’s Demurrer

 5   (“Opp.”) at 5-6 [“Mandamus ‘does not lie to compel a public agency to exercise discretionary

 6   powers in a particular manner, only to compel it to exercise them in some manner’”].) Second,

 7   Petitioner continues to attack CHP’s written explanation for its Form B certification denial as not

 8   “legally adequate” by characterizing it as “no explanation at all,” without providing any basis for

 9   his demand that CHP provide a written explanation in a form not required by the statute. Third,

10   Petitioner’s argument that CHP has misapplied the rebuttable presumption of helpfulness from

11   Penal Code section 679.10 relies on Petitioner’s misreading of the statute. Fourth, Petitioner

12   simply fails to engage with Respondent’s arguments as to why declaratory relief may not issue.

13         Fundamentally, Petitioner’s arguments arise from a misapplication of the demurrer

14   standard. “[T]he function of a demurrer is not to test the truth or accuracy of the facts alleged in

15   the complaint . . . [the court’s] focus is on the legal sufficiency of the complaint.” (SJJC Aviation

16   Servs., LLC v. City of San Jose (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1043, 1051-52.) A court does not accept

17   as true “contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law” in that pleading. (Yvanova v. New

18   Century Mortg. Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924.) In other words, CHP’s demurrer challenges

19   the facial legal defects in the Petition and argues that Petitioner has simply not pled enough to

20   state any cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10.) In doing so, the Petition must be

21   given “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Blank v.

22   Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

23         Petitioner’s arguments fail to recognize that while the Court may consider matters that may

24   be judicially noticed, as well as take judicial notice of facts appearing in any exhibits attached to

25   the petition and complaint, the Court may not “assume the truth of ‘mere contentions or assertions

26   contradicted by judicially noticeable facts.’” (SJJC Aviation Servs., LLC v. City of San Jose,

27   supra, 12 Cal.App.5th at p. 1052, citing Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 20; Blatty

28   v. New York Times Co. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 1033, 1040 [“when the allegations of the complaint
                                                        4
                                                Reply iso Demurrer to Petition for Writ of Mandate (26CV191136)
 1   contradict or are inconsistent with such facts, we accept the latter and reject the former”]; Holland

 2   v. Morse Diesel Int’l, Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447 [“We may also take notice of

 3   exhibits attached to the complaints. If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the

 4   facts in the exhibits take precedence.”].)

 5                                                ARGUMENT
 6   I.    PETITIONER FAILS TO STATE FACTS SUFFICIENT TO CONSTITUTE ANY CAUSE OF
           ACTION.
 7

 8         Each of Petitioner’s writ causes of action, as well as cause of action for declaratory relief, is

 9   subject to dismissal. For each writ of cause of action, Petitioner has failed to allege that

10   Respondent “has a clear, present, and ministerial duty to act in a particular way” or that Petitioner

11   “has a clear, present and beneficial right to performance of that duty.” (County of San Diego v.

12   State of California (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 580, 593.) Further, Petitioner is not entitled to his

13   requested declaratory relief because he seeks advisory opinions on purely legal questions and to

14   reverse a past administrative decision.

15         A.    Petitioner Fails to Allege that Respondent Had a Ministerial Duty to
                 Certify Petitioner’s Form B.
16

17         Although Petitioner previously argued that Respondents failed to perform a mandatory duty

18   by “refus[ing] to certify [his Form B] on the asserted ground that the underlying conduct does not

19   constitute ‘qualifying criminal activity,’” Petitioner now attempts to reframe his argument as one

20   that CHP failed to perform its ministerial duty to comply with its “procedural obligations” under

21   Penal Code section 679.10. See Opp. at 6. Petitioner argues that “[t]he Petition plausibly alleges

22   that CHP failed to apply the governing standards to the material records before it.” (Opp. at 7.)

23         However, nowhere does Petitioner identify these “governing standards” or “procedural

24   obligations” that purportedly arise from the statute. (See Opp. at 6-7.)1 Rather, Petitioner points

27   explanation whether it improperly treated the absence of a charge, investigation, or prosecution as
     dispositive.” (See Opp. at 6-7.) But these alleged “governing standards” are nowhere to be found

11         B.    Petitioner Fails to Allege that He Has a Clear, Present, and Beneficial
                 Right to a Form B Certification.
12

13         Petitioner has not pled sufficient facts to support his bare legal conclusion that he was the

14   victim of “false imprisonment, unlawful criminal restraint, felonious assault, obstruction of

15   justice, [and] perjury.” (See Pet., ¶ 82.) In his Opposition, Petitioner insists that he has alleged

16   enough, pointing to “fourteen exhibits, including the denial letter itself (Ex. 1), Petitioner’s eight-

17   page legal analysis identifying four contradictions by dashcam timestamp (Ex. 3), the verbatim

18   dashcam transcript2 (Ex. 5), the CHP arrest report containing the disputed statements (Ex. 6), and

19   the section 851.6 detention certificate (Ex. 7).” (Opp. at 4.) While Petitioner is correct that the

20   Court should consider these exhibits as part of his pleading (see Holland v. Morse Diesel Int’l,

21   Inc., supra, 86 Cal.App.4th at p. 1447), Petitioner is wrong that his contentions, inferences, and

22   legal conclusions drawn from these exhibits should be taken as true. (See Yvanova v. New

23   Century Mortg. Corp., 62 Cal.4th at p. 924 [courts do not accept as true “contentions, deductions,

24   or conclusions of fact or law in that pleading”].)

25         Petitioner incorrectly presumes that Respondent asks this Court to “weigh competing

26   inferences.” (Opp. at 5.) Not so. Rather, Respondent asks this Court to evaluate the face of the

17         C.    Petitioner Fails to Allege that Respondent Did Not Provide a Written
                 Explanation for the Form B Certification Denial.
18

19         Petitioner insists that Respondent did not comply with Section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2)’s

20   requirement that a certifying entity provide a written explanation for denying Form B

21   certification. (Opp. at 7-9.) However, Petitioner’s insistence relies upon his invention of a legal

22   standard that does not exist. Section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2) provides, in full:

23         If a certifying entity does not certify a Form I-918 Supplement B certification, they shall
           provide a written explanation for the denial of the Form I-918 Supplement B certification.
24
           The written denial shall include specific details of any reasonable requests for cooperation
25         and a detailed description of how the victim refused to cooperate.

26         Respondent does not dispute that Section 679.10, subdivision (i)(2) requires a written

27   explanation from a certifying entity that denies a Form B certification. That is, Respondent

28   agrees that “any denial must be accompanied by a written explanation.” See Opp. at 8. As shown
                                                        7
                                                Reply iso Demurrer to Petition for Writ of Mandate (26CV191136)
 1   by Petitioner’s Exhibit 1, Respondent did, in fact, provide a written explanation for its Form B

 2   certification denial. (See Pet., Ex. 1.) CHP did not, as Petitioner asserts, “merely restate[] the

 3   ultimate conclusion.” (See Opp. at 9.) Rather, CHP wrote to Petitioner:

 4         A thorough review of this incident was conducted. Unfortunately, this incident does not
           meet the standard of qualifying criminal activity identified in Section 679.10 of the
 5
           California Penal Code (PC) . . . Additionally, this review found no substantially similar
 6         qualifying criminal activity as defined in Section 679.10(d) PC.

 7   (Pet., Ex. 1.)

 8         That is precisely the explanation that Petitioner now states is “legally adequate.” (See Opp.

 9   at 9.) Petitioner argues that to be legally adequate, a written explanation must “disclose whether

10   CHP rejected the request because it found no qualifying criminal activity, no substantially similar

11   activity, insufficient victim status, lack of helpfulness, or other legally permissible basis.” (See

12   id.) CHP did just that, disclosing to Petitioner that it had rejected his request because it found no

13   qualifying criminal activity or substantially similar activity.

14         To the extent Petitioner relies on the Attorney General’s Information Bulletin No. 2024-

15   DLE-05 (see Pet., Ex. 14), Petitioner reads the FAQ answer out of context. That guidance

16   provided: “There is a rebuttable presumption that a victim is helpful, has been helpful, or is likely

17   to be helpful, if the victim has not refused or failed to provide information and assistance

18   reasonably requested by law enforcement. To comply with the rebuttable presumption of

19   helpfulness in the VTVPA, the reason for denial of certification should be clearly articulated

20   and specific (i.e., non-cooperative because of three unreturned phone calls on dates X, Y, and

21   Z).” (Pet., Ex. 14 at 5 (emphasis added).) In other words, the bulletin recommended specificity

22   in situations where the applicant’s helpfulness was at issue. In this instance, Respondent’s

23   helpfulness was not at issue, because CHP had already found no qualifying criminal activity or

24   substantially similar activity.

 1         D.    Petitioner Fails to Allege that Respondent Had a Ministerial Duty to Apply
                 the Rebuttal Presumption of Helpfulness on the Facts Alleged.
 2

 3         Contrary to Petitioner’s assertions, Respondent’s demurrer does not “flip[] the presumption

 4   of helpfulness on its head” or render the presumption “meaningless.” (See Opp. at 9-10.) Rather,

 5   it is Petitioner who contorts the statute beyond its intended purpose.

 6         As an initial matter, Petitioner’s reading of Section 679.10, where “agencies could defeat

 7   the presumption simply by never asking for help,” relies on his assumption that the statute was

 8   designed to address situations where the certifying agency is also accused of subjecting the

 9   applicant to a crime, or at the very least is in an adversarial opposition to the applicant. As laid

10   out in Respondent’s demurrer, Section 679.10 was enacted to impose procedural requirements on

11   law enforcement agencies processing Form B certification requests (some of which had declined

12   altogether to issue such certifications), not necessarily to broaden the scope of the U Visa statute.3

13         It is Petitioner who reads out critical language from Section 679.10 by insisting that CHP

14   was required to apply the rebuttable presumption of helpfulness to him because he did not refuse

15   or fail to cooperate. See Opp. at 10. The statute provides: “[T]here is a rebuttable presumption

16   that a victim is helpful, has been helpful, or is likely to be helpful, if the victim has not refused or

17   failed to provide information and assistance reasonably requested by law enforcement.” (Pen.

18   Code, § 679.10, subd. (h) (emphasis added).) Petitioner ignores the requirement that information

19   and assistance be first reasonably requested by law enforcement.

20         Petitioner’s reading would allow almost every immigrant to be presumed helpful to a

21   criminal investigation and eligible for a Form B certification by virtue of “not refusing or failing

22   to provide information,” regardless of whether any information or assistance was actually

23   solicited by law enforcement. More specifically, Petitioner’s reading would also allow every

24   immigrant who lodged a complaint against a law enforcement agency to be presumed helpful

25   simply by virtue of “affirmative provision of information,” as Petitioner alleges. See Opp. at 10.

     3
      As set forth in Respondent’s Demurrer, one of the primary objectives of the U Visa, which was
27   created as part of the reauthorization of the Violence Against Women Act, was to remove
     obstacles that could hinder battered immigrants from fleeing domestic violence safely and
28   prosecuting their abusers. (See MPA in support of CHP’s Demurrer at 7.)

 1         E.    Petitioner Fails to Explain Why He is Entitled to Declaratory Relief.

 2         Petitioner fails to substantively engage with Respondent’s arguments that declaratory relief

 3   is not appropriate to review a past administrative decision. (See MPA in support of CHP’s

 4   Demurrer at 19.) Instead, Petitioner insists that an actual controversy exists, because he asks the

 5   Court to declare whether CHP’s denial letter satisfied the Penal Code. (Opp. at 10.) However, as

 6   Respondent explained, “[d]eclaratory relief operates prospectively to declare future rights, rather

 7   than to redress past wrongs.” (County of San Diego v. State of California, supra, 164

 8   Cal.App.4th at p. 607.) Petitioner does not deny that CHP issued him a denial letter on April 20,

 9   2026, over three months ago.

10         Further, Petitioner’s attempt to distinguish Griset v. Fair Political Practices Commission

11   (2001) 25 Cal. 4th 688, 697-700, misinterprets Respondent’s argument regarding mootness. (See

12   MPA in support of CHP’s Demurrer at 19-20.) To be clear, Respondent argument was that, if the

13   Court determined as a legal matter, that “there is no ministerial duty compelling CHP to provide a

14   written denial in the form demanded by Petitioner,” the declaration sought by Petitioner (that

15   CHP’s denial letter does not satisfy Section 679.10) becomes moot. (See id. at 20.) Petitioner’s

16   general statements regarding whether a declaratory cause of action is subject to demurrer are not

17   adequately responsive.

18   II.   PETITIONER CANNOT CURE THE DEFECTS IN HIS PETITION THROUGH
           AMENDMENT.
19

20         Petitioner bears the burden of showing amendment is not futile. He cannot meet this

21   burden. As an initial matter, Petitioner’s suggested amendments, which indicate Petitioner would

22   “identify more specifically the factual basis of each alleged qualifying or substantially similar

23   criminal activity” and “plead with greater specificity the materials submitted to CHP” lack any

24   factual details about what those amendments would involve and are plainly insufficient. (Nelson

25   v. Tucker Ellis, LLP (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 827, 849 [leave to amend futile where plaintiff had

26   “not identified any additional facts” to overcome legal barriers]; see also Shaeffer v. Califia

27   Farms, LLC (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 1125, 1145 [“the onus is on the plaintiff to articulate the

28   ‘specifi[c] ways’ to cure the identified defect.”] [citation omitted].)
                                                       10
                                                Reply iso Demurrer to Petition for Writ of Mandate (26CV191136)
 1         More fundamentally, amendment would be futile because no additional facts alleged could

 2   alter the Petition’s facial deficiencies: CHP maintained discretion to issue him a denial (and so

 3   had no ministerial duty to issue him a Form B certification); CHP provided Petitioner with a

 4   written explanation for this denial; and the reasonable presumption of helpfulness was not

 5   applicable given that CHP (or any other law enforcement agency) did not request any information

 6   or assistance from Petitioner. Accordingly, leave to amend should be denied.

 7                                            CONCLUSION
 8         The Court should sustain CHP’s demurrer without leave to amend.
