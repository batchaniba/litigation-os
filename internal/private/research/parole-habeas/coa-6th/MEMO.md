## 1 The draft and its factual conditions

The proposed appellate paper is a verified petition for writ of mandate under California Constitution article VI section 10 and Code of Civil Procedure sections 1085 and 1086. It seeks an initial ruling as defined by rule 4.551(a)(8), within 15 days after service of the writ or another short period set by the Court, followed by a compliance report. Fifteen days is proposed relief, not an existing statutory deadline.

The record reviewed supports the April 3 petition date, the July 2 extension order specifying August 1, and the reported in-person September 1 HC-004 filing. It does not establish the present state of the live docket. The retrieved September 1 HC-004 is a one-page form; it does not independently establish that the required petition attachment was filed with it. Obtain the complete clerk-endorsed packet.

No presiding-judge completeness finding is assumed. The HC-004's 30-day calendaring provision is conditional. The initial-ruling requirement and the court's expired extension supply an independent basis to seek action. The alternative prayer asks for prompt determination of the notice's status if needed.

Rule 4.551(i) does not limit the court to one extension. Rule 4.551(a)(6) does not expressly prohibit another HC-004. The revised paper omits those unsupported absolutes. A request for an informal response counts as the initial ruling; the 45-day period following receipt of that response and the other later-stage deadlines require separate attention if such an order exists.

## 4 Distinguish treatment review from parole discharge

Section 3574(e) expressly makes the treatment-completion assessment independent of parole discharge. Subdivision (f) requires Form 3043 for the specified determination retaining a participant in treatment. These provisions do not by themselves prove that every parole-discharge assessment must replace Form 1502-DR with Form 3043.

Any later merits paper should identify the separate legal sources governing treatment participation, parole-discharge consideration, and the requested remedy. Analyze Penal Code sections 3000(b)(5)(B) and (b)(6), as well as the ten-year provision and any applicable section 3001 rules. Their consideration and successful-completion language deserves examination; no entitlement to early discharge is established by this memorandum. Obtain Policy 19-03 itself and its adoption history before making a definitive underground-regulation argument.

The report's page 4 contains the wrong name Eagan, but also contains Petitioner's correct name, CDCR number, and other personal information. The wrong-name passage supports a reliability challenge. It does not establish that the entire review concerned a different person. Investigate the approximately-four-years calculation, the treatment information dated October 2024, actual time-credit calculations, and which defects affected the result. Do not substitute Newanforbi when quoting Eagan.

## 5 Remedy cases need accurate limits

*In re Prather* (2010) 50 Cal.4th 238 rejects improper restrictions on the Board's parole-suitability discretion on remand and reverses Molina's judicial release order. Its application to a DAPO discharge decision requires an explained analogy. The statement that a second unjustified denial might warrant an outright grant is from Justice Moreno's concurrence, not the majority holding. Repeated denial is not an automatic discharge trigger.

*In re Masoner* (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1098 rejected an idle-act rationale for bypassing a new Board suitability hearing. The separate *Masoner* opinion at 179 Cal.App.4th 1531 involved reinstatement of a Board grant after a Governor reversal. Neither should be described as a DAPO earned-discharge case.

*In re Lira* (2014) 58 Cal.4th 573 principally rejects parole credit for confinement after an erroneous Governor reversal. Its remedial discussion is relevant, but it is not a categorical holding governing every defective discharge decision. *Dexter* exhaustion futility is distinct from futility of remand as a basis for release.

These cases are not needed to decide this delay mandate and are omitted from its argument. Immediate discharge remains a difficult merits request; the proposed review alternative also requires a demonstrated duty and an answer to Matter 9. The earlier assertion that timed review was already the clearly available remedy is withdrawn.

## 7 Federal habeas alternative

Federal habeas is a possible later route to challenge the legality of ongoing parole custody. A parolee subject to significant restraints can be “in custody.” *Jones v. Cunningham* (1963) 371 U.S. 236, 242–243. Because the parole arises from a state-court judgment, analyze any federal petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2254, including its federal-ground, exhaustion, limitation, and prior-petition requirements. Confirm the correct federal district and respondent using actual residence, supervision, judgment, and claim; do not assume either the Northern or Eastern District solely from the parole office’s location. Northern District Habeas L.R. 2254-3 addresses convictions from Santa Clara County and execution claims involving confinement in its listed counties.

The same federal constitutional claim ordinarily must be fairly presented through an available complete round of California review, including the California Supreme Court, before federal relief. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)–(c); *O'Sullivan v. Boerckel* (1999) 526 U.S. 838, 845; *Redd v. Guerrero* (9th Cir. 2023) 84 F.4th 874. The pending Santa Clara petition and a possible Sixth District delay writ do not themselves establish exhaustion of a substantive federal discharge claim. Section 2254(b)(1)(B) allows an exception when no state corrective process is available or circumstances make it ineffective; extraordinary state delay is fact-specific, not automatic. *Coe v. Thurman* (9th Cir. 1990) 922 F.2d 528, 530–532; *Phillips v. Vasquez* (9th Cir. 1995) 56 F.3d 1030, 1035. A current docket and documented efforts to obtain a ruling matter before asserting that exception. The state delay mandate is a plausible way to obtain a state decision and preserve the ordinary exhaustion route.

The federal ground must be more than an error under Penal Code section 3008, regulation 3574, or Policy 19-03. Section 2254(a) requires custody in violation of federal law. *Swarthout v. Cooke* (2011) 562 U.S. 216, 219–222 holds that in its parole-suitability setting federal process was limited to an opportunity to be heard and reasons for denial; it does not make every state procedural or evidentiary flaw a federal due-process violation. The Eagan reference can support a fact-specific contention that the review was not meaningful, but the mixed-name record, actual procedures received, and whether an applicable liberty interest exists must be examined. Matter 9's adopted disposition and any preclusion issue also require review.

Remedy matters. A direct claim that parole custody must terminate belongs in the core of habeas if established. A request only for a new discretionary parole review, which might again end in denial, may instead belong under 42 U.S.C. § 1983: *Wilkinson v. Dotson* (2005) 544 U.S. 74, 82; *Nettles v. Grounds* (9th Cir. 2016) 830 F.3d 922, 934–935 (en banc). Their parole-suitability facts are analogous, not a ruling on DAPO discharge. Federal habeas is not a federal mandamus device to force Judge Hawk to decide C2604791; 28 U.S.C. § 1361 covers duties of federal officers or employees.

Calculate the claim-specific one-year period under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1), including any later factual predicate under subsection (D), and statutory tolling only while a properly filed, pertinent state collateral application is pending under subsection (d)(2). Do not assume the 2018 conviction date starts or ends the clock for this later parole-decision claim. Investigate prior federal habeas petitions, if any, before preparing a federal pleading because second-or-successive rules may apply. The current record is insufficient to draft a signed federal petition or claim exhaustion. This federal route is tracked in research, not added to the Sixth District prayer.
