## 1. Who he is, in one paragraph

Appointed August 1, 2024. Before the bench he spent more than twelve years as
an ACLU civil-rights plaintiffs' lawyer and also served as an Assistant Federal
Public Defender (see the 2026 audit for detail). Expect a judge who understands
plaintiffs'-side litigation from the inside, manages cases actively rather than
passively, and treats settlement as a normal phase of the case rather than an
afterthought. He is helpful to pro se litigants but not indulgent — see §7.

## 2. The October 8 conference: what he will ask

Riordan's conference-setting orders use a standard 14-point status-report
checklist. Two primary sources confirm the current form:

- *Reuter v. United States*, 2:26-cv-00327 (order Aug. 5, 2026) — status report
  addressing (1) service, (2) possible joinder, (3) expected amendments,
  (4) jurisdiction and venue, (5) anticipated motions, (6) Rule 26(f) discovery
  plan including experts, (7) discovery/motion/pretrial/trial deadlines,
  (8) special procedures, (9) estimated trial time, (10) departures from
  standard procedures based on complexity, (11) related cases,
  (12) whether a settlement conference should be set, (13) whether the parties
  consent to Riordan acting as settlement judge or prefer another judge, and
  (14) anything else promoting just and expeditious disposition.
- *Jenkins v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc.*, 2:25-cv-03051-DJC-SCR
  (order Oct. 30, 2025) — a **pro se plaintiff** case against Experian (removed,
  answered) in which Riordan set the same style of Zoom status conference and
  the same checklist (a–n), and additionally offered the parties the option,
  with consent, to have the case **tried by the magistrate judge** under
  28 U.S.C. § 636(c), with the consent form attached and due back within
  21 days.

Matter 25 checkpoint: the plaintiff's status report is recorded as due
**September 24, 2026**; the conference is **October 8, 2026, at 11:00 a.m. by
Zoom**. The report is the work product that frames everything he does at the
conference — treat it as the most important filing in the case to date.

## 3. The scheduling order he is likely to issue

The closest verified comparator is *Fitzgibbon v. Experian Information
Solutions, Inc.*, 2:25-cv-02508-TLN-SCR, Doc. 11 (filed Dec. 1, 2025) — an
FCRA case against a consumer reporting agency in which Riordan held the status
conference, heard the parties, considered the defendant's status report, and
then issued a three-page pretrial scheduling order. Its anatomy:

- **Service.** Defendant said service was defective, but it had answered and
  raised no Rule 12(b)(5) challenge — held waived. (FedEx answered Aug. 31,
  2026, Dkt. 4; the same waiver logic applies.)
- **Amendment.** A short, specific amendment deadline (in *Fitzgibbon*,
  December 3, 2025 — two days after the order).
- **Discovery.** Initial disclosures by a fixed near-term date; nonexpert
  discovery cutoff roughly 5.5 months out. "Completed" is defined: all
  depositions taken, all disputes resolved by appropriate order, and any
  ordered discovery complied with. Motions to compel nonexpert discovery must
  be noticed on his calendar per the Local Rules and **heard no later than
  about three weeks before the discovery cutoff** — this backstop matters for
  calendaring.
- **Experts.** Fixed expert-disclosure, rebuttal, and expert-discovery-cutoff
  dates.
- **Law and motion.** All motions except continuances, TROs, emergency relief,
  and discovery motions filed by a cutoff roughly eight months out. Caution:
  **failure to raise a dispositive legal issue by the cutoff may constitute
  waiver.** Motions in limine are for admissibility only; substantive motions
  disguised as MILs are disfavored.
- **Final pretrial conference and trial.** Set after the motion cutoff passes
  or motions are resolved — he does not set trial at the initial conference.
- **Settlement.** "Absent an objection by one of the parties, the undersigned
  will serve as a settlement judge in this case." The defendant was ordered to
  meet and confer with the plaintiff to identify three dates (in
  *Fitzgibbon*, February or March 2026) and discuss in-person vs. Zoom, then
  email his courtroom deputy (sher@caed.uscourts.gov) to set it.

Expect the Matter 25 order to follow this skeleton with dates fitted to the
case. The settlement paragraph is the one to watch: Riordan volunteers as
settlement judge by default and puts the burden on the parties to object.

## 4. Hard rules from his civil standing order

Verified from the court-hosted civil standing order (MASTER 2024_08_11 SCR
Civil Standing Order V2):

- Non-discovery civil motions are heard **Thursdays at 10:00 a.m.**
- Motions and oppositions capped at **20 pages**; replies at **10 pages**.
  Unauthorized supplemental briefs, surreplies, and excess pages will not be
  considered.
- He may submit motions without argument under L.R. 230(g) — briefs must
  stand alone.
- Remote appearances are generally disfavored absent hardship (the October 8
  conference is already set by Zoom, so this is not an issue for that date).
- Discovery meet-and-confer by represented parties must occur **in person, by
  telephone, or by video**; written communication alone does not satisfy the
  requirement. He "strictly enforces" this.
- After a genuine meet-and-confer, he offers **informal telephonic discovery
  resolution**, normally via a **two-page joint letter filed at least 48 hours
  before the conference**. He retains power to impose monetary or other
  sanctions during that informal process.
- Settlement conferences are in person or by Zoom — **no hybrid format**.
- A person with **full settlement authority must attend**; an insurer
  representative must attend or remain available throughout.
- He may hold a pre-settlement meeting with counsel.
- Settlement statements are due **seven days beforehand** and may include a
  confidential submission.

## 5. Discovery management: observed samples

- *Fitzgibbon* order (§3 above): structured, deadline-driven, with the
  three-week MTC-hearing backstop before the discovery cutoff.
- Sua sponte extension (Oct. 30, 2025): Riordan extended discovery and
  dispositive-motion deadlines **on his own motion** to accommodate a pro se
  plaintiff's subpoena request, and directed the defendants to explain why the
  requested information had not been produced. He will move deadlines himself
  when fairness to the less-resourced party requires it.
- *Valenzuela v. Schmidt* (Nov. 21, 2025) and *Witkin v. Thomas* (Nov. 21,
  2025): granted supported scheduling modifications for good cause while
  leaving the rest of the scheduling order intact. Extensions are available;
  wholesale rewrites are not.
- *Ellis v. Williams* (Nov. 24, 2025): granted a pro se prisoner's subpoena
  request with procedural instructions — he walks unrepresented parties
  through the mechanics.
- 2:22-cv-01231 (Mar. 18, 2025): **reopened discovery for 30 days despite
  untimeliness** because the requested material was directly relevant, and
  referred the dispute to his informal telephonic-resolution process. Merits
  access and proportionality beat rigid cutoff enforcement when the record
  warrants relief.
- 2:25-cv-02866 (Apr. 15, 2026): granted a partial discovery stay — ordered
  overdue initial disclosures within 21 days, allowed narrowly tailored
  written discovery, stayed broader discovery during unresolved substantive
  motions, and encouraged a cooperative stipulated protective order. On
  sealing, the strong presumption of public access applies; compelling reasons
  required.

Synthesis: he enforces structure (page limits, conferral rules, cutoff
backstops) but adjusts deadlines and permits targeted discovery when doing so
protects fair adjudication. A well-documented, conferral-compliant discovery
ask gets a hearing; a sloppy or conferral-skipping one gets sanctions risk.

## 6. Settlement practice: what the orders show

- Default posture: he serves as settlement judge **absent objection**
  (*Fitzgibbon*), and his conference orders routinely pair a **pre-settlement
  conference** (Zoom, 2:30 p.m. the day before, lead attorneys only) with the
  settlement conference itself (*Jackson v. Pinnacle Live, LLC*,
  2:25-cv-3392, order Aug. 5, 2026; *V.V.V. & Sons v. Meenakshi Overseas*,
  2:14-cv-02961; *Adams v. City of Redding*, 2:20-cv-01610). At his
  discretion the joint pre-settlement discussion may be followed by private
  discussions with each side.
- Confidential settlement statements go to **scrorders@caed.uscourts.gov**
  (not filed; a one-page Notice of Submission is e-filed), due about seven
  days before the conference, marked CONFIDENTIAL. Required contents:
  (1) brief facts; (2) strengths and weaknesses from the party's perspective;
  (3) **itemized expected costs** for discovery, pretrial, and trial in
  specific dollar terms; (4) **estimated probability of a liability finding,
  in percentage terms**; (5) realistic high-end, low-end, and most-likely
  recovery in dollars; (6) settlement history — expectations, all past and
  present offers with terms, and whether the party would make the opening
  offer; (7) participant list with names/titles; (8) related cases; plus a
  completed pre-settlement worksheet.
- Authority standard: trial counsel, the parties, and individuals with **full
  authority to negotiate and settle "on any terms"** must participate. An
  authorization limited to a sum certain can be found noncompliant — the
  order language contemplates "unfettered discretion and authority" to change
  position during the conference.
- If a party believes the conference will not be productive, the order
  directs them to tell the court **as far in advance as possible** — a
  pressure valve, not a free pass; use it only with a real reason.

## 7. Treatment of a pro se employment plaintiff

- *Ulloa v. A. Teichert & Son*, 2:24-cv-03172 (order May 8, 2026): Riordan
  rejected a defective Title VII theory but recognized the same facts could
  support ADEA discrimination and retaliation, granted 30 days to amend,
  identified the specific facts needed, and appended a plain-language
  **"Pro Se Plaintiff Summary."** Rule: pro se litigants get notice and an
  opportunity to amend unless cure is impossible.
- *Chand v. Rich-Banales*, 2:23-cv-01583: claim-by-claim analysis of a pro se
  FEHA/§1981 employment complaint; partial dismissal **with leave to amend**;
  only a legally nonexistent board entity was dismissed with prejudice. He
  separates curable pleading defects from true futility.
- *Jenkins v. Experian* (§2 above): the same full conference/report framework
  applied to a pro se plaintiff — no shortcut version, but also no penalty
  for proceeding without counsel; the §636(c) consent option was offered with
  the form attached.
- The limit: amended pleadings must identify each defendant's actions; he
  will not let a plaintiff force the court or the defendant to guess.

## 8. Tactical takeaways for October 8 (Matter 25)

1. **The September 24 status report is the decisive filing.** Address all 14
   checklist points; it becomes the raw material for his scheduling order.
   Weak or vague answers on anticipated motions, the 26(f) plan, or deadlines
   will be converted into a defense-friendly schedule.
2. **Come with a discovery plan, not a wish list.** Propose concrete dates:
   initial-disclosure exchange, a nonexpert cutoff roughly six months out,
   expert dates only if experts are genuinely needed, and a law-and-motion
   cutoff. Remember the MTC-hearing backstop (~3 weeks before the cutoff)
   when calendaring.
3. **Flag amendments early.** If the complaint may need amendment, say so in
   the report (checklist item 3); *Fitzgibbon* shows he sets a short, fixed
   amendment deadline at the conference.
4. **Decide the settlement-judge question in advance.** His default is to
   serve as settlement judge absent objection, and he will ask defense to
   bring three dates. Know before October 8 whether to consent to Riordan or
   request another judge — and have your own three dates ready.
5. **Build the settlement worksheet now.** His confidential statements demand
   itemized future costs, a liability probability, and high/low/likely
   recovery figures. Assembling those numbers before the conference is set is
   leverage, not paperwork.
6. **Anticipated motions (checklist item 5) is where FedEx tips its hand.**
   Watch whether defense signals a Rule 12(c) or summary-judgment motion;
   state your own anticipated motions with equal specificity. His waiver
   caution means unraised dispositive issues can be lost at the cutoff.
7. **Meet-and-confer discipline starts immediately.** Any future discovery
   dispute requires a real voice/video/in-person conferral first, then a
   two-page joint letter 48+ hours before his informal call. Written-only
   "conferral" will be sanctionable noncompliance.
8. **§636(c) consent is a decision, not a formality.** Expect the
   consent-to-MJ-trial form. Declining keeps Article III review (and Drozd)
   over dispositive rulings via the F&R process; consenting trades that away
   for a judge whose plaintiffs'-side background is known. The low-risk
   default is to decline unless his case management proves favorable.
9. **If discovery goes sideways, document and confer — he will move
   deadlines.** The observed record shows sua sponte extensions for fairness
   and reopened discovery for directly relevant material. The price of that
   flexibility is strict compliance with his conferral and joint-letter
   rules.
10. **Pro se is accommodated, not advantaged.** Expect plain-language
    explanations and guided leave to amend, but every pleading and report
    must still identify who did what. Precision is the pro se plaintiff's
    best exhibit in his courtroom.
