## 2. Ground rules for the brief

1. **Argue the pleading, not the evidence.** The motion is confined to the FAC. Rule 12 requires accepting well-pleaded facts and drawing inferences for Plaintiff. Don't attach or describe the video, transcript, affidavit, or tox.
2. **Rule 11(b)(3) applies to "later advocating" the FAC.** Don't advocate: (a) the "Beverage Present" checkbox; (b) that the odor entry was false; (c) that Plaintiff had not been drinking; (d) a parole hold. You may still argue that **defendants' probable-cause theory depends on matters outside the FAC.** That is a statement about the record, not a factual assertion.
3. **Do not volunteer**: FST results, the HGN entry, the party, the wristband, the "parking lot" exchange, parole or 290 status, or the dismissal timeline.
4. **New arguments in reply get no weight.** Defendants did not challenge personal participation (Melano on Count 3), the Nieves protected-activity element, or incorporation of the report. Note in one sentence that the motion raises no other grounds. Under *Zamani v. Carnes*, 491 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2007), the court need not consider arguments first raised in reply.
5. **Credibility over coverage.** Concede what's lost (fees) and decline to defend what is unsound (Counts 2 and 6, with leave). Spend the pages on Counts 4, 5, and 3 and the Rule 12(f) issue.
6. **Format:** N.D. Cal. Civil L.R. 3-4 and 7-4; caption per current docket name (pending name-conform motion); Plaintiff's signature image, no typed /s/.

## 5. Argument modules

Each module gives: the thesis, the governing rules with their verification status, how the FAC applies, the motion's errors, the likely reply, and the rebuttal. Quotations marked **[P]** were read in the primary opinion text in `03-authorities/` this session. **[L]** means existence is VERIFIED in the portfolio citation ledger but the proposition comes from secondary sources. **[S]** means search-corroborated only. **[VERIFY]** means do not cite until checked (§ 9).

### 5.1 Rule 12 standard and the record limit (frame for everything)

**Thesis:** ECF 22 asks the Court to choose defendants' version of a disputed roadside encounter. It cites facts the FAC doesn't plead (FST performance, "unconscious," odor, slurring), adopts the very report entries the FAC alleges are contradicted, and offers speculative "innocent explanations." Rule 12 forbids all three.

**Rules:**
- Plausibility: *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) [L]. The motion pin-cites "667" for the 678 language (MTD 5:10, 6:8, 12:3).
- Competing plausible explanations: *Starr v. Baca*, 652 F.3d 1202, 1216 (9th Cir. 2011) [L]. Where both sides' explanations are plausible, the complaint survives. [VERIFY exact quote and pin; the Ninth Circuit amended the opinion, so use the 652 F.3d version.]
- Pro se pleadings are construed liberally after Iqbal: *Hebbe v. Pliler*, 627 F.3d 338, 342 (9th Cir. 2010) [L; VERIFY pin].
- Incorporated documents **[P]**: "it is improper to assume the truth of an incorporated document if such assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint." *Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.*, 899 F.3d 988, 1003 (9th Cir. 2018).
- The same rule applied to non-operative documents **[P]**: "if specific facts alleged in the complaint contradict specific facts related in a non-legally-operative document … the conflict is resolved in the plaintiff's favor." *Grier v. Finjan Holdings, Inc.*, No. 21-16702, slip op. at 7–8 n.1 (9th Cir. Jan. 20, 2023) (citing Khoja at 1003). Police reports are not legally operative documents.

**Application:**
- MTD 6:4–11 offers "the simple explanation" and "another possibility" for the never-spoken odor entry. That is fact-finding.
- MTD 6:27–28 and 10:21–24 fault the FAC for "silence" on FST results. A complaint need not plead the defense's evidence. The absence of a fact from the FAC can't supply probable cause on a Rule 12 motion.
- MTD 2:8, 3:13, and 8:8 call Plaintiff "unconscious." The FAC says asleep (¶¶ 1, 11, 16) and in "a normal resting posture" (¶ 56).
- MTD 10:1–3 lists "slurred speech and other signs of intoxication" as facts. Those are the challenged entries (FAC ¶¶ 20, 26(b)–(c)). Treating them as true is the circularity Khoja forbids.
- **Do not cite FAC ¶ 26(a) (odor) or ¶ 26(d) (beverage) as contradicted facts.** Rely on ¶ 20 (coherent speech; spelling surname; discussing the alternator; identifying location), ¶ 26(b)–(c), ¶ 27 (omissions), and ¶ 50.

**Likely reply:** The FAC "extensively references" the DS-367 and report (¶¶ 25–28), so they're incorporated and the court may consider the HGN and FST entries. The reply may even attach the CHP 202 or affidavit.
**Rebuttal (pre-empt in one paragraph):** Even if incorporated, Khoja bars assuming the truth of a police narrative to dispute the pleading. The FAC specifically contests the observation entries and alleges material omissions (¶ 27). Arguments and documents first offered in reply should be disregarded (Zamani; *Provenz v. Miller*, 102 F.3d 1478, 1483 (9th Cir. 1996) [S; VERIFY]).

### 5.2 Count 4: warrantless door-opening (lead argument)

**Thesis:** At 00:56:43 Melano opened a parked car's passenger door and physically entered its interior. That is a search. Defendants offer two justifications. Emergency aid fails because the FAC pleads no objective sign of medical distress and the officers' own pre-entry words show none was perceived. Probable cause fails because a parking infraction never authorizes a vehicle search and an officer's hunch about an unidentified cup isn't probable cause.

**Rules:**
- Opening the door and leaning in is a search requiring probable cause or a particularized justification **[P]**: officers who lack "probable cause or any other particularized justification, such as a reasonable belief that the driver poses a danger-may not open the door to a vehicle and lean inside." *United States v. Ngumezi*, 980 F.3d 1285, 1287–89 (9th Cir. 2020) [P text; VERIFY reporter pins]. Physical intrusion is "constitutionally significant" because the officer "entered the interior space of the vehicle when he leaned in across the plane of the door" (citing *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1, 5 (2013); *United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012)). In most circumstances, "probable cause is necessary to conduct a warrantless search of a vehicle" (quoting *United States v. Rodgers*, 656 F.3d 1023, 1024 (9th Cir. 2011)) **[P]**.
- Emergency aid requires "an objectively reasonable basis for believing" that someone needs immediate aid. *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398, 403–06 (2006) [L]; *Michigan v. Fisher*, 558 U.S. 45, 47–49 (2009) (per curiam) [S]. Ninth Circuit two-part test: (1) an objectively reasonable basis, under the totality of circumstances, for concluding there was an immediate need to protect others or themselves from serious harm; and (2) a search scope and manner reasonable to meet that need. *United States v. Snipe*, 515 F.3d 947, 951–52 (9th Cir. 2008) [S; VERIFY].
- The emergency exception is "narrow" and its boundaries "rigorously guarded"; it rests on community caretaking, not investigation. *Hopkins v. Bonvicino*, 573 F.3d 752, 763 (9th Cir. 2009) [S]. Hopkins involved officers investigating a minor collision after the driver had drunk a few beers, then entering his home; the court denied QI to two officers [S; VERIFY holding text]. Community caretaking is not a free-standing warrant exception. *Caniglia v. Strom*, 593 U.S. 194 (2021) [L] (home context; use for that principle only).
- A citable infraction does not authorize a vehicle search. *Knowles v. Iowa*, 525 U.S. 113 (1998) [S].
- Plain-view seizure needs probable cause that the item is contraband or evidence. *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321, 326–27 (1987) [VERIFY]. Also *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983) (plurality) (cited in Ngumezi **[P]** for the point that flashlight viewing is not a search; use only for that).
- Veh. Code § 21718(a)(1) excepts stops "necessary to avoid injury or damage to persons or property" [S; VERIFY current text on leginfo].

**Application (FAC only):**
- **Search:** ¶ 16 says Melano opened the passenger door; ¶ 55 says he opened it and entered or reached into the interior; ¶ 60 says he reached in, seized the cup, and smelled it. This is squarely Ngumezi.
- **No objective emergency:** ¶ 56 alleges an intact vehicle, no damage, smoke, fire, or debris, and an occupant "in a normal resting posture." ¶ 11 alleges he pulled onto the shoulder because he was fatigued. ¶ 14: at 00:55:53, before approach, "It's a rental. Huh."; at 00:56:07, "It wasn't here earlier when we passed by." ¶ 15: at 00:56:09, "I think it's booze." The officers' contemporaneous observations show what they *perceived*. Under Fisher the test is objective, so frame these as evidence of the objective facts available, not of motive. None of them describes distress, injury, or danger.
- **The scope prong defeats the motion even if a welfare check was justified.** Snipe's second prong asks whether the intrusion matched the need. Knocking, calling out, shining a light, or opening the *driver's* window are all less intrusive than opening a door and reaching in. Defendants never address scope.
- **Investigation is not emergency.** MTD III.B (8–9) concedes the alternative purpose was "to investigate a crime." Hopkins separates emergency aid (community caretaking) from exigency (investigation), and the latter requires probable cause.
- **§ 21718 doesn't help them.** (i) The FAC pleads fatigue (¶¶ 11–12), so the (a)(1) safety exception is plausible on the pleadings. (ii) Even a completed parking infraction doesn't authorize entering the vehicle (Knowles: no search incident to a citation). (iii) Devenpeck (MTD 8:17) concerns which offense supports an *arrest*. It doesn't create a vehicle-search power.
- **The "booze" cup isn't probable cause.** ¶ 15 pleads a cup "visible through the vehicle window" and Melano's "I think," formed "before any contact … and before any observation of his condition." A guess that an opaque or unidentified cup holds alcohol isn't a fair probability that contraband is inside. The FAC alleges the guess was wrong (¶ 17). MTD 8:27–9:2 relies on the guess alone.

**Motion's errors:**
- Calls Mincey a Ninth Circuit holding (MTD 7:27–28: "The Ninth Circuit has held … See Mincey v. Arizona"). Mincey is a Supreme Court case, decided in the home-entry, homicide-scene context.
- Never mentions Ngumezi, though the FAC cites it four times (¶¶ 55–57, 61–62).
- Argues Plaintiff was "unconscious" (MTD 7:25–8:8) against the FAC's "asleep."
- Argues "Defendants could not have known [there was no emergency] before opening the door" (MTD 8:9–10). Under Snipe the burden is to show an objective basis *for* believing an emergency existed. Uncertainty is not a basis.

**Likely reply:** A driver unresponsive behind the wheel on a freeway shoulder at 1 a.m. is a classic welfare check. Fisher says officers need not have ironclad proof, and hindsight is irrelevant. Also: no clearly established law on sleeping drivers.
**Rebuttal:** Fisher and Snipe require an objective basis, and the FAC pleads its absence. Scope still governs. QI is fact-dependent and premature (§ 5.8). **Honest risk:** this is the real battleground. Courts are often deferential to welfare checks on sleeping drivers in traffic positions. Search found no Ninth Circuit opinion squarely on point either way, and a Florida appellate case found a sleeping-driver door-opening unreasonable (§ 9, out-of-circuit [S]). Look for N.D. Cal. or E.D. Cal. decisions on sleeping-driver welfare checks before filing (Westlaw queue, § 10).

**SAC opportunity (F5):** Meza's own sworn statement says the knock woke Plaintiff *before* the door was opened, and Melano said "Open the window" three seconds before opening the door. If the video confirms this, re-plead ¶ 16. A responsive, awake occupant eliminates the emergency premise. **Don't use F5 in the opposition. It contradicts FAC ¶ 16 as filed and belongs in the amendment proffer.**

**Damages note:** Under *Lingo v. City of Salem*, 832 F.3d 953, 957–60 (9th Cir. 2016) [S; VERIFY pins], the exclusionary rule doesn't apply in § 1983 cases, and unlawfully obtained evidence can supply probable cause. FAC ¶ 58's "chain of events" theory (entry → arrest → blood draw) is therefore weak. Count 4 damages are those of the intrusion itself (dignitary harm, emotional distress, nominal damages), plus punitive damages if the conduct was reckless or callous. Don't overclaim causation in the opposition.

### 5.3 Count 5: seizure and search of the cup

**Thesis:** Reaching in, removing the cup, and smelling its contents were a further search and seizure beyond the door-opening. They need their own justification, and they inherit Count 4's defect.

**Rules:** Ngumezi **[P]** (physical intrusion plus an attempt to obtain information is a search, quoting Jones); Hicks [VERIFY]. On probable cause dissipating: *United States v. Lopez*, 482 F.3d 1067, 1073–74 (9th Cir. 2007) [L; "substantial, countering indicators" can vitiate probable cause, S]; *Sialoi v. ### 5.5 Count 1: judicial deception (blood-draw warrant)

**Thesis:** The FAC pleads the elements: specific sworn entries contradicted by the recording, omissions within the affiant's personal knowledge, and an alleged corrected affidavit lacking probable cause. There is no heightened pleading standard. Materiality requires comparing the actual affidavit with its corrected version, and defendants haven't put the affidavit before the Court.

**Rules:**
- Elements: deliberately or recklessly false statements or omissions, material to probable cause. *Galbraith v. County of Santa Clara*, 307 F.3d 1119, 1126 (9th Cir. 2002) [L] (also cited by the MTD).
- No heightened pleading: Galbraith overruled *Branch v. Tunnell*'s heightened-pleading rule. Chism describes Branch as "overruled on other grounds by Galbraith" **[P]**. [VERIFY the Galbraith passage itself.]
- Recklessness may be inferred when the false statements and omissions were within the affiant's personal knowledge **[P]**: "The most commonsense evidence that the officers acted with at least a reckless disregard for the truth is that the omissions and false statements contained in the affidavit were all facts that were within [the affiant's] personal knowledge." *Chism v. Washington State*, 661 F.3d 380 (9th Cir. 2011) [P text; VERIFY pins, ~386–88]. Intent and recklessness are fact questions once shown (Chism, quoting *Liston v. County of Riverside*, 120 F.3d 965, 974 (9th Cir. 1997)) **[P]**.
- Materiality: whether "the affidavit, once corrected and supplemented," would have provided a substantial basis for probable cause **[P]** (Chism, quoting *United States v. Stanert*, 762 F.2d 775, 782 (9th Cir. 1985)). It is a legal question.
- QI: the Ninth Circuit has "consistently applied the rule that summary judgment on the ground of qualified immunity is not appropriate once a plaintiff has made out a judicial deception claim" **[P]** (Chism, citing Liston and *Hervey v. Estes*, 65 F.3d 784, 788 (9th Cir. 1995)).

**Application (narrowed):**
- Use ¶ 26(b) (slurred, against ¶ 20's coherent conversation, spelling, and mechanical discussion), ¶ 26(c) (confused and disoriented, against correctly identifying Livermore and reading signage), and ¶ 27 (omissions: the urine identification, the advisement that the PAS was voluntary, FSTs declined because of the ground, no observed driving), all within Meza's personal knowledge (¶ 46).
- **Omit ¶ 26(a) (odor) and ¶ 26(d) (beverage).** If defendants raise them, say the opposition does not rely on them and that Plaintiff seeks leave to correct ¶ 26(d).
- Materiality: ¶ 30 pleads the corrected application. Defendants' materiality argument (MTD 6:24–28) depends on FST results the FAC doesn't plead and on "beverages," which the affidavit does not claim.

**Honest merits risk:** The real affidavit (F2) includes facts the FAC doesn't contest: the party, the wristband, the admission of driving, HGN, the knock and waking. With F1 in the background, a corrected affidavit will very likely still show probable cause for a blood warrant. **Decision for the SAC:** keep Count 1 only if the video gives strong contradictions beyond the odor entry, for example audibly clear speech throughout and the Romberg 38 s versus 43 s gap. Otherwise drop it and keep the warrant facts as background.

### 5.7 Count 6: retaliation (non-opposition with leave; best version if you fight)

**The unbriefed problem:** First Amendment retaliation requires constitutionally protected activity. Refusing a breath test is the exercise of a statutory right and a Fourth Amendment choice, not speech. *Ballentine v. Tucker*, 28 F.4th 54 (9th Cir. 2022) [L], involved chalk messages, which is expressive. *Camara v. Municipal Court*, 387 U.S. 523 (1967), is a Fourth Amendment case. Search found **no** Ninth Circuit authority treating refusal to consent to a search as First Amendment activity [S, negative result]. The closest analogy is *Moore v. Garnand* (9th Cir. 2023), where QI was granted in a case about silence during police questioning [S, unverified; adverse in posture]. Defendants didn't raise this point, but the Court can see it, and it can't be cured by argument.

**Nieves:** Retaliatory-arrest plaintiffs generally must plead and prove the absence of probable cause. *Nieves v. Bartlett*, 587 U.S. 391, 402 (2019) [L]. Trevino*, 602 U.S. 653 (2024) (per curiam) **[P]**: "The only express limit we placed on the sort of evidence a plaintiff may present for that purpose is that it must be objective." FAC ¶ 69 offers a general statutory argument, not objective evidence of how comparable drivers were treated.

**Motion's error to note even if conceding:** It applies Bostock's Title VII but-for definition (MTD 11:9–19) and never cites Nieves, the governing retaliatory-arrest framework that the FAC itself pleads (¶¶ 65, 69).

**If re-pleaded later:** Plead verbal criticism as the protected activity (01:09:23–01:09:54 "you're giving me all these tests…", "So I haven't passed any of your tests yet?"). Criticism of police is protected (*City of Houston v. Hill*, 482 U.S. 451, 461 (1987) [VERIFY]). Pair it with objective Nieves-exception evidence. Realistic value is low.
