 1                          MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

 2    I.    INTRODUCTION

 3          This case involves a search of Plaintiff Brendan Newanforbi’s (“Plaintiff”) vehicle and the

 4    subsequent arrest of Plaintiff for driving under the influence (“DUI”). Plaintiff alleges that the

 5    CHP officers began a DUI investigation and arrested Plaintiff for driving under the influence after

 6    searching Plaintiff’s vehicle without probable cause or a warrant while Plaintiff was resting in his

 7    car on the on the side of Interstate 580 East. Plaintiff further alleges that the arrest triggered a

 8    parole violation hold and that he feared for his safety while in custody awaiting arraignment.

 9          Based upon the above, Plaintiff filed suit against Defendant State of California, by and

10    through the California Highway Patrol (“Defendant CHP”), and CHP Officers Santiago Meza-

11    Gonzalez (“Defendant Meza-Gonzalez”) and Jose Melano (“Defendant Melano”) (collectively

12    “Defendants”). While the Complaint fails to specify which causes of actions are brought against

13    which defendants, the Complaint states that Defendant Meza-Gonzalez and Defendant Melano

14    are sued in both their individual and official capacities. The Complaint contains three causes of

15    action all brought pursuant to 42 U.S.C. section 1983, alleging unreasonable search and seizure,

16    violation of due process, and a Monell liability claim.

17          This Motion to Dismiss with Motion to Strike is brought on behalf of all Defendants

18    pursuant to Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) and 12(f). Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and

19    Melano should be granted qualified immunity for Plaintiff’s claims against them in their

20    individual capacities as no constitutional violation occurred and/or the law is not clearly

21    established regarding the particularized allegations in this case.

22          Plaintiff’s official claims against Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano and Monell

23    liability claim against the State likewise fail as a matter of law because such claims are barred by

24    the Eleventh Amendment and the State is not a “person” under 42 U.S.C. section 1983.

25          Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and attorneys’ fees should be stricken as the

26    Complaint fails to allege any facts supporting an award of punitive damages and Plaintiff is not

27    entitled to attorneys’ fees as a pro se litigant.

 1    II.   BACKGROUND FACTS

 2          According to the Complaint, in the early morning hours of November 1, 2024, Plaintiff was

 3    resting in a rental Tesla on the shoulder of Interstate 580 after feeling fatigued. (ECF No. 1

 4    Complaint, ¶ 10.) CHP Officers Meza-Gonzalez and Melano came up to Plaintiff’s vehicle and,

 5    upon observing a cup in the center console, stated: “I think it’s booze.” (Id. at ¶ 11.) The officers

 6    proceeded to order Plaintiff out of his vehicle, questioned Plaintiff, and subjected Plaintiff to field

 7    sobriety tests. (Id. at ¶ 14.) Plaintiff was then placed in handcuffs and transported for chemical

 8    testing under suspicion of DUI. (Id. at ¶ 17.) No charges were filed against Plaintiff, (Id. at ¶ 18)

 9    but the arrest triggered a parole hold and Plaintiff alleges that he feared for his safety while in

10    custody awaiting arraignment. (Id. at ¶ 19.)

11    III. A MOTION TO DISMISS IS APPROPRIATE

12          A defendant may assert a defense that a complaint served upon him fails to state a claim

13    upon which relief may be granted by way of a motion pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure

14    12(b)(6). The purpose of a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) is to test the legal sufficiency of

15    the claims asserted in the complaint. Navarro v. Black, 250 F.3d 729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001).

16    Although a complaint does not need to state detailed factual allegations, “a plaintiff’s obligation

17    to provide the grounds of his ‘entitlement to relief’ requires more than labels and conclusions, and

18    a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v.

19    Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). A complaint that alleges “naked assertions” that are “devoid

20    a further factual enhancement” will not suffice. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 667 (2009).

21    Although generally in reviewing a motion to dismiss the court must accept as true all allegations

22    contained in the subject Complaint, “threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action,

23    supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id. at 678.

24    IV.   ARGUMENT

25          A.     Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano are Entitled to Qualified
                   Immunity for Plaintiff’s Claims Against Them in Their Personal
26                 Capacities.

27          “Qualified immunity shields federal and state officials from money damages unless a

28    plaintiff pleads facts showing (1) that the official violated a statutory or constitutional right, and

 1    (2) that the right was clearly established at the time of the challenged conduct.” Ashcroft v. al-

 2    Kidd, 563 U.S. 731, 735 (2011); accord Chavez v. Robinson, 12 F.4th 978, 995 (9th Cir. 2021).

 3                  1.    No constitutional violation exists under facts pled by the complaint.
 4          To establish a claim of unlawful search and seizure and violation of due process, Plaintiff

 5    must assert Defendants did not have probable cause to effectuate a search of Plaintiff’s vehicle

 6    and/or arrest Plaintiff. “[A] warrantless arrest [or search] by a law officer is reasonable under the

 7    Fourth Amendment where there is probable cause to believe that a criminal offense has been or is

 8    being committed.” Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146, 152 (2004) (citations omitted). Probable

 9    cause exists when a prudent person would have concluded there was a fair probability that the
10    suspect had committed a crime, based on the totality of the circumstances known to the officer.

11    Crowe v. County of San Diego, 608 F.3d 406, 434 (9th Cir. 2010). However, fair probability does

12    not require “certainty or even a preponderance of the evidence.” United States v. Gourde, 440

13    F.3d 1065, 1069 (9th Cir.2006). Whether probable cause existed on the facts known to the

14    defendant is a question of law for the court. Roberts v. McAfee, Inc., 660 F.3d 1156, 1164 (9th

15    Cir. 2011).

16          California Vehicle Code section 23152(a) prohibits anyone from driving a vehicle while

17    under the influence of alcohol. (Cal. Veh. Code § 23152(a)).

18          The allegations in Plaintiff’s Complaint regarding the search of Plaintiff’s vehicle and

19    Plaintiff’s subsequent arrest are as follows:
20          1. Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano encountered Plaintiff while he was “resting” in

21    his vehicle on the side of Interstate 580 East at approximately 12:54 a.m. on November 1, 2024.

22    (Complaint ¶ 10.)

23          2. Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano observed a cup in the center console of

24    Plaintiff’s vehicle, and one officer remarked: “I think it’s booze.” (Complaint ¶ 11.)

25          3. Plaintiff was subsequently ordered out of his vehicle, questioned, and subjected to field

26    sobriety tests. (Complaint ¶ 14.)

 1          4. Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano conducted an “invasive” search of Plaintiff’s

 2    vehicle, which included reaching into cupholders, examining the central console area, and

 3    scanning the footwells and floorboards of the vehicle. (Complaint ¶ 15.)

 6          Probable cause existed to search Plaintiff’s vehicle and arrest Plaintiff on suspicion of DUI

 7    based upon the totality of circumstances as shown by the facts alleged in the Complaint. The

 8    Complaint alleges that the investigatory stop began when Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano

 9    stated that they saw a cup of what they believed to be alcohol in Plaintiff’s vehicle. (Complaint ¶
10    11.) Defendants therefore had probable cause to believe that Plaintiff was in violation of

11    California Vehicle Code section 23223, which prohibits possessing an open container of alcohol

12    in a vehicle on a highway. (Cal. Veh. Code § 23223). Furthermore, Plaintiff was arrested for

13    chemical testing only after Defendants conducted field sobriety tests and after Plaintiff

14    (presumably) refused a breathalyzer test. (Complaint ¶ ¶ 17, 18.)

15          While Plaintiff’s Complaint repeatedly denies that any signs of intoxication or evidence of

16    a crime were found (Complaint ¶¶ 15, 16), these allegations are conclusory statements presented

17    without factual support and are not entitled to the presumption of truth. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.

18    Notably, Plaintiff does not allege how he could have evaluated the results of his field sobriety

19    tests or known whether he displayed certain signs of intoxication, such as bloodshot eyes, which
20    would only be apparent to another observer.

21          For the reasons stated above, probable cause existed for both Plaintiff’s arrest and the

22    search of his vehicle. Therefore, Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano are entitled to qualified

23    immunity on all of Plaintiff’s causes of action against them in their individual capacities.

                          probable cause did not exist in the circumstances as pled in the
25                        complaint.
26          Assuming, arguendo, that no probable cause existed to support the search of Plaintiff’s

27    vehicle and/or for Plaintiff’s arrest, Defendants Gonzalez and Melano would still be entitled to

 1    qualified immunity as it was not “clearly established” that probable cause to search and arrest did

 2    not exist in the circumstances as pled by the Complaint.

 3          Qualified immunity, as it relates to arrests, is afforded to officers if they had probable cause

 4    to make the arrest, or if the officers reasonably believed they had probable cause to make the

 5    arrest. Rosenbaum v. Washoe County, 663, F.3d 1071, 1076 (9th Cir. 2011). This applies to

 6    knowledge at the time, and not whether or not an independent prosecutor declined to press

 7    charges, or the plaintiff was found innocent. Id. An officer’s decision to search or arrest is

 8    protected by qualified immunity unless “all reasonable officers would agree that there was no

 9    probable cause in this instance.” Johnson v. Barr 79 F.4th 996, 1005 (9th Cir. 2023).
10          “While there does not have to be ‘a case directly on point,’ existing precedent must place

11    the lawfulness of the particular arrest or search ‘beyond debate.’” District of Columbia v. Wesby,

12    583 U.S. 48, 64 (2018) (quoting al-Kidd, 563 U.S. at 741). “The plaintiff bears the burden of

13    proof that the right allegedly violated was clearly established at the time of the alleged

14    misconduct.” Romero v. Kitsap Cty., 931 F.2d 624, 627 (9th Cir. 1991)).

15          Here, there is no “clearly established” law that should have put Defendants Meza-Gonzalez

16    and Melano on notice that all reasonable officers would believe that probable cause did not exist

17    in the circumstances as pled in the Complaint. To the contrary, it is clearly established that

18    probable cause exists when “a prudent person would have concluded there was a fair probability

19    that the suspect had committed a crime, based on the totality of the circumstances known to the
20    officer.” Crowe v. County of San Diego, 608 F.3d 406, 434 (9th Cir. 2010). Here, Plaintiff’s

21    conclusory allegation that “no evidence of intoxication was found” notwithstanding, there is no

22    “clearly established law” holding that probable cause for a vehicle search and/or arrest does not

23    exist after Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano found Plaintiff “resting” in his vehicle on the

24    side of the freeway (Complaint ¶ 10), saw what appeared to be alcohol in the vehicle (Complaint

25    ¶ 11), conducted field sobriety tests on Plaintiff (Complaint ¶ 14), and Plaintiff refused chemical

26    testing (Complaint ¶ ¶ 17, 18).

 1            B.    Plaintiff’s Monell Claim Against the State and Official Capacity Claims
                    Against Defendants Meza-Gonzales and Melano Fail as a Matter of Law
 2                  Due to Eleventh Amendment Sovereign Immunity and Because the State is
                    Not a Person Under Section 1983.
 3

 4            The Complaint brings a Monell liability claim against the State and brings claims against

 5    Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano in their official capacities. (Complaint ¶¶ 7, 8, 24, 25.)

 6    Plaintiff’s official capacity claims and Monell claim against the State are barred by the Eleventh

 7    Amendment and neither the State nor individuals sued in their official capacity qualify as

 8    “persons” under 42 U.S.C. section 1983.

 9            A damages action against a state official in his or her official capacity is treated like a suit

10    against the State itself. Hartmann v. California Dep't of Corr. & Rehab., 707 F.3d 1114, 1127

11    (9th Cir. 2013). That means absent consent from the State, “Eleventh Amendment immunity...

12    shields state officials from official capacity suits.” Krainski v. Nevada ex rel. Bd. of Regents of

13    Nevada Sys. of Higher Educ., 616 F.3d 963, 967 (9th Cir. 2010). Although a narrow exception

14    exists for prospective relief under the Ex Parte Young doctrine, Plaintiff seeks no prospective

15    relief here (see Complaint Prayer for Relief). Because the State has not consented to this damages

16    action, Eleventh Amendment sovereign immunity bars all claims against Defendants Meza-

17    Gonzalez and Melano in their official capacity, as well as Plaintiff’s Monell liability claim against

18    the State.

19            Moreover, Plaintiff cannot sue the State or Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano in their

20    official capacity because the State is not a “person” under Section 1983 and the Complaint

21    contains no claims for injunctive relief. As the Supreme Court explained in Will v. Michigan

22    Dep't of State Police, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989), a “State is not a ‘person’ within the meaning of §

23    1983 ...[.]” Because “a suit against a state official in his or her official capacity is” essentially “a

24    suit against the State itself,” “officials acting in their official capacities are [not] ‘persons’ under §

25    1983” and thus cannot be sued under Section 1983. Id. at 71. For these reasons, Plaintiff’s claims

26    against Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano in their official capacity and Plaintiff’s Monell

27    liability claim against the State in this damages-only action are not cognizable under Section

28    1983.

 1          C.     Plaintiff’s Prayer for Attorneys’ Fees and for Punitive Damages Should Be
                   Stricken.
 2

 3                 1.     Plaintiff has not alleged facts supporting punitive damages.

 4          Plaintiff's request for punitive damages should be stricken because Plaintiff has not alleged

 5    any facts giving rise to punitive damages. A Motion to Strike “may be used to strike any part of

 6    the Prayer for Relief when the damages sought are not recoverable as a matter of law.” Bureerong

 7    v. Uvawas, 922 F.Supp. 1450, 1479 n. 34 (CD. Cal. 1996); Wilkerson v. Butler, 229 F.R.D. 166,

 8    172 (E.D. Cal. 2005) (stating that “a motion to strike is appropriate to address requested relief,

 9    such as punitive damages, which is not recoverable as a matter of law.”).

10          A jury can assess punitive damages in a federal Section 1983 action “when the defendant’s

11    conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous

12    indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983).

13    Here, Plaintiff's Complaint is devoid of any allegations that may support an award of punitive

14    damages. Accordingly, Plaintiff's prayer for punitive damages found in Prayer for Relief #2 of

15    the Complaint should be stricken.

16          D.     Plaintiff is not entitled to attorneys’ fees as a pro se litigant.

17          Plaintiff seeks attorneys’ fees as part of his Prayer for Relief. (Complaint Prayer for Relief

18    #3.) While 42 U.S.C. § 1988 allows for attorney's fees for plaintiffs who prevail in Section 1983

19    actions, an award of attorneys’ fees is not available to pro se litigants. See Kay v. Ehrler, 499 U.S.

20    432, 438 (1991). Accordingly, Plaintiff's prayer for attorneys’ fees found in Prayer for Relief #3

21    of the Complaint should be stricken.

22    V.    CONCLUSION

23          For the foregoing reasons, Defendants respectfully request that the Court dismiss all claims

24    within Plaintiff’s Complaint with prejudice and strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and

25    attorneys’ fees from the Complaint.
