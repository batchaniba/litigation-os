H| MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES
H| I. INTRODUCTION
P| Defendants ask the Court to dismiss, with prejudice, a First Amended Complaint ("FAC") that describes a roadside encounter using timestamps from the officers' own dashcam. They do not show that the FAC's facts, accepted as true, fail to state a claim. Instead, they replace those facts with their own. They call Plaintiff "unconscious" where the FAC alleges a tired driver who had pulled off the road to sleep. They treat contested entries in Defendant Meza-Gonzalez's reports as established. They offer a "simple explanation" for discrepancies the FAC alleges. And they treat the FAC's silence about field sobriety results as an admission of results the FAC never pleads. Rule 12(b)(6) permits none of that.
P| The motion also misstates the governing law. It never addresses United States v. Ngumezi, 980 F.3d 1285, 1288-89 (9th Cir. 2020), which holds that officers who lack probable cause or another particularized justification may not open a car door and lean inside. That is the conduct alleged in Count IV. Defendants defend the entry with an emergency-aid theory that no pleaded fact supports, and with a parking-infraction theory that would not authorize a vehicle search even if an infraction were established. To support that theory, the motion quotes Devenpeck v. Alford with the words "[or search]" added to a sentence that concerns only arrests. And Defendants invoke Rule 12(f) to strike punitive damages, a use the Ninth Circuit has squarely rejected. Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co., 618 F.3d 970, 974-75 (9th Cir. 2010).
P| Plaintiff opposes the motion as to Counts I, III, IV, and V and as to the punitive-damages request. To narrow the issues, Plaintiff does not oppose dismissal of Counts II and VI without prejudice and with leave to amend, and he confirms that he does not seek attorney's fees for his own work as a self-represented litigant. If the Court finds any other claim deficient, Plaintiff requests leave to amend and identifies below the specific amendments he would make.
H| II. STATEMENT OF ISSUES TO BE DECIDED
N| 1.|Whether Counts IV and V state Fourth Amendment claims where the FAC alleges that Defendant Melano opened a closed passenger door, entered the vehicle, and seized and examined a cup, without a warrant, consent, or any objectively observable emergency.
N| 2.|Whether Count III states a claim for arrest without probable cause where Defendants' contrary theory depends on facts the FAC does not plead and on report entries the FAC specifically disputes.
N| 3.|Whether Count I states a judicial-deception claim, and whether materiality can be decided against Plaintiff when the warrant application is not before the Court.
N| 4.|Whether qualified immunity supports dismissal at the pleading stage on Defendants' generalized showing.
N| 5.|Whether Rule 12(f) authorizes striking a request for punitive damages.
N| 6.|Whether any dismissal should be with leave to amend.
H| III. FACTS ALLEGED IN THE FAC
P| On the night of October 31 to November 1, 2024, Plaintiff, a Class A commercial driver, became fatigued while driving east on Interstate 580. He left the travel lanes, parked on the right shoulder near the Isabel Avenue exit in Livermore, and rested in the driver's seat. FAC ¶¶ 1, 7, 11-12. No officer saw him drive. Id. ¶ 11.
P| At 00:54:36, the dashcam of a California Highway Patrol car occupied by Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano began recording as the patrol car stopped behind Plaintiff's car. Id. ¶ 13. Before approaching, Melano remarked, "It's a rental. Huh." Id. ¶ 14. The officers walked up and shined flashlights through the windows. Id. Melano said, "It wasn't here earlier when we passed by," and then, referring to a cup visible through a window, "I think it's booze." Id. ¶¶ 14-15. Melano formed that hypothesis before either officer had spoken to Plaintiff or observed anything about his condition. Id. ¶ 15.
P| At 00:56:43, Melano opened the front passenger door. Id. ¶ 16. Plaintiff had not consented, and there was no warrant. Id. The car was intact and undamaged; there was no smoke, fire, or crash debris; and Plaintiff was in a normal resting posture. Id. ¶ 56. No emergency was articulated on the recording. Id. ¶¶ 16, 56.
P| Between 00:58:49 and 00:59:07, Melano reached into the car, removed the cup from the console area, smelled it, and announced: "Is it water? No this is piss. This is pee." Id. ¶¶ 17, 60. The officers did not end the encounter. Id. ¶ 19. They ordered Plaintiff out onto uneven ground and administered field sobriety exercises; Melano acknowledged on the recording that the ground was unsuitable and declined to administer certain balance exercises for that reason. Id. Throughout, Plaintiff spoke coherently. He spelled his surname, discussed the mechanical design of the rental car, explained commercial-driver fatigue protocols, and correctly identified his location as Livermore. Id. ¶ 20.
P| At 01:10:54, Meza-Gonzalez told Plaintiff that the preliminary alcohol screening ("PAS") test was voluntary: "You're not required to do this." Id. ¶ 21. Nine seconds later he added: "If you choose to not consent to it, then it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not." Id. ¶ 22. Plaintiff declined. Id. ¶ 23. Meza-Gonzalez arrested him seconds later, with no new observation in between. Id. ¶ 24.
P| Meza-Gonzalez then executed sworn documents that were used to obtain a warrant for a compelled blood draw. Id. ¶¶ 25, 29, 40. The FAC alleges that those documents described Plaintiff's speech as "slurred" and described him as "very confused and disoriented," descriptions the FAC alleges the recording contradicts. Id. ¶ 26(b)-(c). It alleges that the documents omitted that the suspected alcohol had been identified as urine, that certain exercises were not administered because of the ground, that the PAS had been described as voluntary, and that no officer had seen Plaintiff drive. Id. ¶¶ 27, 30. Plaintiff's blood was drawn by force under the warrant, and he was booked into Santa Rita Jail. Id. ¶¶ 29, 31. Four days later, CHP issued a Penal Code section 851.6 certificate describing the event as "a detention only, not an arrest." Id. ¶ 32. The District Attorney filed no charges. Id. ¶ 34.
H| IV. LEGAL STANDARD
P| A complaint survives Rule 12(b)(6) if it pleads "factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The Court accepts well-pleaded facts as true and draws reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. Id.; Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555-56 (2007). "[A] well-pleaded complaint may proceed even if it strikes a savvy judge that actual proof of the facts alleged is improbable . . . ." Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556. And "[i]f there are two alternative explanations, one advanced by defendant and the other advanced by plaintiff, both of which are plausible, plaintiff's complaint survives a motion to dismiss." Starr v. Baca, 652 F.3d 1202, 1216 (9th Cir. 2011). Where the plaintiff is self-represented, courts remain obligated "to construe the pleadings liberally and to afford the petitioner the benefit of any doubt." Hebbe v. Pliler, 627 F.3d 338, 342 (9th Cir. 2010) (quoting Bretz v. Kelman, 773 F.2d 1026, 1027 n.1 (9th Cir. 1985) (en banc)).
P| The motion cites page 667 of Iqbal for the "naked assertion[s]" and "merely consistent with" language. Mot. 5:9-10, 6:8-10, 12:1-3. That page recounts the case's background; the pleading discussion, which quotes Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557, appears at page 678. The standard itself is not in dispute, but it does not permit the Court to adopt Defendants' version of disputed facts.
P| Defendants attached no exhibit and requested no judicial notice. Even when a document is incorporated by reference, "it is improper to assume the truth of an incorporated document if such assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint." Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc., 899 F.3d 988, 1003 (9th Cir. 2018). That rule is "consistent with the prohibition against resolving factual disputes at the pleading stage." Id. It applies with full force to a police report whose accuracy the complaint specifically disputes.
P| Citations to "Mot." are to the internal page and line numbers of ECF No. 22. The motion's caption and footers bear the number 3:26-cv-08271-ASK; the case number is 4:26-cv-08271-ASK. And although the memorandum relies on more than twenty decisions, its table of authorities lists three cases. Mot. ii; see Civil L.R. 7-4(a)(2). Plaintiff notes these points only so that the Court can locate the authorities addressed below.
H| V. ARGUMENT
S| A.|The Motion Rests on Facts the FAC Does Not Plead and on Inferences Drawn Against Plaintiff.
P| The motion's method is to recast the FAC. It opens with a narrative the FAC does not contain: that the officers "stopped to check if [Plaintiff] needed assistance" and "noticed a strong smell of alcohol" when they opened the door. Mot. 2:7-9. Neither assertion appears in the FAC, and neither may be considered on this motion. Four further examples illustrate the problem.
B| Defendants describe Plaintiff as "unconscious." Mot. 2:8, 3:13, 8:8. The FAC alleges sleep, not unconsciousness: a fatigued driver who left the travel lanes to rest and was asleep in a normal resting posture in an intact car. FAC ¶¶ 11, 16, 56. Nothing in the FAC suggests injury, illness, or distress.
B| Defendants offer "[t]he simple explanation" and "[a]nother possibility" for discrepancies between Meza-Gonzalez's sworn statements and the recording. Mot. 6:4-6. Choosing between competing explanations is fact-finding. Starr, 652 F.3d at 1216.
B| Defendants rely on "slurred speech and other signs of intoxication" as established facts. Mot. 10:1-3, 10:13. Those are the report entries the FAC alleges the recording contradicts. FAC ¶¶ 20, 26(b)-(c). Accepting them as true would use a disputed document to defeat the pleading, which Khoja forbids. 899 F.3d at 1003.
B| Defendants call the FAC "conspicuously silent" about field sobriety results and treat that silence as though it established adverse results. Mot. 6:27-28; see id. at 10:21-24. A complaint need not plead the defendant's evidence, and the absence of an allegation is not an admission.
P| Defendants also misread the FAC's facts. The motion states that "Officer Gonzalez determines that the cup in Plaintiff's vehicle is filled not with booze, but with urine." Mot. 10:4-5. The FAC alleges that Melano made that identification. FAC ¶ 17. These are not technicalities. Each one converts a disputed or misread fact into a premise for dismissal.
S| B.|Count IV States a Claim for Unlawful Warrantless Entry.
S2| 1.|Opening the passenger door was a search.
P| The Ninth Circuit has held that police officers who have reasonable suspicion sufficient to justify a traffic stop, "but who lack probable cause or any other particularized justification, such as a reasonable belief that the driver poses a danger," may not "open the door to a vehicle and lean inside." Ngumezi, 980 F.3d at 1288. The court "appl[ied] a bright-line rule that opening a door and entering the interior space of a vehicle constitutes a Fourth Amendment search." Id. at 1289. It explained that the officer's physical intrusion into the car "is constitutionally significant," and that although "the intrusion here may have been modest, the Supreme Court has never suggested that the magnitude of a physical intrusion is relevant to the Fourth Amendment analysis." Id. In most circumstances, "probable cause is necessary to conduct a warrantless search of a vehicle." Id. at 1288-89 (quoting United States v. Rodgers, 656 F.3d 1023, 1024 (9th Cir. 2011)).
P| The FAC alleges that kind of intrusion. Melano opened the closed passenger door of Plaintiff's car without a warrant or consent, FAC ¶¶ 16, 56, and then reached through the open door into the interior, where he removed and smelled the cup, id. ¶¶ 17, 60. Under Ngumezi's bright-line rule, what matters is the physical intrusion into the vehicle, not how far the officer leaned. 980 F.3d at 1289. Opening a closed door to see and question the occupant, followed by reaching inside to seize an object, is that intrusion. The motion does not cite Ngumezi, even though the FAC relies on it. FAC ¶¶ 55-57, 61-62. And while Ngumezi recognizes that a "particularized justification" other than probable cause can suffice, the only one Defendants offer is emergency aid, which, as shown next, the FAC's facts do not support. Nothing on the face of the FAC establishes any exception to the warrant requirement.
S2| 2.|The FAC pleads no objective basis for emergency aid.
P| The emergency-aid exception permits a warrantless entry only when officers have "an objectively reasonable basis for believing that an occupant is seriously injured or imminently threatened with such injury." Brigham City v. Stuart, 547 U.S. 398, 400 (2006); see id. at 406; Michigan v. Fisher, 558 U.S. 45, 47 (2009) (per curiam). In this circuit, the government must show both (1) an objectively reasonable basis, considering the totality of the circumstances, for concluding that there was an immediate need to protect others or themselves from serious harm, and (2) that the scope and manner of the search were reasonable to meet that need. United States v. Snipe, 515 F.3d 947, 951-52 (9th Cir. 2008). The emergency and exigency exceptions are "narrow," and their boundaries are "rigorously guarded." Hopkins v. Bonvicino, 573 F.3d 752, 763 (9th Cir. 2009) (home entry) (quoting United States v. Stafford, 416 F.3d 1068, 1073 (9th Cir. 2005)).
P| Defendants rely on Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385 (1978), which they describe as a holding of "[t]he Ninth Circuit." Mot. 7:27-28. Mincey is a Supreme Court decision that rejected a warrantless search of a homicide scene. It states the general emergency principle, but it supplies no fact that would make Plaintiff's situation an emergency.
P| The FAC alleges facts that negate an emergency. The car was intact, with no damage, smoke, fire, or debris, and Plaintiff was asleep in a normal resting posture. FAC ¶¶ 16, 56. He had pulled off the road because he was tired, consistent with safety guidance for drowsy drivers. Id. ¶¶ 11-12. A person sleeping in a parked car is not, without more, a person in medical distress; sleep is what tired drivers are supposed to do instead of driving. The pre-entry remarks the FAC pleads concern the car and a suspicion of crime, "It's a rental" and "I think it's booze," and none describes a sign of injury, illness, or distress. Id. ¶¶ 14-15, 56. Under Fisher the test is objective, and Plaintiff does not rest on the officers' motives. But what officers say they are observing is strong evidence of the objective facts available to them, and those facts disclosed no emergency.
P| Hopkins illustrates the point. There, officers investigating a suspected drunk-driving collision entered the driver's home and defended the entry as a response to a possible medical emergency. The Ninth Circuit held that the facts known to them supplied no objectively reasonable basis to believe that he needed immediate medical attention. 573 F.3d at 764-66. A suspicion of drinking is not evidence of a medical emergency, in a house or in a car.
P| Defendants' only response is that the officers "could not have known" that no emergency existed "before opening the door." Mot. 8:9-10. That inverts the test. The question is whether objective facts gave the officers a reasonable basis to believe aid was immediately needed, not whether opening the door could dispel uncertainty. Every driver resting in a parked car presents some uncertainty. That does not make every parked car an emergency.
P| The motion also ignores Snipe's second requirement: the scope and manner of the intrusion must be reasonable to meet the asserted need. A warrantless search justified by an exigency "must be 'strictly circumscribed by the exigencies which justify its initiation.'" Mincey, 437 U.S. at 393 (quoting Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 25-26 (1968)). Opening a passenger door and reaching into the interior is not the only, or the least intrusive, way to check on a sleeping driver. Whether the officers' conduct fit any welfare concern is a factual question that cannot be resolved against Plaintiff on the pleadings. If the Court considers the FAC's account of the entry too thin, Plaintiff would amend to plead the full sequence: the officers' knocking woke him; Melano told him to "[o]pen the window"; and Melano opened the passenger door about three seconds later, before Plaintiff could comply, asking whether he was "okay" only after the door was open. See Part V.H. Those facts make any emergency rationale weaker, not stronger.
S2| 3.|A suspected parking infraction does not authorize a vehicle search.
P| Defendants argue in the alternative that, if there was no emergency, Plaintiff's stop violated Vehicle Code section 21718, giving the officers grounds "to investigate a crime." Mot. 8:14-23. The argument fails twice. First, section 21718(a)(1) permits stopping on a freeway "[w]hen necessary to avoid injury or damage to persons or property." The FAC alleges that Plaintiff left the travel lanes because he was fatigued, consistent with commercial-driver safety principles. FAC ¶¶ 11-12. On the pleadings, the exception is at least plausibly alleged. Second, even a completed infraction does not authorize a search of the vehicle. Knowles v. Iowa, 525 U.S. 113, 117-19 (1998).
P| Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146 (2004), on which Defendants rely, does not say otherwise. It addresses whether probable cause for some offense supports an arrest. Id. at 152-53. The motion quotes it as stating that "[a] warrantless arrest [or search] by a law officer is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment where there is probable cause to believe that a criminal offense has been or is being committed." Mot. 9:10-12. The bracketed words "[or search]" are Defendants' addition; the Court's sentence concerns only a "warrantless arrest." Devenpeck, 543 U.S. at 152. Devenpeck creates no power to enter a car to investigate a parking infraction.
S2| 4.|An officer's guess about a cup is not probable cause.
P| Finally, Defendants contend that Melano's remark, "I think it's booze," supplied probable cause. Mot. 8:24-9:2. The FAC alleges that Melano formed that hypothesis about a cup seen through the window "before any contact with Plaintiff and before any observation of his condition." FAC ¶ 15. An officer may seize an item in plain view only if he has probable cause to believe it is contraband or evidence. Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321, 326-27 (1987). Defendants' own motion concedes that the officers "did not discover the cup in Plaintiff's vehicle contained urine until they searched Plaintiff's vehicle." Mot. 8:28-9:2. (The motion cites FAC ¶ 16 for that point; the FAC alleges the urine identification in ¶ 17.) That concession confirms that the cup's contents were not apparent from outside, and that the search, not the view, produced the information. Information obtained through an intrusion cannot justify the intrusion that produced it. At a minimum, whether a guess about an unidentified cup amounted to probable cause is a fact-dependent question that cannot be resolved against Plaintiff on a motion to dismiss.
S| C.|Count V States a Claim for the Seizure and Search of the Cup.
P| Count V challenges conduct beyond opening the door. Melano reached inside, removed the cup from the console, smelled it, and examined its contents while Meza-Gonzalez directed Plaintiff's attention and movements. FAC ¶¶ 17, 60. A physical intrusion combined with an attempt to find something or to obtain information is a search. Ngumezi, 980 F.3d at 1289 (citing United States v. Jones, 565 U.S. 400, 408 n.5 (2012)). Removing the cup was also a seizure of Plaintiff's property, which required probable cause. Hicks, 480 U.S. at 326-27. Because Count IV plausibly alleges that the entry was unjustified, Count V's allegations that the officers then seized and examined an item inside the car state a claim for the same reasons.
P| The FAC further alleges that once Melano identified the liquid as urine, the basis for suspicion arising from that cup was gone, yet the officers continued the warrantless search and the detention. FAC ¶¶ 18, 61. Officers "may not disregard facts tending to dissipate probable cause." United States v. Lopez, 482 F.3d 1067, 1073 (9th Cir. 2007) (internal quotation marks omitted). Evidence that may support probable cause can be "vitiated by countervailing evidence." Id. at 1074. And when "additional information obtained at the scene" shows "less than a fair probability" of a crime, "continuation of the arrest is illegal." Sialoi v. City of San Diego, 823 F.3d 1223, 1232 (9th Cir. 2016) (quoting Lopez, 482 F.3d at 1073). The FAC plausibly alleges that the officers disregarded exactly that kind of dissipating fact here.
S| D.|Count III States a Claim for Arrest Without Probable Cause.
P| Probable cause to arrest exists when the facts known to the officer would lead a prudent person to believe that the suspect has committed an offense. Devenpeck, 543 U.S. at 152. The Court considers the totality of the circumstances. District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48, 60-61 (2018). On a motion to dismiss, the totality is the one the complaint pleads.
P| Taking every pleaded fact into account, including those Defendants emphasize, the FAC alleges the following as of the arrest at 01:11:15 to 01:11:33. Plaintiff was asleep in the driver's seat of a car stopped on the freeway shoulder shortly before 1:00 a.m., having pulled over because he was tired. FAC ¶¶ 11, 13-16. Melano suspected that a cup in the car held alcohol, and that cup turned out to hold urine. Id. ¶¶ 15, 17-18. Plaintiff spoke coherently and identified his location. Id. ¶ 20. No officer saw him drive. Id. ¶ 11. Field sobriety exercises were administered, but the officers declined to administer certain balance exercises because the ground was unsuitable, and the FAC pleads no result. Id. ¶ 19. And Plaintiff declined a preliminary test the officer had just described as voluntary. Id. ¶¶ 21-23. Viewed together, as Wesby requires, those facts describe a tired driver who did what tired drivers should do, not one a prudent officer would believe was driving under the influence. The one concrete fact that pointed toward alcohol, the cup, was dispelled at the scene, and officers may not disregard such facts. Sialoi, 823 F.3d at 1232-33.
P| Defendants' contrary argument depends on facts outside the FAC. They rely on "slurred speech and other signs of intoxication," Mot. 10:1-3, which are the report entries the FAC disputes, FAC ¶¶ 20, 26(b)-(c). And they rely on the FAC's silence about field-exercise results. Mot. 6:27-28, 10:21-24. Neither is a pleaded fact. What remains is that Plaintiff "was asleep in a vehicle parked on the side of the freeway with a cup of urine next to him." Mot. 10:19-20. That describes a tired driver who stopped driving. Even Defendants retreat from it, arguing only that "[a]t the very least" those facts gave them "probable cause to order Plaintiff to perform field sobriety tests." Id. at 10:20-21. Grounds to investigate are not probable cause to arrest.
P| The sequence of the arrest matters for an objective reason, not because of the officer's motive. Probable cause turns on the facts known, Devenpeck, 543 U.S. at 152, and the FAC alleges that no new fact emerged between the advisement that the PAS was voluntary and the arrest. The only intervening event was Plaintiff's refusal of that test, and Meza-Gonzalez arrested him seconds later. FAC ¶¶ 21-24. California law makes the PAS an investigative tool and requires the officer to advise the driver "of the person's right to refuse to take the preliminary alcohol screening test," Cal. Veh. Code § 23612(h)-(i), and a "passive refusal to consent to a warrantless search is privileged conduct which cannot be considered as evidence of criminal wrongdoing." United States v. Prescott, 581 F.2d 1343, 1351 (9th Cir. 1978). If the facts known before the refusal did not amount to probable cause, the refusal could not supply it. FAC ¶¶ 24, 50.
P| The FAC also pleads each officer's role. Meza-Gonzalez made the arrest, and Melano conducted the entry, the cup search, and the field exercises and took part in the joint investigation through the arrest. FAC ¶ 53. The motion does not challenge either officer's personal participation, and the Court "need not consider arguments raised for the first time in a reply brief." Zamani v. Carnes, 491 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2007).
S| E.|Count I States a Judicial-Deception Claim.
P| A judicial-deception claim requires facts showing that the defendant deliberately or recklessly made false statements or omissions in support of a warrant, and that those statements or omissions were material to the finding of probable cause. Chism v. Washington State, 661 F.3d 380, 386-89 (9th Cir. 2011). At the pleading stage, the question is whether the FAC plausibly alleges those elements. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. Defendants' reliance on Hervey v. Estes, 65 F.3d 784, 789 (9th Cir. 1995), for what a plaintiff must "ultimately establish," Mot. 5:19-23, addresses proof, not pleading. Chism describes the same "substantial showing" requirement as what a plaintiff needs for a judicial-deception claim "to survive summary judgment." 661 F.3d at 386.
P| The FAC meets the pleading standard. It identifies the affiant (Meza-Gonzalez), the sworn documents, the warrant they supported, and the compelled blood draw that followed. FAC ¶¶ 25, 29, 38-42. It identifies specific descriptions, namely that Plaintiff's speech was "slurred" and that he "seemed very confused and disoriented," and alleges the facts on the recording that contradict them: extended coherent conversation, spelling his surname, discussing the car's mechanics, correctly identifying his location, and reading distant signs. Id. ¶¶ 20, 26(b)-(c). And it identifies material omissions: that the cup suspected of holding alcohol had been identified as urine, that certain exercises were not administered because of the ground, that the PAS had been described as voluntary, and that no officer saw Plaintiff drive. Id. ¶ 27.
P| Recklessness is plausibly alleged because each challenged statement and omission concerned facts within Meza-Gonzalez's personal knowledge; he was present for the encounter he described. FAC ¶¶ 41, 46. The Ninth Circuit has called that circumstance "[t]he most commonsense evidence that the officers acted with at least a reckless disregard for the truth." Chism, 661 F.3d at 388. A factfinder may also infer recklessness where "the false statements and omissions contained in the affidavit all bolster the case for probable cause, which suggests that the mistakes were not the product of mere negligence." Id. The FAC alleges that pattern: each challenged description points toward impairment, and each omitted fact points away from it. FAC ¶¶ 26(b)-(c), 27. And once the required showing is made, "the question of intent or recklessness is a factual determination" for the trier of fact. Chism, 661 F.3d at 388 (quoting Liston v. County of Riverside, 120 F.3d 965, 974 (9th Cir. 1997)).
P| Defendants' materiality argument cannot be decided on this record. Materiality asks whether the warrant application, "once corrected and supplemented," would have provided a substantial basis for probable cause. Id. at 389 (quoting United States v. Stanert, 762 F.2d 775, 782 (9th Cir. 1985)). That inquiry requires the application itself, and Defendants have not placed it before the Court. The FAC pleads what the corrected application would show, FAC ¶ 30, and Defendants do not engage that allegation. Their argument instead keeps the disputed entries and relies on field-exercise results the FAC does not plead. Mot. 6:24-28. Materiality cannot be decided against Plaintiff on that basis. If the Court concludes that the FAC should identify the warrant application and its specific statements more precisely, the remedy is leave to amend, which Plaintiff requests in Part V.H.
S| F.|Plaintiff Does Not Oppose Dismissal of Counts II and VI With Leave to Amend.
P| To focus the issues, Plaintiff does not oppose dismissal of Count II (deliberate fabrication of evidence) and Count VI (First Amendment retaliation), provided that the dismissal is without prejudice and with leave to amend. Plaintiff notes one point so that the motion's framing does not shape any amended pleading.
P| Defendants read Caldwell v. City & County of San Francisco, 889 F.3d 1105 (9th Cir. 2018), as making a criminal charge a prerequisite to a fabrication claim. Mot. 7:8-17. Caldwell holds that "being criminally charged is enough" to show a deprivation of liberty. 889 F.3d at 1115. It does not hold that a charge is necessary. The elements of the claim are that the defendant deliberately fabricated evidence and that the fabrication caused a deprivation of liberty. Spencer v. Peters, 857 F.3d 789, 798 (9th Cir. 2017). And the Ninth Circuit has recognized deliberate-fabrication claims arising outside criminal prosecutions where a protected liberty or property interest is lost. Costanich v. Dep't of Soc. & Health Servs., 627 F.3d 1101, 1108 (9th Cir. 2010).
S| G.|Qualified Immunity Does Not Warrant Dismissal on This Record.
P| The motion's qualified-immunity argument addresses only whether there was probable cause to search and arrest. Mot. 12-13. It does not separately analyze the door entry, the seizure of the cup, or judicial deception. Qualified immunity is assessed on the facts alleged in the complaint, and dismissal on that ground at the pleading stage is proper only if the complaint fails to allege a violation of clearly established law. Keates v. Koile, 883 F.3d 1228, 1234-35 (9th Cir. 2018).
P| Count IV. The motion offers no clearly-established-law analysis of the door entry at all. Its immunity argument concerns only probable cause to search and arrest. Mot. 12:7-13:21. An argument about the entry raised for the first time in reply need not be considered. Zamani, 491 F.3d at 997. In any event, Ngumezi held in 2020, four years before this encounter, that opening a car door and entering the interior is a search that requires probable cause or another particularized justification. 980 F.3d at 1288-89. The emergency-aid exception has long required an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone needs immediate aid. Brigham City, 547 U.S. at 406; Snipe, 515 F.3d at 951-52; Hopkins, 573 F.3d at 763-64. Whether the facts here supplied that basis is a factual question on which the FAC pleads against Defendants. Qualified immunity cannot be decided by substituting "unconscious" for the FAC's allegations, and it cannot be resolved without a record of what the officers actually saw.
P| Count V. The same authorities, and the settled rule that a plain-view seizure requires probable cause, Hicks, 480 U.S. at 326-27, clearly established the right as to the cup.
P| Count III. The right to be free from arrest without probable cause is clearly established. Defendants invoke the rule that officers are immune if they reasonably believed probable cause existed. Mot. 12:14-18 (citing Rosenbaum v. Washoe County, 663 F.3d 1071, 1076 (9th Cir. 2011)). But that inquiry, too, is measured by the facts the officers knew. On the facts the FAC pleads, set out in Part V.D, a reasonable officer could not have believed that a driver found asleep after pulling over, whose suspected alcohol was urine, who spoke coherently, and who declined a voluntary test, had committed a crime. Defendants reach a different answer only by crediting the disputed report entries. That cannot be done on the pleadings.
P| Count I. The Ninth Circuit has "consistently applied the rule that summary judgment on the ground of qualified immunity is not appropriate once a plaintiff has made out a judicial deception claim." S| H.|The Prior Litigation Does Not Make Amendment Futile.
P| Leave to amend should be freely given when justice so requires. Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2). Absent prejudice, or a strong showing of another relevant factor, "there exists a presumption under Rule 15(a) in favor of granting leave to amend." Eminence Capital, LLC v. Aspeon, Inc., 316 F.3d 1048, 1052 (9th Cir. 2003). A court should grant leave "unless it determines that the pleading could not possibly be cured by the allegation of other facts." Lopez v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1130 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (quoting Doe v. United States, 58 F.3d 494, 497 (9th Cir. 1995)). A pro se complaint should not be dismissed without leave to amend unless "it is absolutely clear that the deficiencies of the complaint could not be cured by amendment." Akhtar v. Mesa, 698 F.3d 1202, 1212 (9th Cir. 2012) (internal quotation marks omitted). Defendants acknowledge that the Court must tell a pro se plaintiff of a pleading's deficiencies and provide an opportunity to cure them. Mot. 13:23-25 (citing Garity v. APWU Nat'l Lab. Org., 828 F.3d 848, 854 (9th Cir. 2016)).
P| Defendants' futility argument rests on the history of other cases, not on any defect that amendment could not cure. Mot. 13:22-14:21. Filings in other cases may be noticed for their existence, not for the truth of disputed matters in them. Khoja, 899 F.3d at 999. No court has adjudicated these claims on the merits. The Eastern District ruling the motion cites was a magistrate judge's recommendation, not a judgment, and it was issued on April 20, 2026, not in 2025 as the motion states. Mot. 14:8. The Alameda County action ended without any ruling on the merits. Newanforbi v. Serrato, No. 3:26-cv-05953-CRB, involves a different defendant and a different claim. In that related action, the Court screened a complaint resting on an overlapping factual record under 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2), applying the Rule 12(b)(6) standard, and found that it "supports the plausible inferences that CHP officers falsely imprisoned [Plaintiff] in November 2024 based on fabricated evidence." Order re Motion to Proceed In Forma Pauperis at 4:17-19, Newanforbi v. Serrato, No. 3:26-cv-05953-CRB (N.D. Cal. June 29, 2026), ECF No. 5; see FAC ¶ 35. That screening order is not a ruling on Defendants' arguments here, but it confirms that the core factual theory is not frivolous or incurable. This is Plaintiff's first amendment in this case, made as of right. Nothing in this history shows that the specific amendments below would be futile.

S| I.|Rule 12(f) Does Not Authorize Striking the Punitive-Damages Request.
P| "We therefore hold that Rule 12(f) does not authorize district courts to strike claims for damages on the ground that such claims are precluded as a matter of law." Whittlestone, 618 F.3d at 974-75. Defendants' authorities are district-court decisions that predate Whittlestone, and the motion does not cite Whittlestone. Mot. 14:26-15:2 (citing Bureerong v. Uvawas, 922 F. Supp. 1450 (C.D. Cal. 1996), and Wilkerson v. Butler, 229 F.R.D. 166 (E.D. Cal. 2005)). To the extent those decisions treat Rule 12(f) as a vehicle for deciding whether damages are recoverable as a matter of law, Whittlestone forecloses that use.
P| Punitive damages are available under section 1983 when a defendant's conduct involves "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). The FAC alleges that Melano entered a car without any articulated justification while voicing investigative suspicions, FAC ¶¶ 14-16, 56, and that Meza-Gonzalez swore to descriptions contradicted by events he personally witnessed, id. ¶¶ 26(b)-(c), 41, 46. Those allegations plausibly support punitive damages. Whether Plaintiff can prove the required state of mind is a question for a later stage.
P| Plaintiff confirms that he does not seek attorney's fees under 42 U.S.C. § 1988 for his own work as a self-represented litigant. See Kay v. Ehrler, 499 U.S. 432, 435 (1991). He preserves his request for taxable costs under Rule 54(d), FAC Prayer ¶ C, and for fees for any counsel who may later appear.
