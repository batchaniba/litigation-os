 1   MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

 2   The surviving claim challenges the substance of the certification decision

 3   Petitioner seeks review of a specific administrative decision, not a second adjudication of the

 4   dismissed reason-giving claim. CHP denied his request because it concluded that the underlying

 5   incident involved no qualifying criminal activity. The surviving question is whether that

 6   conclusion was a lawful, reasonable exercise of the certification authority on the facts of this

 7   incident. (Pet., Ex. 1, denial letter; Order Sustaining Demurrer in Part, Aug. 29, 2026, pp. 2–4.)

 8   The Court has already identified the factual issue that matters: whether evidence credited at trial
 9   would establish that the officers' asserted signs of intoxication were untrue, that probable cause

10   was lacking, and that the certification denial consequently constituted an abuse of discretion. The

11   Court did not decide those facts on demurrer. Petitioner now requests their determination on

12   admissible evidence, not their acceptance merely because they were pleaded. (Aug. 29 Order, pp.

13   3–4.)

14   The strongest directly testable discrepancy concerns the walk-and-turn. CHP's report attributes

15   its nonperformance to Petitioner's choice and difficulty; Petitioner's timestamped transcript

16   instead records his willingness to attempt it and the officers' decision not to administer it on

17   uneven freeway terrain. The report then invokes poor field-test performance as an arrest factor.

18   Petitioner asks the Court to compare the original recording, the report, and his testimony, and to

19   assess the materiality of that discrepancy together with every remaining asserted basis for

20   custody. (Pet., Ex. 5, transcript at 01:10:14–01:10:30; CHP Form 202, AGO 000031–000032;

21   Newanforbi Decl., ¶¶ 3–5.)

22   Petitioner does not contend that one inaccurate description automatically proves every element of

23   a qualifying offense. Nor does he seek an automatic remand because the denial was brief. Relief

24   depends on a finding of material error in the qualifying-activity determination; direct

25   certification additionally requires a finding that no lawful alternative remains.

 1   The certification statute supplies an enforceable but conditional duty

 2   CHP is a certifying entity, and section 679.10 expressly lists unlawful criminal restraint and false

 3   imprisonment as qualifying criminal activity. Subdivision (d) also covers offenses with

 4   substantially similar nature and elements. (Pen. Code, § 679.10, subds. (a), (c)(18)–(19), (d).) 1

 5   Upon request, an authorized official “shall certify victim helpfulness” when the person was a

 6   victim of qualifying criminal activity and “has been helpful, is being helpful, or is likely to be

 7   helpful” in its detection, investigation or prosecution. The helpfulness presumption is rebuttable

 8   and does not dispense with the qualifying-activity requirement. (Id., subds. (g)(1), (h).) 2

 9   An open investigation, filed charges, apprehension, prosecution and conviction are not
10   prerequisites to certification. The statute also prohibits denial for specified reasons, including the

11   victim's criminal history and cooperation or refusal to cooperate in a separate case. Petitioner

12   does not assume that Serrato relied on any prohibited factor merely because the statute prohibits

13   it. (Id., subd. (k)(1), (3)(A), (H).) 3

14   Traditional mandate can compel an official to exercise authority under the correct law and can

15   correct an administrative abuse of discretion; ordinarily it cannot dictate a particular

16   discretionary outcome. (Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442;

17   Saleeby v. State Bar (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547, 560–562.) 4 5

18   Where a statute leaves discretion, Petitioner must show action that was arbitrary, beyond the

19   bounds of reason, or contrary to governing legal standards. A specific outcome may be
