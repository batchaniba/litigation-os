## I. INTRODUCTION

**1.** This is a civil-rights action arising from a private, for-profit corporation's exploitation of
its government-conferred monopoly over mandatory sex-offender treatment to deprive a California parolee
of his constitutional rights. Defendant SHANNON SMITH, LCSW, as Chief Executive Officer and Founder of
HOPE Psychotherapy, Inc. ("HOPE"), personally approved, implemented, and enforced a blanket retention
policy under which no parolee could ever be determined to have completed treatment before discharge
from parole — regardless of clinical progress, compliance, risk reduction, or homework completion.
HOPE, acting under color of state law as CDCR's contracted and sole certified treatment provider in the
Stockton region, maintained and enforced this policy statewide.

**2.** Smith directed this policy from HOPE's Hayward headquarters. On information and belief, HOPE's
blanket retention practice was judicially condemned in *In re Dwyer*, No. SC-080355A (San Mateo Cnty.
Super. Ct. Mar. 20, 2023) (Hill, J.), where the court found HOPE applied its treatment phases without
regard to individual risk and that the practice "vitiates the Legislature's directive" under Penal Code
§ 3008(d)(2). Smith took no corrective action after *Dwyer*.

**3.** HOPE's own Clinical Operational Manual (Sept. 2024 ed.) confirms the scheme in writing: page 294
states HOPE is "prohibited from discharging a client from treatment for any reason," while page 295
describes a six-step "graduation" process culminating in a Treatment Completion Certificate that
clinicians are "not permitted to provide … directly to clients." HOPE thus manufactured the illusion of
an achievable completion pathway while making completion structurally impossible during mandatory
participation.

**4.** In verified discovery in the related state action, HOPE admitted that at *every* Containment Team
Meeting ("CTM") concerning Plaintiff it recommended continued treatment and never recommended a
completion determination (HOPE RFA No. 4); its Site Director admitted the same (Palacios RFA No. 6); and
for every CTM from August 2022 through August 2025 HOPE gave the identical rote reason — "had not
completed Block I" — regardless of Plaintiff's attendance or homework (HOPE SROG No. 11). That is the
documentary fingerprint of a blanket policy, not individualized clinical judgment.

**5.** As a direct result, Plaintiff has been trapped in mandatory treatment since March 2021, subjected
to false status reports to CDCR, denied parole discharge, kept under GPS monitoring and curfew, and
deprived of over $109,000 in lost wages as a Class A CDL commercial driver. When Plaintiff complained,
HOPE's Site Director retaliated by labeling him a "ringleader" and "abhorrent" before his parole agents
and, through HOPE's false attendance and status reporting, prolonged his supervision.

**6.** Plaintiff sues under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (First and Fourteenth Amendments), 18 U.S.C. § 1962 (RICO),
California Civil Code § 52.1 (Bane Act), and California common law (fraud and negligent
misrepresentation), and seeks compensatory, treble, and punitive damages, declaratory and injunctive
relief, and attorney's fees and costs.

## IV. FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

### A. CDCR's Sex Offender Management Program and the Completion Determination

**14.** Under California Penal Code § 3008(d)(2), a parolee "shall successfully complete a sex offender
management program … as a condition of parole," for a period "not less than one year, up to the entire
period of parole, **as determined by the certified sex offender management professional** in
consultation with the parole officer and as approved by the court." The statute delegates *to HOPE's
certified professional* the determination of how long Plaintiff remains in mandatory treatment as a
condition of his liberty, and presupposes a meaningful possibility of completion before discharge.

**15.** Title 15, California Code of Regulations, § 3574 (eff. Apr. 17, 2025) codifies SOMP standards.
Section 3574(f)(1) provides that treatment completion and parole discharge are legally separate
decisions and that retention requires *written good cause*, signed by the treatment provider and the
parole agent on CDCR Form 3043. Section 3574(e)(2) makes the one-year CTM "the first review to determine
whether the supervised person's … requirements have been satisfied." Subdivision (d) confirms supervised
persons "may complete" the program "before discharge from parole."

**16.** CASOMB's Maintenance and Aftercare Guidelines (Sept. 2024) state that "[d]ischarge planning
starts at intake," and that "[t]he final determination regarding whether an individual has 'completed'
treatment should never come as a surprise to the client."

**17.** During the relevant period, HOPE was the **sole CASOMB-certified SOMP provider in the Stockton
region**, holding an exclusive monopoly over the mandatory treatment on which Plaintiff's parole
discharge depended.

### B. HOPE and Smith Act Under Color of State Law

**18.** *Delegated public function.* HOPE performs a function California is statutorily obligated to
administer to persons under its supervision: mandatory sex-offender treatment under Penal Code § 3008,
with the completion determination delegated by statute to HOPE's certified professional. A private party
that contracts with the State to provide a service to persons in state custody or under state
supervision acts under color of state law. *West v. Atkins*, 487 U.S. 42, 54–57 (1988).

**19.** *Joint action and close nexus.* HOPE does not merely hold a contract; it is embedded in the
parole-supervision machinery. HOPE prepares and submits treatment-status determinations to CDCR on the
CDCR Form 3043 Containment-Team pipeline and contributes to the CDCR Discharge Review Report (Form
1502-DR); CDCR's Division of Adult Parole Operations institutionally defers to HOPE's determinations;
and Plaintiff's parole agents have each separately told him, in substance, that "HOPE controls" and
that "without sex-offender completion there will be no discharge from parole." HOPE's determinations are
thus jointly transformed into state action governing Plaintiff's liberty.

**20.** The joint character of the conduct is confirmed by the record. On or about March 31, 2026,
Parole Agent Long Moua emailed HOPE (copying Supervisor Gary Noguchi) acknowledging that Plaintiff had
suffered "a technological error" preventing him from joining a group session, yet instructing HOPE to
"mark him as a no show." A parole agent directing a treatment provider to enter a false attendance
record, and the provider doing so, is willful joint participation between HOPE and CDCR agents in the
creation of the very records used to prolong Plaintiff's supervision.

**21.** HOPE and Smith exercised "power possessed by virtue of state law and made possible only because
the wrongdoer is clothed with the authority of state law": the statutory completion determination, the
Form 3043 reporting channel, and the exclusive certification without which Plaintiff could not obtain
treatment or discharge.

**22.** These allegations satisfy the public-function, joint-action, and close-nexus tests for state
action. The challenged conduct is not a discretionary private decision the State did not command; it is
HOPE's exercise of a state-delegated power over Plaintiff's liberty, jointly effectuated with CDCR.

### C. HOPE's Blanket Retention Policy

**23.** HOPE maintains a blanket retention policy under which no parolee is determined to have completed
treatment before discharge from parole. HOPE's four-phase structure (preparatory, core, maintenance,
aftercare) treats the aftercare phase as mandatory for parolees and voluntary only post-discharge,
making completion structurally impossible during mandatory participation.

**24.** HOPE's Clinical Operational Manual (Sept. 2024 ed.) confirms the policy in its own words. Page
294 states: "Due to specific guidelines within our contracts, HOPE is prohibited from discharging a
client from treatment for any reason." Page 295 describes a six-step graduation process — (1) completion
of the Maintenance Program; (2) adherence to all titration criteria; (3) a non-deceptive maintenance
polygraph; (4) clinician presentation in group supervision; (5) Clinical Director approval of the
CASOMB titration form; and (6) a Treatment Completion Certificate — and then provides: "Clinicians are
not permitted to provide this certificate directly to clients."

**25.** The juxtaposition reveals the architecture: HOPE published a detailed graduation pathway to
maintain the appearance that completion is achievable while maintaining a blanket prohibition on
discharge ensuring no client reaches the end of that pathway during mandatory participation.

**26.** On information and belief, HOPE's blanket retention practice was judicially condemned in *In re
Dwyer*, No. SC-080355A (San Mateo Cnty. Super. Ct. Mar. 20, 2023) (Hill, J.), which found HOPE applied
its treatment phases without regard to individual risk and that the practice "vitiates the Legislature's
directive" under Penal Code § 3008(d)(2), and terminated the petitioner's treatment.

**27.** HOPE did not modify the policy after *Dwyer*. The Sept. 2024 Manual, issued eighteen months
later, continued to prohibit discharge "for any reason," and HOPE admitted in verified discovery that it
made no material change to its completion policies after the April 17, 2025 effective date of 15 C.C.R.
§ 3574(f)(1) — the regulation enacted to stop that very practice.
