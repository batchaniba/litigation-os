# One CHP Stop, Two Federal Civil Rights Cases

At 12:55 on the morning of November 1, 2024, a California Highway Patrol cruiser pulled onto the dirt shoulder of eastbound Interstate 580 in Livermore and stopped behind a gray Tesla Model Y. Inside, a commercial truck driver was asleep; he says he had stopped to rest. "It's a rental. Huh," one of the two officers said, before either had stepped out. Approximately sixteen minutes after the cruiser stopped, the driver, Brendan Newanforbi, was handcuffed. Within four months, a one-year suspension of his Class A commercial driver's license began. He alleges that the suspension disrupted his livelihood as a professional driver. The recording documents approximately sixty-two minutes of the encounter and subsequent transport.

One roadside encounter became the subject of two federal civil-rights lawsuits. The first challenges the officers' entry, search, arrest, and subsequent reporting. The second challenges the fairness of the DMV hearing that sustained the resulting commercial-license suspension.

Brendan Ngehsi Newanforbi, proceeding without a lawyer, has carried those questions through state court, the Eastern District of California, and the Northern District of California. Court captions use both *Newanforbi* and *Nforbi*. This article uses the party name as it appears on each caption. Both cases are now pending in the Northern District of California; their status is at the end of this article.

What follows is a record of the filed papers and the public orders. It is not a finding that anyone violated the Constitution, and it does not forecast either case.

## The November 1, 2024 CHP Stop: Plaintiff's Dashcam Transcript

The account below follows a timestamped transcript of the CHP recording in Case No. RJ68845. Exhibit A is a transcript prepared by the plaintiff, rather than an official or jointly stipulated transcript. Its speaker identifications and bracketed descriptions reflect the plaintiff's interpretation of the recording; the original video has not been independently verified for this article. Times are the recording's clock. The submitted transcript identifies a recording window of 00:54:36 to 01:57:10, totaling approximately sixty-two minutes.

**The knock.** At 00:55:37 the cruiser stopped behind the Tesla. Officers Santiago Meza-Gonzalez and Jose Melano walked up the passenger side on the dirt shoulder, flashlights in hand, and shone them through the windows. "It wasn't here earlier when we passed by," Melano said. Then: "I think it's booze." Meza-Gonzalez knocked on the front passenger window and kept knocking. "Oh look at this," Melano said. At 00:56:40 he said, "Open the window." At 00:56:43, a bracketed description supplied by the plaintiff in Exhibit A identifies Officer Melano as opening the passenger door.

"Hello," Melano said. "Uh-huh," Newanforbi answered. "Are you okay?" "Yeah, we good." Asked where he was coming from, Newanforbi said, "We're coming from uh, uh, a party." Meza-Gonzalez noted that the Tesla's dash clock read 12:57 a.m. and told him the shoulder "is not a parking lot." Newanforbi said he had been stopped there "like 5-7 minutes." Asked how much he had had to drink, he answered, "Zero."

**The cup.** "What's in that cup right there?" Meza-Gonzalez asked. "Don't drink it. Yeah don't drink it," Newanforbi said. Melano took the cup. "Is it water? No this is piss. This is pee." Newanforbi: "I don't know what that is." Melano: "I'm telling you it's piss. It smells like piss." When Melano held the cup out, Meza-Gonzalez leaned back: "No I'm not gonna smell that." "OK. Leave it there. Put it back," Melano said. Five seconds later, Meza-Gonzalez asked Newanforbi to step out of the car.

**The can.** Newanforbi walked to the patrol car, gave his name, and spelled it: "NEW-WAN-FOR-BI." According to a bracketed description in Exhibit A, Officer Melano opened a rear passenger door while Meza-Gonzalez escorted Newanforbi toward the patrol car. Melano subsequently appeared with an empty can. "Did you drink that?" Meza-Gonzalez asked. "No." "You haven't drank anything since you've been parked?" "No."

**Where he was.** "You know where you're at right now?" "Right now, Livermore." Newanforbi read the exit sign about fifty yards away, "Isabella Avenue, Portola Avenue," and Meza-Gonzalez said, "Correct." He explained why he had stopped: "You know I'm a truck driver. So when I feel tired, when I feel fatigued, whenever I am unable to continue to drive that truck, I'm gonna pull over to the side of the road, try to get a nap. … How quickly can I move over to the side of the road, catch some shut-eye, wake up and continue on to finish the route?" During later questioning about his presence in the vehicle, Newanforbi stated, "I was in the driver's seat, yes sir." That statement, as transcribed, does not by itself establish when or under what circumstances the vehicle had been driven.

**The tests.** Exhibit A attributes the recorded instructions for the eye and time-estimation tests to Officer Meza-Gonzalez. The transcript's speaker identifications have not been independently confirmed against the native recording. When Meza-Gonzalez described a one-leg stand, Newanforbi pushed back: "It seems as though I'm passing them and then the level of difficulty just keeps increasing and increasing and increasing. I just wanna know if I've passed your test." "Yeah there's not pass or fail," Melano said. Meza-Gonzalez: "I go through all of them regardless if it's a pass or what you think is a pass or fail." Newanforbi said he did not know if he could balance on one knee. Meza-Gonzalez told him, "It's fine if you don't. You don't have to." Newanforbi agreed to try a walking test: "I mean I think we can do that." Melano cut in: "It's kind of sketch here bro. … it's on the side of the freeway and it's like uneven ground. I wouldn't do that one." "Alright then," said Meza-Gonzalez.

**"I don't consent to that."** At 01:10:33 Meza-Gonzalez asked him to take a preliminary alcohol screening test. "You may refuse to take this test," he said. "So you're saying that I am required or not required?" "You're not required to do this." "And if I choose not to consent to it?" "If you choose to not consent to it then it'll be up to my discretion whether I put you under arrest for DUI or not." The officers readied the device. "Yes or no?" At 01:11:15 Newanforbi said, "I don't consent to that." At 01:11:16, according to Exhibit A, Meza-Gonzalez said, "OK. Brendan. Alright then. I'm gonna have you just turn around please. Place your hands behind your back." By 01:11:33, the transcript records, he was putting on the handcuffs.

**After the handcuffs.** According to Exhibit A, Meza-Gonzalez searched him, put him in the back seat of the cruiser, and attached the handcuffs to the vehicle frame. He then asked whether Newanforbi would take a breath test or a blood test at the jail. "I don't know. It seems like torture." "Right now you're not refusing," Meza-Gonzalez told him, "you're just saying that basically you don't want to choose." He read the chemical-test admonition and explained that a refusal would bring an administrative suspension of at least a year, even if Newanforbi was sober. "Even as a truck commercial driver, that's what you're willing to do, force the DMV's hand?" Newanforbi asked. "I'm not forcing the DMV to do it, that's their rule." At 01:22:15 Meza-Gonzalez asked, "So you're refusing to take a test?" Exhibit A transcribes no audible response between that question and the officer's subsequent "OK." The officers compared notes. Melano: "We just have to write what time we last passed him here because he wasn't here earlier." Meza-Gonzalez: "But he said he was here for seven minutes." Then Melano: "Yeah you want to start your warrant?"

At 01:36:47 Melano returned to read the implied-consent advisement. Newanforbi asked if it was Miranda. "No, no, no," Melano said. "Miranda rights we only give when we still wanna ask you questions about the investigation. … We already have everything we need right?" If he did not choose a test, Melano told him, "we're gonna get it anyways. It says it's going to be with a warrant and you're gonna automatically lose your license." "OK so we're getting a warrant."

**The last conversation.** Before the cruiser pulled away, the officers wondered aloud whether the Tesla had an alternator. Newanforbi explained: "I don't think electric vehicles have alternators. … Just like your phone gets charged." At 01:52:05 the cruiser left the shoulder. The recording ends at 01:57:10 with its arrival at the Dublin CHP transfer.

## What the complaints allege

The operative First Amended Complaint in the roadside action (N.D. Cal. Dkt. 5, filed August 17, 2026) and the operative First Amended Complaint in the hearing-officer action (N.D. Cal. Dkt. 6, filed the same day) describe the same starting point.

The operative complaints identify CHP Officers Santiago Meza-Gonzalez and Jose Melano as the officers involved in the November 1, 2024 roadside encounter near Livermore. The plaintiff alleges that they initiated a DUI investigation after approaching his stationary vehicle (CHP Case No. RJ68845 / CAD No. 241101GG0064).

The complaints allege a warrantless opening of a passenger door, a search of the interior, and an arrest under Vehicle Code § 23152(a). The complaints allege that officers identified the contents of a cup as urine and that, according to the plaintiff's transcript, neither officer verbally reported smelling alcohol on the plaintiff's breath or person. The transcript also records an empty can. They further allege that a blood-draw warrant was later obtained and blood was drawn, and that a later administrative form designated a chemical-test refusal.

As quoted and characterized in the Candelaria complaint, the DMV's February 11, 2025 decision summarized the officer's observations and accepted the grounds for suspension. The complaint recites that the decision described Meza-Gonzalez as finding Newanforbi "asleep" on the shoulder, knocking on the passenger window, and making contact "through the open right passenger side door"; that it recorded observations of "Slurred Speech, Red and/or Watery Eyes, and Odor of Alcohol" and an odor "emitting from Respondent's breath and person while speaking to him in front of the patrol vehicle"; and that it recorded an arrest at 1:12 a.m. and what the decision called an "admission to driving." Those quotations have not been independently re-checked against the decision PDF for this article.

The Meza-Gonzalez complaint alleges that after the arrest, Meza-Gonzalez signed a DS-367 Officer's Statement and a CHP Arrest-Investigation Report under penalty of perjury. It alleges those papers were used to obtain the blood-draw warrant, to begin the license suspension, and to refer charges to the District Attorney. It alleges that four entries are unsupported by the recording: a "strong distinct odor of an alcoholic beverage," which, according to the plaintiff's transcript, neither officer says aloud; speech described as "slurred"; a statement that he "seemed very confused and disoriented"; and a checked box reading "Beverage Present: Yes," which the complaint disputes. The complaint acknowledges that odor, speech, and orientation are "experiential observations" an officer need not voice. It alleges that the recording, which captures coherent speech, correct orientation, and a cup identified as urine, is inconsistent with those entries. The complaint alleges that a magistrate issued the warrant and that blood was drawn at the CHP Dublin Area office. These are the plaintiff's legal allegations, not findings of misconduct. A motion to dismiss (Dkt. 22) is pending.

The complaints allege that Dublin CHP issued a Detention Certificate dated November 5, 2024, under Penal Code § 849(b)(1) (Dublin CHP-01991; CHP File No. 202400898), identifying the custody disposition as a detention rather than an arrest. The certificate's precise date and contents have not been independently verified for this article. The complaints allege that the Alameda County District Attorney declined to file charges.

## Two theories: Fourth Amendment and DMV due process

Although both lawsuits arise from the November 1 encounter, they challenge different government actions and involve different defendants.

### The roadside case: *Nforbi v. Meza-Gonzalez, et al*

The Northern District First Amended Complaint names Santiago Meza-Gonzalez and Jose Melano, each individually, plus Doe defendants. It does not name the State of California or the Highway Patrol. The operative complaint asserts six claims under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, including alleged unlawful entry, search, and arrest; a judicial-deception claim involving the warrant application; an evidence-fabrication claim; and a First Amendment retaliation claim tied to a preliminary-alcohol-screening refusal. These are the plaintiff's legal allegations, not findings of misconduct.

The earlier Alameda caption, *Newanforbi v. Meza-Gonzalez et al.*, No. 26CV168913, had pleaded false imprisonment, battery, and a Bane Act claim under Civil Code § 52.1. That entire action was dismissed without prejudice on August 15, 2026. Those state-law theories, and the State/CHP defendant, are not in the live Northern District complaint.

A still-earlier federal caption, *Newanforbi v. CHP et al.*, No. 2:25-cv-01460-DC-CSK (E.D. Cal., filed May 27, 2025), had pleaded Fourth Amendment, due-process, and *Monell* theories against the State, the CHP, and the two officers. It was also dismissed without prejudice on August 15, 2026.

### The hearing-officer case: *Nforbi v. Candelaria et al.*

The Candelaria complaints describe a second proceeding. They allege that a DS-367 form and an arrest report triggered DMV Administrative Per Se Case No. 34739340; that Hearing Officer Diana Candelaria held a Microsoft Teams hearing on January 24, 2025; and that on February 11, 2025, she issued a Notification of Findings and Decision sustaining a one-year Class A commercial-license suspension from February 20, 2025, through February 19, 2026, plus a reissue fee and an SR-22 financial-responsibility requirement.

Before any hearing, the complaint alleges, DMV issued a temporary license valid "through the outcome of hearing" that barred him from "driving commercially". On December 9, 2024, he filed a five-page written response identifying the dashcam evidence and the Detention Certificate. He also offered the certificate and a transcript of the recording into the administrative record. The DMV's own notice described the hearing officer's job in one sentence: "The Hearing Officer will develop the facts and render a final decision on your matter."

The hearing was held over Microsoft Teams at 9:00 a.m. on January 24, 2025. Newanforbi testified under penalty of perjury. The complaint alleges that Candelaria introduced the Department's exhibits, ruled on evidentiary objections, and decided the matter. The plaintiff characterizes that combination of functions as a departure from impartial adjudication — in the complaint's words, a crossing "from neutral fact development into advocacy." No court has yet determined whether her conduct violated due process. As quoted in the operative Candelaria complaint, the DMV decision rejected the plaintiff's challenges to the officer's reported observations and relied in part on the statutory presumption of regular official conduct. The decision, as quoted there, states: "It is presumed official duty has been regularly performed. No evidence was presented to the contrary." The complaint alleges that the written decision failed to address the Detention Certificate and the particular recording discrepancies identified by the plaintiff. The suspension ran from February 20, 2025, through February 19, 2026. Defendants have answered the amended complaint (Dkt. 26).

The live First Amended Complaint names Diana Candelaria individually and Steve Gordon, Director of the California DMV, in his official capacity. The four live counts are: denial of an impartial adjudicator; denial of a fair hearing; declaratory relief under 28 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2202 seeking vacatur of APS Decision 34739340; and injunctive / *Ex parte Young* relief against Director Gordon.

The earlier Eastern District caption, *Newanforbi v. Candelaria*, No. 2:26-cv-00983-DC-CSK, pleaded a similar due-process theory, including a claim that Vehicle Code §§ 13353 and 13353.2 require a “lawful arrest” predicate and that the November 5 Detention Certificate removed that predicate before the January hearing. Magistrate Judge Chi Soo Kim issued findings and recommendations on June 15, 2026. The district judge never ruled on them. Plaintiff filed a notice of voluntary dismissal without prejudice on August 15, 2026.

A parallel state petition, *Newanforbi v. Candelaria, et al.*, Sacramento Superior Court No. 26WM000135, sought a writ of administrative mandate under Code of Civil Procedure § 1094.5 and Vehicle Code § 13559. It was filed May 18, 2026. The petition did not proceed; the APS challenge continues in the federal case.

Published California decisions that the pleadings invoke — including *California DUI Lawyers Ass'n v. DMV*, 77 Cal. App. 5th 517 (2022), and *Knudsen v. DMV*, 101 Cal. App. 5th 186 (2024) — address when a DMV hearing officer's dual role requires a new hearing. Whether they control this record is for the assigned judge.

## Filing Federal Civil Rights Lawsuits Without an Attorney

The procedure is the part of these files that helps another person who may someday file alone. It can be described without arguing who should win.

## Lessons for self-represented litigants in federal court

These points do not depend on who is right about November 1.

One incident can generate more than one civil case because the legal questions differ. A Fourth Amendment claim against arresting officers is not the same lawsuit as a due-process claim against a later hearing officer and an agency director. Readers tend to merge them into one story. Splitting them into two captions is a choice, with two case numbers, two service campaigns, and two assignment tracks.

Forum and caption are not trivia. The same person appears as *Newanforbi* in the Eastern District and Alameda papers and as *Nforbi* in the Northern District refiles. The clerk's office treated those as different docket names. A later stipulation corrected one docket.

Amending quickly is ordinary under Rule 15 and still has consequences. A defendant who answers the original complaint may be answering the wrong paper. That is what the Candelaria strike order addressed. Identify the operative pleading on the docket, and say so if the numbering is confusing.

Service on individual officers is slower than service on an agency mail room. One of the two CHP officers required substitute service, a diligence declaration, and a later mailing. Service is complete only when the rules say it is, and the response clock runs from that date.

A magistrate judge's findings and recommendations are not a judgment. The Eastern District Candelaria findings and recommendations never became an order of the district judge. Related-case motions can also split a cluster: the court related one Northern District action to another and refused to relate the third. That is Civil Local Rule 3-12 case management, not a comment on the facts.

Neither federal court has yet resolved the merits of the challenged roadside conduct or DMV proceeding. The cases illustrate how a single encounter can generate distinct constitutional claims, with separate defendants, procedural histories, and evidentiary questions.

## FAQ

**Are these two cases the same lawsuit?**

No. They share a November 1, 2024 CHP contact but name different defendants and theories. *Nforbi v. Meza-Gonzalez* is a § 1983 action against two officers. *Nforbi v. Candelaria* is a § 1983 action against a DMV hearing officer and the DMV director.

**Has a court decided who was right about the stop?**

No. The roadside case is at a motion to dismiss. The hearing-officer case has an answer to the amended complaint. Earlier cases were dismissed without prejudice or were not pursued.

**Why do some captions say Newanforbi and others say Nforbi?**

The filed complaints used different name forms. Eastern District, Alameda, and Sacramento papers use Newanforbi. The August 2026 Northern District complaints use Nforbi. This article follows each caption.

**What is an APS hearing?**

Administrative Per Se is the DMV license-suspension process after certain DUI-related arrests or alleged test refusals. It is not a criminal prosecution. The Candelaria complaints challenge APS Decision 34739340.

**Does a DMV license suspension mean a driver was convicted of DUI?**

No. California's Administrative Per Se process is separate from a criminal DUI prosecution. A license suspension can be imposed through an administrative proceeding without a criminal conviction. The Candelaria lawsuit challenges the fairness of the administrative process involved in this particular suspension.

**Are the Meza-Gonzalez and Candelaria lawsuits being heard together?**

The lawsuits proceed under separate case numbers before different federal judges. The related-case orders discussed here concern their relationship to a third action, not a consolidation of the two featured lawsuits.

**Can a California DMV hearing officer act as both advocate and judge?**

California appellate decisions the pleadings invoke, including *California DUI Lawyers Ass'n v. DMV* and *Knudsen v. DMV*, address when a hearing officer's dual role of developing the record and deciding the case requires a new hearing. Whether those decisions control this record is for the assigned judge. The Candelaria complaint alleges that the hearing officer's conduct in this case crossed from neutral fact development into advocacy; no court has yet decided that claim.
