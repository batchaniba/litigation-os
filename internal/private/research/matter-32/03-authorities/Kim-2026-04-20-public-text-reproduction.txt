11 II.
LEGAL STANDARDS
12 A claim may be dismissed because of the plaintiff’s “failure to state a claim upon
13 which relief can be granted.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6). A complaint fails to state a claim if
14 it either lacks a cognizable legal theory or sufficient facts to support a cognizable legal
15 theory. Mollett v. Netflix, Inc.,
```
795 F.3d 1062, 1065
```
 (9th Cir. 2015). To state a claim on
16 which relief may be granted, the plaintiff must allege enough facts “to state a claim to
17 relief that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly,
```
550 U.S. 544
```
, 570
18 (2007). “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows
19 the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
20 alleged.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal,
```
556 U.S. 678
```
 (2009). When considering whether a claim has
21 been stated, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and
22 construe the complaint in the light most favorable to the non-moving party.
```
Id.
```
 However,
23 the court is not required to accept as true conclusory factual allegations contradicted by
24 documents referenced in the complaint, or legal conclusions merely because they are
25 cast in the form of factual allegations. Paulsen,
```
559 F.3d at 1071
```
.

20 III. DISCUSSION
21 Where Plaintiff consented to dismiss Defendant State of California, his Monell
22 claims against Defendant CHP, and his official capacity claims against Defendants
23 Meza-Gonzalez and Melano (Pl. Opp. at 15-17), the only claims remaining are:
24 (1) unlawful search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment and (2) violation of due
25 process rights under the Fourteenth Amendment against Defendants Meza-Gonzalez
26 and Melano in their individual capacities.
27
```
42 U.S.C. § 1983
```
 “provides a cause of action for the deprivation of any rights,
28 privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws of the United States.”
1 Wilder v. Virginia Hosp. Ass'n,
```
496 U.S. 498, 508
```
 (1990) (internal quotation marks
2 omitted). “Section 1983 is not itself a source of substantive rights but merely provides a
3 method for vindicating federal rights elsewhere conferred.” Albright v. Oliver,
```
510 U.S. 4 266, 271
```
 (1994) (citation and internal quotation marks omitted). To state a cognizable
5 § 1983 claim, a plaintiff must allege the violation of a right protected by the Constitution
6 and laws of the United States, and that the alleged deprivation was committed by a
7 person who acted under color of state law.
```
42 U.S.C. § 1983
```
. See also Florer v.
8 Congregation Pidyon Shevuyim, N.A.,
```
639 F.3d 916, 921
```
 (9th Cir. 2011). An individual
9 defendant is not liable on a civil rights claim unless the facts establish the defendant's
10 personal involvement in the constitutional deprivation or a causal connection between
11 the defendant's wrongful conduct and the alleged constitutional deprivation. See Hansen
12 v. Black,
```
885 F.2d 642, 645
```
 (9th Cir. 1989); Johnson v. Duffy,
```
588 F.2d 740, 743-44
```
 (9th
13 Cir. 1978).
14 As to any remaining non-Monell claims against Defendant CHP, a § 1983 claim
15 cannot be brought against a state agency because a state is not a “person” for purposes
16 of § 1983 due to a state’s sovereign immunity under the Eleventh Amendment. See
17 Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona,
```
520 U.S. 43, 69
```
 (1997); Cortez v. Cnty. of Los
18 Angeles,
```
294 F.3d 1186, 1188
```
 (9th Cir. 2002); see, e.g., McCain v. California Highway
19 Patrol,
```
2011 WL 3439225
```
, **2-4 (E.D. Cal. Aug. 4, 2011) (§ 1983 claims against CHP
20 barred by Eleventh Amendment). Thus, Plaintiff’s claims as to CHP should be dismissed.
21 As to Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano, Plaintiff claims unconstitutional
22 search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment based on their alleged lack of probable
23 cause to search his vehicle and arrest him. “The Fourth Amendment protects ‘[t]he right
24 of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against
25 unreasonable searches and seizures.’”
Devenpeck v. Alford,
```
543 U.S. 146, 152
```
 (2004)
26 (citing U.S. Const. amend. IV). A warrantless search or arrest by a law enforcement
27 officer “is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment where there is probable cause to
28 believe that a criminal offense has been or is being committed.”
```
Id.
```
 Probable cause
1 exists when “the facts and circumstances within the officer’s knowledge are sufficient
2 [for] a prudent person to believe that the suspect has committed” a crime. Crowe v.
3 County of San Diego,
```
608 F.3d 406, 432
```
(9th Cir. 2010) (internal citation omitted).
4 California Vehicle Code 23152(a) makes it unlawful for a person “under the
5 influence” of alcohol to drive a vehicle. Section 23223 of the Vehicle Code makes it
6 unlawful for a driver to possess an open container containing an alcoholic beverage.
7 The Complaint alleges that, at around 1 a.m., Defendants Meza-Gonzalez and Melano
8 found Plaintiff resting in the driver’s seat of his car, which was parked on the shoulder of
9 the I-580 freeway. One Defendant observed a cup in the center console and said to the
10 other, “I think it’s booze.” Defendants told Plaintiff to exit the vehicle and proceeded to
11 search it. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants lacked probable cause for the search.
12 However, the totality of the circumstances reasonably suggested that Plaintiff might have
13 been driving under the influence prior to parking on the freeway shoulder.
Plaintiff
14 alleges that “no evidence of intoxication or illegal activity was found,” Compl., ¶ 16, but
15 this is contradicted by Defendant Meza-Gonzalez’s investigative report, a copy of which
16 is attached to the Complaint. The report notes that, when the officers approached the
17 car, they “could smell the distinct odor of an alcoholic beverage emitting from within the
18 vehicle.” Compl. at 13. The report also notes that Plaintiff’s eyes were “[r]ed and watery”
19 and his speech was “[s]lurred.” Id. at 11. Plaintiff does not allege a Fourth Amendment
20 violation on these facts, and his claim of illegal search should be dismissed.
21 Similarly, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants arrested him without probable cause.
22 Compl., ¶ 17. However, in addition to the circumstances described above, this allegation
23 is contradicted by the investigative report, which notes that Plaintiff “displayed objective
24 signs and symptoms of intoxication” and performed poorly on field sobriety tests
25 conducted at the scene. Id. at 13. These circumstances reasonably suggested that
26 Plaintiff had violated one or more sections of the Vehicle Code. As Plaintiff does not
27 allege an unconstitutional arrest, his Fourth Amendment claims are subject to dismissal.
28 Nor does Plaintiff allege specific facts showing that either defendant violated his
1 constitutional due process rights, either in arresting him or in events subsequent to his
2 arrest. Plaintiff’s Fourteenth Amendment claims are also subject to dismissal.
3 Finally, even if Defendants lacked probable cause to search Plaintiff’s car or to
4 arrest him, their actions are entitled to qualified immunity on the facts alleged. See
5 Rosenbaum v. Washoe County,
```
663 F.3d 1071, 1076
```
(9th Cir. 2011) (officer who makes
6 an arrest without probable cause may be entitled to qualified immunity if he reasonably
7 believed there to have been probable cause). Thus, all of Plaintiff’s remaining claims
8 should be dismissed.
9 Because the Court recommends granting the motion to dismiss and dismissal of
10 the Complaint, the Court declines to reach Defendants’ requests to strike Plaintiff’s
11 prayer for attorney’s fees and punitive damages.
12 IV. LEAVE TO AMEND
13 In considering whether leave to amend should be granted, the Court considers
14 that the Complaint does not present a non-frivolous claim, and it appears granting leave
15 to amend would be futile. See Lathus,
```
56 F.4th at 1243
```
. The Complaint should therefore
16 be dismissed without leave to amend.
