ABOUT THE HOPE MAINTENANCE TREATMENT PROGRAM

The HOPE maintenance treatment program is designed for individuals who have successfully
completed the Preparatory and Core Curriculum. The maintenance curriculum is designed to
help maintain treatment gains and to prepare the individual for life after probation or parole. As
such, the following modules incorporate review of some of the more pertinent material from the
Preparatory and Core Curriculum, as well as some new modules and exercises that build on
previous material and prepare the client for the future.

Goal Setting and the Good Lives Model

Facilitators should discuss the importance of goals with the group and link it back to the earlier
goal setting exercise initially presented during the preparatory program and discussed
throughout the core curriculum. Ask the group which goals they felt they achieved and are most
proud of during their time at HOPE. Positive reinforcement is key. The purpose here is to orient
the client toward the importance of acknowledging the goals that have been achieved as well as
to establish a set of personal goals that will improve his or her life moving forward.

It will be important to remind clients, via discussion, about some key elements of goal setting
and goal attainment. These include differences between short-term and long-term goals,
avoidance versus approach motivated goals, and the SMART goal framework. Remember,
clients often frame their goals as avoidance-based (i.e., things they should not do) such as
avoid children and certain places. While this is appropriate and deserves some attention,
considerable discussion needs to be placed on approach goals (i.e., things they will do in the
future). Examples of approach goals are a) establish a healthy relationship and b) establish a
group of prosocial friends.

What is the Good Lives Model (Handout 1)

What is the Good Lives Model?

The Good Lives Mode! recognizes that all human beings have similar goals in life, and that
offending relates to attempts to meet these life goals. However, offending as a means to
achieve life goals results in harm to other people, as well as problems in life. The Good Lives
Mode! helps people understand the relationship between their life goals and offending, and
how to achieve life goals and live a satisfying life without harming others.

Common life goals

Everyone's idea of what makes up a good life is different, however there is a set of life goals
that are common to most people. The importance that people place on each goal helps
define what matters most in their lives. These goals include:

* Health and physical safety © Having meaning in life
* Relationships and friendships * Being part of a group
© Peace of mind © Creativity

* Happiness/pleasure

Being good at what we do — either in work, hobbies, or both
e Learning and having knowledge in areas that interest us
* Independence (i.e., being in control of our lives)

As human beings, we all seek some or all of these goals in our lives. But sometimes, we
encounter problems in our attempts to achieve these goals.

Problems experiencing our good life

Seeking out life goals can be like finding our way through a maze — sometimes we encounter
dead ends and challenges. Instead of trying to navigate our way through the maze it can be
tempting to take short-cuts to get what we want — including using drugs, doing crime, and
being violent. While these short-cuts might give us
some immediate satisfaction — they also give us many
long-term negatives that take us farther away from our goals and
our good life. For example, someone who is having relationship
problems might turn to violence in a desperate attempt (i.e., a
short-cut) to hold onto a relationship. That moment might bring a
very brief sense of satisfaction, however the long-term
consequences — for the offender and the victim — are far from
satisfying.

How can the Good Lives Model help me or someone | care about?

The more short-cuts we take, the more lost in the maze we become. Psychologists, social workers,
probation officers and others can help people to navigate their way through the maze by addressing
some of the problems that led them to take short-cuts. Likewise, friends and family can help by showing
their loved ones other ways to get what is important in their lives that do not cause harm to others and
that do not lead to problems in their lives. The Good Lives Model helps people identify what they want
their life to look like — and how to get there without hurting others.

Understanding Emotional States

A brief review of emotions is helpful and facilitators should ask the group members to define
emotions. Emotions have been defined as a “process, a particular kind of automatic appraisal
influenced by our evolutionary and personal past, in which we sense that something important
to our welfare is occurring and a set of psychological changes and emotional behaviors begins
to deal with the situation” (Ekman, 1992).

Facilitator tip: Although much of this content could be delivered in a psychoeducational fashion,
it is strongly recommended that the information is delivered with the goal of having an in-depth
discussion. Very little (if any) of the content in this manual should be read directly to the client

but rather discussed and related to their personal experiences.

While emotions can vary in duration, they are typically brief, and occur in response to particular
events, situations, or memories. Emotions generally occur along a spectrum from pleasant
(e.g., joy, excitement, and love) to unpleasant (e.g., depression, anger and anxiety), and can
also differ in intensity (e.g., weak, mild, strong, intense).

The Cognitive Component pertains to the statement: “What I think.” Thoughts are often
associated directly with feelings, and are a useful indicator for recognizing emotional states.
Common questions clients should ask themselves and which have been repeated
throughout this program is, “What types of thoughts do you notice when you feel depressed
or angry.” What about when you feel happy?

Thoughts and emotions are mental constructs, but can be differentiated by attaching
thoughts to statements that begin with “I think”, and emotions to those that begin with “I
feel.” It is not uncommon for individuals to use “I feel” statements with thoughts, such as
“| feel that | shouldn't have to discuss my emotions.” In reality what they are saying is, “I
do not think | should have to discuss my emotions because | am feeling anxious.”

Behavioral
The Behavioral Component refers to “What I do.” This pertains to actions or behaviors as a
response to emotional states. People can respond to feelings without thinking because our
bodies often respond impulsively in order to deal with the situation. Barlow and colleagues

(2004) have referred to these as Emotion-Driven Behaviors (EDBs).

At this point, it would be helpful to remind participants about Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
(CBT) and the key components to cognitive restructuring. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
emphasizes the notion that changing maladaptive thinking leads to changes in mood and
behavior. Therapists use CBT techniques to help individuals challenge their thinking patterns
and beliefs and replace "errors in thinking,” such as overgeneralizing and catastrophizing, with
more realistic and adaptive thoughts, thus decreasing emotional distress and self-defeating

behavior. These errors in thinking are known as cognitive distortions.

Coping: Emotion Regulation

At this point in the program, group members should present with a solid understanding of
emotional states and be very skilled at recognizing these states within themselves. Emotional
recognition is a crucial part of healthy living and also contributes to effective emotional self-
regulation.

Generally speaking, emotional regulation defines the ability to exert some degree of control over
our emotional state, which also has an influence over behavior. Most people understand and
discuss emotion regulation in the context of “down-regulation” such as when we attempt to
reduce the intensity of emotional states. It does, however, also incorporate “up-regulation” such
as when there is imminent danger or a challenge and we need to amp up our emotional states

such as excitement or anxiety.

Psychologist James Gross has noted that people can act to control their emotions at different
points in time, such as before the emotion has taken effect (i.e., antecedent-focused emotion
regulation) and/or after they have experienced the emotion (i.e., response-focused emotion
regulation).

There are a few categories or types of emotion regulation. The first is suppression which is a
response-focused strategy that involves inhibiting or suppressing an emotional experience after
it has been activated. In contrast, reappraisal is an antecedent-focused (i.e., before the
emotion has been activated) strategy that involves interpreting one’s situation in a more
adaptive and accurate manner. Other strategies include acceptance, mindfulness techniques,
etc., several of which will be reviewed in this module

Facilitator Tip — Ask the group members to select which broad type of emotion regulation
technique is best: suppression or reappraisal. Elicit examples from all participants about times
they used one versus the other and the effect (and affect) they experienced. After some
discussion, it is helpful to point out that, statistically, studies have shown that suppression
results in less positive emotions, worse relationships, and reduced quality of life compared to
those who reappraise or utilize another adaptive strategy.

The goal, of course, is to live our life in “wise mind,” although this is not always possible no
matter how skilled one is. However, to help ensure we live our life in balance more often than
not, there are a set of skills that can be practiced. Linehan (1993) referred to these skills as
“core mindfulness skills” and were broken down into the WHAT and HOW skills.

Facilitator Tip — \t is helpful to ask group members to define and describe these concepts prior
to going into much detail to determine how much information was retained from the core
program. Of course, detailed descriptions should then be provided to ensure proper
understanding and skills should be practiced along with feedback and coaching.

The WHAT skills

e Observe — This skill asks you to be “in the moment” and surrender to the experience
from a distance by allowing all thoughts and emotions to happen without reacting, as
well as taking note of all sensory information (sight, sound, feel, smell, taste).

e Describe — With this skill you will define your experience by using words to explain what
thoughts and feelings you experienced. The important caveat of this skill is to resist the
desire to attach meaning to the thought or feeling. Simply describing the experience is
the primary goal here.

e Participate — As in the observe skill, you will surrender to the experience, but instead of
watching from a distance, you will immerse yourself in the experience and learn to go
with the flow of events. Attend only to what is necessary in the moment and appropriate
to the situation. Think of this as adjusting the volume of your voice to fit a particular
environment so you can be heard. The final part of participating is practicing. As you
practice, your response will become more natural.

The HOW Skills

The HOW skills can be viewed as adjectives and adverbs for the WHAT skills. They provide
information about the manner in which the WHAT skills are to be performed. They are non-
judgmental, one-minded, and effective.

e Non-Judgement — Let the experience and the thoughts and emotions related to it just
“be.” Resist the temptation to “escape” the experience, although it may not feel
completely comfortable. Reject nothing. What you resist, persists.

e One-Minded — Attend to the moment. Though your mind may wander (e.g., | wonder

e Effective — As with the other skills both in HOW and WHAT, this is concerned with being

concern with whether it is right or wrong. With mindfulness, the virtue or value is in the
outcome of experience, not the correctness. Again, focus on the present and not a

“wishful” or “could be” response and result.

Experiential Exercise: Wise Mind Meditation

Remind group members about the difference in emotional and rational minds and the benefits to
adopting “wise mind.” At its most basic form, wise mind helps us make healthy decisions about
our life. A useful tool that can reinforce adoption of wise mind is to practice meditation. Some
people can be turned off by meditation given its occasional spiritual undertones, but it does not
have to involve spirituality (although it can). Meditation is essentially a skill to be practiced
where we train our minds to be present, in the moment, and less reactive. However, like when
building a muscle, one practice session won't change anything; it is best to incorporate this
practice into your daily life, ideally when not overly stressed or anxious. Even 10-minutes per
day can make a huge difference. Research has shown that meditation has a positive influence
on anxiety and tension, improved sleep, chronic pain, improved blood pressure, as well as other
benefits.

There are two basic forms of meditation that can be practiced daily (i.e., Progressive Muscle
Relaxation and Deep Breathing) and have shown to have an important effect on healthy living. It
is recommended that clinicians practice both types of relaxation techniques with clients in group
and to encourage clients to continue to practice these strategies daily. Returning to these
exercises in group is also recommended on a regular basis.

Distress Tolerance

Sometimes it is difficult to find a quiet space to engage in a mindfulness meditation exercise.
Sometimes we need to sound the alarm and have a quicker response to a distressing emotional
response. Of course, the goal is not to completely eliminate the emotion, but rather lower the
intensity just enough for some space to refocus and refresh (Linehan, 1993).

Considerable time can be spent on the following skills. Facilitators are encouraged to ensure
that clients 1) have a comprehensive understanding of each skill and 2) have determined which
skills are more effective for them. Group exercises and role-plays can be very effective for
practicing some of these skills; between-session “behavioral experiments” where clients practice
implementing these, recording the results, and discussing during the following group are

particularly important.

Wise Mind ACCEPTS (Linehan, 1993) — The purpose of this skill is to give potential options for
distracting from a particularly distressing event, and potentially provide an opportunity to
intervene and alter the emotional response.

ACCEPTS is an acronym where each letter has a potential option.

A — Activities: This means getting involved in something other than attending to the
emotional experience. Choices may include exercising, walking, read a book, listen to
music, etc. It is important to be specific about what you intend to do. What exact song,
album, or playlist will you listen to? What type and how many push-ups will you do?

C — Contribute: This type of activity focuses on helping others. Choosing to contribute
might mean volunteering your time, writing a note to a significant other, cooking dinner
for the family, buying or making a small gift, or a random act of kindness for the person
behind you in the drive-thru.

C — Comparisons: Generally, comparing yourself to others can be counterproductive,
especially if we’re only doing it to downgrade our own life. However, making
comparisons to other situations can help take the sting out of our present circumstances.

E — Emotions: This is a process of finding an activity that will help exchange the current
emotion for one less intense and distressing. An example of this would be listening to a
high tempo, upbeat, song when feeling depressed or extremely sad.

P — Pushing Away: This option involves rejecting the current emotion in the present
moment and denying it any energy. Statements such as, “I am choosing to not let the
anger get the best of me,” and “Whatever” are examples of this. A word of caution:
Pushing away should only be used sparingly and only in extreme cases. Emotional
experience is important, as previously noted, otherwise persistent use of this skill can be
counterproductive.

T — Thoughts: Here the intervention is quite simply changing the thought to one that is
more positive or distracting. It can be relevant or completely unrelated to the present
situation. The intention here is to lessen the fuel provided to the emotional response.

a thought by experiencing a sensation. This could be clenching your fists as hard as
possible and counting to 10, followed by releasing them, and then repeating the process
a couple of times. Another could be eating something extremely sour or holding a piece
ice in your hand. Sensation is another device that should be used in small doses

Automatic Thought (AT): Automatic thoughts are fixed beliefs and thoughts that you have
every day. Everyone has them. They are separate self-statements or ideas that occur so rapidly
that it doesn’t seem as if they are even conscious thoughts. They seem to come out of the blue
for no apparent reason. Automatic thoughts are typically specific in content and tell you
something directly about the present situation you are experiencing. This has also been defined
as a type of cognitive appraisal in that it helps filter our experiences and allows us to respond to
situations in a particular way. They also occur continually throughout the day as we move about
and engage in constant evaluation of our environment and ourselves. Again, automatic thoughts
set the stage for how you will interpret and/or respond to a given situation; whether that is
influencing another thought, eliciting an emotion, or driving a behavior. Even though we may be
unaware of the source of the thought, we usually have a familiarity with the emotions we

experience.

Facilitator tip: \t is important to ask the clients to define, describe, and relate these definitions to
their own life and understanding prior to providing a more explicit description.

Automatic thoughts have both benefits and drawbacks. A benefit is that they help you process
information quickly and efficiently. If you had to process every thought you have for every new
and unique circumstance, you would likely not get much done. They also have protective factors
that keep you safe from threats, and quite possibly save your life. Take for example when you
are driving and approach a traffic light at an intersection that is changing from green to red.
Chances are you automatically begin to brake and slow to a stop. That is, unless you try to beat
the yellow light! As you brake and come to a stop, you are seldom aware of the chain of
thoughts that cause you to apply pressure to the brake pedal. Examples of statements might
include, “red means stop,” “if | run the light I'll get a ticket,” and so on. If you had to consciously
process any one of these statements before you applied the brake, you would be through the
intersection before you had a chance to respond, putting yourself and others at risk.

As mentioned, some automatic thoughts are not beneficial and may result in negative
consequences or results. Think back to the previous module where you were asked to list all the
thoughts you had when you were told by your supervisor/significant other/probation officer, “I
need to talk to you.” What were those thoughts? Were any of them negative? Examples might

Intermediate beliefs are attitudes or rules that a person follows in his/her life that typically
apply across situations (not situation specific as with automatic thoughts). They make up the
second layer of belief systems. Intermediate beliefs can often be stated as conditional rules: “If
x, then y.” For example, “If | have money, then | will be loved by others.” Individuals create these
assumptions by categorizing the information they receive from the world around them. These
rules guide thoughts and subsequently influence behaviors. They may also be described as
expectations. Dysfunctional core beliefs drive dysfunctional rules (intermediate beliefs) and in
kind, automatic thoughts. For example, the belief “I am unlovable” may be driving the conditional
rule, “if | am rich, then | will be loved by others,” which may drive obsessive thinking about ways

in which to get rich quick and engaging in illegal behavior.

Core beliefs lie at the center of the belief system and consist of the thoughts and assumptions
we have about our environment and our experience of it. They are most often formed in
childhood and are influenced by many factors, including our families, cultural and ethnic
backgrounds, and, may also be shaped by other social and temporal factors (e.g., socio-
economic status, the time period during which we were born, etc.). Core beliefs are solidified
over time as a result of our ongoing perceptions and interpretations of experiences. Aaron Beck,
the “father” of Cognitive Therapy, suggested that core beliefs tend to involve either acceptance
(“I'm unlovable”) or achievement (“I'm incompetent”) issues. Core beliefs may also be about
others (i.e., stereotypes). Because individuals encountering personal/psychological challenges
tend to store information consistent with negative beliefs, but ignore evidence that contradicts
them, core beliefs tend to be rigid and pervasive. Core beliefs tend to be more global and cut
across multiple life and environmental domains. For instance, if the core belief is, “I'm no
good/unlikeable/unworthy,” then the individual may feel that way in their family, their job, or their
group of friends.

Cognitive Schemas

As discussed throughout this curriculum, core beliefs, or cognitive schema as some people call
them, are a set of beliefs and attitudes that help people understand and organize what is going
on around them. Jeffrey Young, one of the leading figures in schema therapy and research,
defined schemas as, “any broad organizing principle for making sense of one’s life experience”
(Young et al., 2003, p. 7). Many schemas are formed early on in life and become entrenched as
we continue to encounter life experiences. Schemas can be positive and adaptive or they can
be negative and maladaptive.

People with different schemas can perceive events in their world very differently than someone
with a different schema, and this can also influence behavior in very different ways. For
example, a person with a schema that he is not a good person will interact with the world in a
much different way than someone with a schema that they are good and trustworthy. Another
example involves authority figures.

A few schemas have been identified that have been found to be more common among people

so even attempting to change is pointless. In this schema, the same principles apply

committing sexual abuse as behavior that is outside of his control. He believes it is
okay to act on his sexual desires, even if the other person doesn’t or can’t consent to

Cognitive Distortions

Cognitive Schema are considered a small subset of cognitive distortions. Considerable time was
spent in the core program on cognitive distortions. Given the importance of thinking errors in
emotions and behaviors, it is helpful to review with the group again.

Ask the group members to define what cognitive distortions are and why people use them.
Cognitive distortions refer to the assessments and judgements that people make in a situation
as it is happening. Cognitive distortions are the things that people tell themselves in a situation

to make themselves feel okay about their reactions and behaviors.

Present the following cognitive distortions. Since this is the maintenance phase, individuals
should be able to present with a relatively good understanding of what each distortion describes
and provide examples from their own life.

1. All or Nothing Thinking — Also referred to as “black and white” thinking, “polarized
thinking,” or “splitting.” The belief that things are all one way or the other, either all good
or all bad. People are either friends or enemies, either complete successes or total
failures, either saints or monsters. This distortion ignores the complexity of situations

and individuals and reduces everything down to a single judgmental quality.

Example: “I didn’t do everything perfectly, so I’m a total screw-up.” Or “That guy said
something that made me mad, he must be a complete asshole.”

2. Catastrophizing — Grossly exaggerating the potential negative consequences of a
situation. Any bad thing that happens becomes the end of the world, and you can’t think
about anything else or keep things in perspective. This can result in “doomsday
thinking,” when you become so focused on the possibility of a terrible crisis that you
become paralyzed and cannot respond to a negative situation with any positive

solutions.

Attachment Theory
Prior to presenting the core tenets of attachment, it is useful to ask the group members first in
order to understand the information that was retained from the core phase of the program.

Attachment is described as a long-lasting psychological connection with a meaningful person
that caused pleasure while interacting and soothed in times of stress. The quality of attachment
has a critical effect on development and has been linked to various aspects of positive
functioning, such as psychological well-being. It has been defined as lasting psychological
connectedness between human beings and is similar in concept to an emotional bond.
Attachment is often established during infancy with the primary caregiver and are further formed
through ongoing “attachment transactions,” defined as a continuing process of seeking and

maintaining a certain level of proximity to another individual.

The attachment styles presented in the core phase of the program were based on Bowlby’s
attachment theory (1988) and were tested with the strange situation paradigm.

Secure Attachment
Bowlby (1988) described secure attachment as the capacity to connect well and
securely in relationships with others while also having the capacity for autonomous
action, as situationally appropriate. Secure attachment is characterized by trust, an
adaptive response to being abandoned, and the belief that one is worthy of love. Secure
adults tend to hold positive self-image and positive image of others, meaning that they
had both a sense of worthiness and an expectation that other people were generally
accepting and responsive. Notably, many secure adults may, in fact, experience
negative attachment-related events, yet they are able to objectively assess people and
events and assign positive value to relationships in general.

Anxious Attachment (some call this “Preoccupied”)
Anxious attachment (also called ambivalent) relationships are characterized by a
concern that others will not reciprocate one's desire for intimacy. As adults, those with an
ambivalent attachment style are overly concerned with the uncertainty of a relationship.
Individuals with this attachment style hold a negative self-image and a positive image of

Page 51 of 85

CONFIDENTIAL
HOPE_000400
===== page 52 =====
others, meaning that they have a sense of unworthiness but generally evaluated others
positively. As such, they strive for self-acceptance by attempting to gain approval and
validation from their relationships with significant others. They also require higher levels
of contact and intimacy from relationships with others.

Avoidant Attachment (some call this “Dismissive”)
A dismissive-avoidant attachment style is demonstrated by adults who hold a positive
self-image and a negative image of others. They prefer to avoid close relationships and

intimacy with others in order to remain a sense of independence and invulnerability.

Dismissive-avoidant adults deny experiencing distress associated with relationships and
downplay the importance of attachment in general, viewing other people as
untrustworthy.

Fearful Attachment
Individuals with a fearful avoidant attachment style have characteristics of both anxious
and avoidant individuals. Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) write that they tend to have
negative views of both themselves and others, feel unworthy of support, and anticipate
that others will not support them. As a result, their behavior may be confusing to friends
and romantic partners; they may encourage closeness at first and then emotionally or
physically retreat when they start to feel vulnerable in the relationship.

A similar, yet more simplified, dimensional model of attachment was formulated by Bartholomew
and Shaver (1998) and is particularly applicable to adult relationships. The four types are
differentiated based on ones’ view of themselves and others.

Love Languages

According to a theory by Gary Chapman (1995), each person has one primary and one
secondary love language. Facilitators, present the love languages to the group, although ask
the members to see if they can recall the key elements of the different types.

Language 1: Words of Affirmation
One way to express love emotionally is to use words. Verbal compliments, or words of
appreciation, are powerful communicators of love. They are best expressed in simple,
straightforward statements of affirmation, such as:

e "You look really nice in that suit."
e "Do you ever look incredible in that dress! Wow!"
e "lL really like how you're always on time to pick me up at work."

e "You can always make me laugh."

Words of affirmation are one of the five basic love languages. Within that language,
however, there are many dialects. All of the dialects have in common the use of words to
affirm one's spouse. Psychologist William James said that possibly the deepest human need
is the need to feel appreciated. Words of affirmation will meet that need in many individuals.

Language 2: Quality Time
Quality time refers to giving someone your undivided attention. This does not mean
sitting on the couch watching television together. When you spend time that way, Netflix
or HBO has your attention — not your spouse. What | mean is sitting on the couch with
the TV off, looking at each other and talking, devices put away, giving each other your
undivided attention. It means taking a walk, just the two of you, or going out to eat and
looking at each other and talking. Time is a precious commodity. We all have multiple
demands on our time, yet each of us has the exact same hours in a day. We can make
the most of those hours by committing some of them to our spouse. If your mate's
primary love language is quality time, she simply wants you, being with her, spending

time.

Language 3: Receiving Gifts
Almost everything ever written on the subject of love indicates that at the heart of love is
the spirit of giving. All five love languages challenge us to give to our spouse, but for
some, receiving gifts, visible symbols of love, speaks the loudest. A gift is something you
can hold in your hand and say, "Look, he was thinking of me," or, "She remembered
me." You must be thinking of someone to give him or her a gift. The gift itself is a symbol
of that thought. It doesn't matter whether it costs money. What is important is that you
thought of him or her. It is not the thought implanted only in the mind that counts, but the
thought expressed in actually securing the gift and giving it as the expression of love.
But what if the person says, "I'm not a gift giver. | didn't receive many gifts growing up. |
never learned how to select gifts. It doesn't come naturally for me." Congratulations, you
have just made the first discovery in becoming a great lover. You and your spouse
speak different love languages. Now that you have made that discovery, get on with the
business of learning your second language. If your spouse's primary love language is
receiving gifts, you can become a proficient gift giver. In fact, it is one of the easiest love

languages to learn.

Language 4: Acts of Service
Consider actions such as cooking a meal, setting a table, emptying the dishwasher,
vacuuming, changing the baby's diaper, picking up a prescription, keeping the car in
operating condition — they are all acts of service. They require thought, planning, time,
effort, and energy. If done with a positive spirit, they are indeed expressions of love.

Awillingness to examine and change stereotypes is necessary in order to express love
more effectively. Remember, there are no rewards for maintaining stereotypes, but there
are tremendous benefits to meeting the emotional needs of your spouse. If your
spouse's love language is acts of service, then "actions speak louder than words.”

Language 5: Physical Touch
We have long known that physical touch is a way of communicating emotional love.
Numerous research projects in the area of child development have made that

conclusion: babies who are held, stroked, and kissed develop a healthier emotional life

is also a powerful vehicle for communicating marital love. Holding hands, kissing,
embracing, and sexual intercourse are all ways of communicating emotional love to
one's spouse. For some individuals, physical touch is their primary love language.
Without it, they feel unloved. With it, their emotional tank is filled, and they feel secure in
the love of their spouse. Implicit love touches require little time but much thought,

Risk Factors: Unhealthy Sexuality

In the context of healthy sexuality, it makes sense to review the following risk factors that are
associated with unhealthy sexuality as well as re-offending: Sexual Preoccupation, Sex as
Coping, and Deviant Sexual Interests. Perhaps some of these were noted during the previous

exercise.
The facilitator should ask participants to define each risk factor and why they are problematic.

Sexual preoccupation is a construct that has many names, such as sexual addiction,
hypersexuality or compulsive sexual behavior disorder. Regardless of what we call it, it
highlights two main components: sex drive and problematic sexuality. Sex drive includes sexual
frequency and preoccupation, such as number of sexual partners and how much one thinks
about sex, whereas problematic sexuality includes impulsivity and using sex to cope with

negative emotional states.

With regard to deviant sexual interests, group members should be reminded about the
paraphilias and the ones that are found more commonly among people who have committed
sexual offenses.

Paraphilias are defined in the latest version of DSM as recurrent and intense sexual arousal to
atypical objects or activities, as manifested in sexual fantasies, urges, or behavior over at least
a six-month period (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013). In the DSM-5, paraphilias
are distinct from paraphilic disorders; the latter term denotes a paraphilia that is accompanied
by distress or impairment in functioning. Paraphilias are necessary, but not sufficient, for
determining the presence of a paraphilic disorders (APA, 2013). Eight paraphilias are
specifically listed in the DSM-5: pedophilia (prepubescent children), exhibitionism (exposing
one's genitals to an unsuspecting stranger), voyeurism (spying on unsuspecting strangers in
normally private activities), sexual sadism (inflicting humiliation, bondage, or suffering), sexual
masochism (experiencing humiliation, bondage, or suffering), frotteurism (touching/rubbing
against a non-consenting person), fetishism (non-genital body parts or nonliving objects), and

Homework: Risk Factors and Coping (Homework Assignment 3.6.1)

In this assignment, group members describe the feelings and thoughts that could put them at
risk for offending again. This should include the events or situations where one could be at risk.
For each risk factor identified, ensure that a specific coping skill is provided. The assignment is
typically presented by the group member, and after positive comments and praise, the group
proceeds to ask questions or provide feedback.

Facilitator Tip — Some clients may indicate that they are not at risk or that there is no situation
that would pose a risk to them moving forward. It is important to reiterate that no one presents
with zero risk and that it is those individuals who acknowledge some risk (even if minor) who
tend to excel in life. A helpful analogy is driving a car. We can and should obey traffic laws, wear
a seat belt, have a valid driver's license, etc., but there is always a risk of getting into a car
accident. At the very least, we should be aware of the possibility of putting ourselves in a
situation where we can be accused of an offense. While it may not seem fair, those individuals
who have a prior criminal record have to be particular vigilant about ensuring they are adhering
to their good life plan.

things on this list do not need to be in the assignment, although at this stage in the curriculum,
many of these items should be present. However, inclusion of any of these items is worthy of
praise. After the question and feedback portion, this assignment is typically re-written so that it
adequately reflects all of the feedback from the group (and you).

Homework: Behavioral Progression (Homework Assignment 3.6.2)

Facilitators should provide clients with the behavioral progression handout (handout 7) and
illustrate it on the board to aid discussion. Ensure that clients understand the main components
of the model.

Distal problems or background factors are the areas of one’s life that make someone vulnerable
to committing a sexual offense. This can include factors in early life or more recent attitudes and
beliefs. Triggering events or life events are factors that tend to start the behavioral progression;
that is, the path toward a sexual offense. Of course, the impact of the triggering event is often
influenced by other factors, such as our interpretation of that event. The distress factor speaks
to how the individual responded to the triggering event. Typically, some emotional distress is
present, whether it is anger, frustration, loneliness, or some other emotional experience. A
maladaptive response describes how the client reacted to the triggering event and their initial
distress. As noted in the handout, this could include substance use, intense emotional states, or
maladaptive attitudes. Finally, there is the offense.

Facilitator Tip — \t would be helpful to integrate elements of the self-regulation model (e.g., goals
and strategies with respect to offending and what type of post-offense evaluation they had) as
well as relapse prevention (e.g., high risk situations) in your discussions (see handouts 5 and 6).

Once everyone has a good understanding of the model and has provided some input, the
clients are ready to be assigned the behavioral progression homework assignment. Given the
depth of this assignment, no more than one or two clients should present, as it is important to
leave room for feedback and questions. Depending on the feedback, clients often redo this
assignment so that they can incorporate the helpful feedback provided.

Homework: Self-Management Plan (Homework Assignment 3.6.3)

In this final assignment, group members reflect on everything they have learned in the entire
program across all phases and modules. Individuals are to write out a plan for how they are
going to manage their life differently and more positively in the future. Of course, some elements
of the previous exercise can be included (i.e., risk factors and coping), but emphasis should also
be placed on their goals for the future. For those nearing their discharge date, these goals
should reflect their life after parole. For those who are not nearing their discharge date, the
goals can reflect life after they transition to the aftercare program at HOPE.

A good self-management plan should incorporate elements that are going to reduce risk as well
as promote a healthy, good life. A good question to start this process is to ask them to visualize
themselves 1 year or 5 years after parole (if relevant). How would they be different. Be specific
about what this person looks like and how do they achieve it.

What makes a good self-management plan?

e Solution-focused coping strategies

e The identification of external support systems (family and friends)

e Achievable and realistic goals and expectations

e References to strengths that the individual will build upon

e Approach-oriented goals

e Choosing appropriate living companions and location

e Sensible work plans that are attainable and free from access to potential victims

Facilitator Tip — Although everyone's self-management plan will be different, the above noted
features are generally a sign of a good plan.
