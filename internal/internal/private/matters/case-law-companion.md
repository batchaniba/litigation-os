## LANE 1 — Protective Orders (Family Law §§ 4-501–4-516)

**Critical practice flag: the burden of proof changed in 2014.** Pre-2014 decisions (Musser, Barton, Ricker, Piper) recite a clear-and-convincing standard. In 2014 the General Assembly amended FL § 4-506(c)(1)(ii), lowering the standard for a final protective order to **preponderance of the evidence**. Read the pre-2014 cases' burden language as superseded on that point; their holdings on sufficiency, credibility deference, fear, and statutory purpose remain good law. (Verified at https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2015RS/fnotes/bil_0005/hb0225.pdf)

### 1. Coburn v. Coburn, 342 Md. 244, 674 A.2d 951 (1996)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 85, Sept. Term 1995, opinion by Chasanow, J., filed April 17, 1996.** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-appeals/1996/85a95-1.html
The husband challenged admission of evidence of alleged prior abusive acts (July, July, and November 1994 incidents) at the protective-order hearing, arguing they were not pleaded in the petition. The Court held that allegations of a prior history of abuse are **admissible at a protective-order hearing regardless of whether they were sufficiently pleaded** in the original petition. The legislature did not intend to limit evidence to the specific act that led to the ex parte filing — such a limit would defeat the statute's remedial and preventive purpose. Evidence of past abuse is often the most indicative evidence of the likelihood of future abuse, helps the judge understand the context of the present allegation, and helps formulate an appropriate remedy. Judgment affirmed.

### 2. Katsenelenbogen v. Katsenelenbogen, 365 Md. 122, 775 A.2d 1249 (2001)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 139, Sept. Term 2000, opinion by Wilner, J., filed July 13, 2001.** Verified at https://www.mdcourts.gov/data/opinions/coa/2001/139a00.pdf
The protective order had expired by its terms (Jan. 3, 2001), making the case moot, but the Court addressed it as a matter of important public concern under *Coburn*. Two holdings matter for counsel. First, the Court of Special Appeals erred in overlooking that the shoving the trial court found to have occurred constituted **second-degree assault (battery)** — an act of "abuse" under § 4-501(b) — and in treating "fear of imminent serious bodily harm" as the only abuse at issue. Second, the Court adopted the **victim-perspective test** for reasonableness of fear: a person subjected to § 4-501(b) abuse "may well be sensitive to nonverbal signals or code words that have proved threatening in the past to that victim but which someone else, not having that experience, would not perceive to be threatening"; reasonableness "must be viewed from the perspective of the particular victim," and "any special vulnerability or dependence by the victim, by virtue of physical, mental, or emotional condition or impairment, also must be taken into account." Disposition: vacated the CSA judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the appeal as moot.

### 3. Piper v. Layman, 125 Md. App. 745, 726 A.2d 887 (1999)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, 1999.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md-app/125/case-pdfs/0745-01.pdf
The leading reported decision on non-physical conduct and appellate review. The Court held the appeal was **not moot** despite the order's expiration: given "the stigma that is likely to attach to a person judicially determined to have committed abuse," the chance to vacate the order gives "substance to [the] appeal." On the merits the Court **reversed** for insufficiency: no testimony permitted an inference that the appellant committed any act meeting the statutory definition of "abuse" — the trial judge's invocation of the criminal harassment statute was error; the Domestic Violence Act requires one of the enumerated acts. The decision also restates the appellate framework: petitioner bears the burden of proof; facts found below stand unless clearly erroneous (Md. Rule 8-131(c)); the appellate court makes its own independent appraisal of the ultimate legal conclusion.

### 4. Ricker v. Ricker, 114 Md. App. 583, 691 A.2d 283 (1997)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, decided March 28, 1997 (Sonner, J.).** Verified at https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/ricker-v-ricker-no-891600301
The foundational reported decision on **denial** of a protective order. Review of a grant or denial is for **abuse of discretion**; where evidence conflicts, the hearing court's facts stand unless clearly erroneous, and credibility determinations belong entirely to the trial judge. The opinion states the petitioner's burden as it then existed (clear and convincing — now superseded by preponderance). *Ricker* also holds that although the General Assembly recognized "battered spouse syndrome" in CJP § 10-916 for criminal cases, a court in a domestic-violence hearing is **not required in all circumstances** to accept that syndrome as an explanation for a petitioner's delayed complaint. Denial affirmed.

### 5. Barton v. Hirshberg, 137 Md. App. 1, 767 A.2d 874 (2001)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, filed March 1, 2001 (Adkins, J.).** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2001/2322s99-1.html
Affirms denial of a permanent protective order and crystallizes two points. First, the evidentiary framework: petitioner must prove abuse; on conflicting evidence facts stand unless clearly erroneous and credibility is the trial court's province (citing *Piper* and *Ricker*). The trial court found the respondent's car struck the petitioner but was not shown to have been done purposefully — findings supported and not clearly erroneous. Second, on **statutory purpose**: quoting *Coburn* (quoting *Barbee*), the Act's goals are "preventive, protective and remedial, not punitive" — it is not punishment for past conduct. With no evidence of past recklessness or intentional abuse creating ongoing danger, the order would have served only as punishment, so denial was proper. Also rejects the argument that intent is irrelevant and a claim of improper judicial motive.

### 6. Musser v. Christie, 131 Md. App. 200, 748 A.2d 1027 (2000)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, filed March 29, 2000 (Salmon, J.).** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2000/1736s99-1.html
Reverses a final protective order for **insufficiency of the evidence** — a cautionary case on what does *not* constitute statutory "abuse." A grandmother obtained an order against her daughter based on parental irresponsibility (leaving a five-year-old for days without contact) and violation of the ex parte order. The Court held there was "not a scintilla of evidence" that any act placed the child in **fear of imminent serious bodily harm** — neglect, however serious, does not satisfy § 4-501(b)(ii), and violating the ex parte order does not convert the conduct into abuse. Also rejected reliance on the child-abuse definition in FL § 5-701(b) absent a finding of physical or mental injury. Judgment reversed. (Burden recited reflects pre-2014 law.)

### 7. Barbee v. Barbee, 311 Md. 620, 537 A.2d 224 (1988)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, decided February 11, 1988 (Murphy, C.J.).** Verified at https://static.case.law/md/311/case-pdfs/0620-01.pdf
The Court of Appeals' early construction of the Act's nature and purpose, repeatedly quoted by *Coburn* and *Barton*. Petitions under the Act are "plainly civil cases, equitable in nature," and "the Act was designed to aid victims of domestic abuse by providing an immediate and effective **nonmonetary** remedy" — no provision for monetary awards. Because the protected interests cannot be valued in money, there is no monetary "amount in controversy," so appeals from District Court final protective orders to circuit court are tried **de novo**. A footnote flags that the Act's household-member/co-residence prerequisites must actually be satisfied — a standing issue-spot for any petition. **Note:** *Barbee*'s "nonmonetary remedy" characterization predates HB 497 (Ch. 791), effective October 1, 2026, which adds monetary relief to final protective orders (new FL § 4-506(d)(14)). See `legislative-update-2026.md`.

### 8. Streater v. State, 352 Md. 800, 724 A.2d 111 (1999)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, filed February 17, 1999 (Chasanow, J.).** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-appeals/1999/30a98-1.html
A criminal case included as the Court of Appeals' principal treatment of the **evidentiary weight of protective-order findings**. The Court held the existence of a protective order was properly before the jury in a stalking/harassment trial, but admitting the written order with its other-crimes findings without a Md. Rule 5-404(b) analysis was error: the trial court must find special relevance beyond propensity, find the accused's involvement by clear and convincing evidence, and weigh probative value against unfair prejudice. Importantly, the Court acknowledged that protective-order factual findings — threats placing the victim in fear of imminent bodily harm, assault/battery — carry **significant probative value** on intent and course-of-conduct elements. Cuts both ways: prior findings are potent evidence of threats/fear/intent, but admission is tightly policed.

### 9. C.M. v. J.M., 258 Md. App. 40, 295 A.3d 193 (2023)
**Appellate Court of Maryland, decided May 24, 2023 (Wright, J.).** Caption/citation/court/date verified at https://www.leagle.com/decision/inmdco20230524307 (full text paywalled; standards and disposition corroborated by opinion excerpts and later quoting opinions — caveat noted).
The modern reported protective-order decision — the only one here applying the **current preponderance standard** — and it extends "abuse" to sustained non-physical conduct toward a child. The circuit court entered a final protective order on findings that the father "repeatedly communicated in person and through text messages homophobic comments and religious beliefs, causing mental injury" to his 12-year-old son, who feared his father's anger. Affirmed. The opinion restates the governing framework: a final protective order issues on a finding "by a preponderance of the evidence that the alleged abuse has occurred"; appellate review defers to facts and credibility but independently appraises the ultimate legal conclusion (*Piper*). Clearest reported authority that sustained verbal/emotional conduct without physical violence can support a protective order through the child "mental injury" prong.

**Lane 1 issue-spots for counsel:** (1) Burden is preponderance now — cite C.M. v. J.M., not the older cases, for the burden. (2) Fear must be tied to a statutory act — *Piper* and *Musser* reversed where no enumerated "abuse" was proved; *Katsenelenbogen* supplies the victim-perspective lens. (3) Past acts are admissible and weighty — *Coburn*, plus *Streater* on protective-order findings proving intent/course of conduct. (4) Denials get heavy deference — *Ricker*, *Barton*. (5) Expired orders are still appealable — *Piper*'s stigma-based mootness exception. (6) Non-physical conduct can qualify — *C.M. v. J.M.* marks the current boundary.

## LANE 2 — Divorce Economics: Monetary Award (§ 8-205), Alimony (§ 11-106), Attorney's Fees

### 10. Tracey v. Tracey, 328 Md. 380, 614 A.2d 590 (1992)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 156, Sept. Term 1991, opinion by Murphy, C.J., decided October 29, 1992.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md/328/case-pdfs/0380-01.pdf
The indefinite-alimony anchor. The primary question was whether the trial court erred in disregarding the wife's part-time-job wages in computing the award. The Court's enduring holdings: "self-sufficiency per se does not bar an award of indefinite alimony if there nonetheless exists an unconscionable disparity in the parties' standards of living after divorce" (328 Md. at 392–93); trial courts have "wide latitude" in fashioning alimony awards (id. at 394, cited in *Blaine*, 336 Md. at 67); and courts must reject "a mechanical application of the statute that would limit the discretion of the court to fashion an appropriate alimony award."

### 11. Sharp v. Sharp, 58 Md. App. 386, 473 A.2d 499 (Md. App. 1984)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, decided April 9, 1984.** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/1984/770-september-term-1983-0.html
The foundational Maryland **dissipation** decision. Where a chancellor finds property was "intentionally dissipated in order to avoid inclusion of that property towards consideration of a monetary award," that is "no more than a fraud on marital rights," and the chancellor "should consider the dissipated property as extant marital property … to be valued with the other existing marital property" — even if unrecoverable from a bona fide purchaser (58 Md. App. at 399). "Dissipation may be found where one spouse uses marital property for his or her own benefit for a purpose unrelated to the marriage at a time where the marriage is undergoing an irreconcilable breakdown" (id. at 401). Also states the monetary-award process (categorize marital vs. nonmarital; value; then award) and places the burden of proving values on the party disputing valuation.

### 12. Omayaka v. Omayaka, 417 Md. 643 (2011)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, filed January 24, 2011.** Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/opinions/coa/2011/111a08.pdf (official cite confirmed; regional-reporter cite not re-verified from a reporter volume — caveat noted).
Maryland's highest court systematized the dissipation doctrine: the test is whether a spouse "spent or otherwise depleted marital funds or property with the principal purpose of reducing the amount of funds that would be available for equitable distribution at the time of the divorce" (quoting *Welsh v. Welsh*, 135 Md. App. 29, 51 (2000)). The critical inquiry is **purpose, not mere expenditure**: "What matters is not that one spouse has, postseparation, expended some of the marital assets, what is critically important is the purpose behind the expenditure." Dissipation can be found even absent an irreconcilable breakdown and even where the principal purpose was something other than reducing distributable funds (e.g., spending on a paramour or gambling). On burdens: the claimant carries the initial burden of production **and** the ultimate burden of persuasion; after a prima facie showing, only the burden of production shifts to the alleged dissipator. A no-dissipation finding is factual, reviewed for clear error.

### 13. Innerbichler v. Innerbichler, 132 Md. App. 207, 752 A.2d 291 (Md. App. 2000)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, amended opinion filed June 16, 2000.** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2000/149s99-1.html
The leading intermediate-court case on business-interest appreciation as marital property under § 8-205: the husband's personal efforts drove his company's post-marriage growth, so appreciation in his 51% interest was marital property; affirmed a $2,581,864.75 monetary award. Upheld a blended alimony structure — $8,000/month rehabilitative for five years followed by $6,000/month **indefinite** — explaining Maryland's scheme favors rehabilitative alimony but expressly authorizes both components, with broad discretion (citing *Coviello*). The only error was valuation of the husband's premarital interest; remanded solely for that recalculation. Practice note: the § 8-205(b) factor playbook for entrepreneurial marriages — and proof that valuation battles, not factor analysis, usually decide these appeals.

### 14. Digges v. Digges, 126 Md. App. 361, 730 A.2d 202 (Md. App. 1999)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, decided May 26, 1999.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md-app/126/case-pdfs/0361-01.pdf
A 26-year marriage case affirming three linked rulings. First, **voluntary impoverishment**: upheld imputing the husband's potential income (a disbarred lawyer making no real effort to find work, living on family "loans and gifts") for child support, alimony, and attorney's fees — the touchstone is capacity and effort. Second, **indefinite alimony**: the trial court converted rehabilitative alimony to permanent after finding that even after the wife made all reasonably expected progress toward self-support, the parties' standards of living would be unconscionably disparate (wife's $30,000–35,000 teacher ceiling vs. husband's six-figure potential) — a textbook § 11-106(c)(2) application. Third, affirmed a $76,000 attorney's-fee award calculated on potential income.

### 15. Crabill v. Crabill, 119 Md. App. 249, 704 A.2d 532 (Md. App. 1998)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, 1998.** Verified at https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/md-court-of-special-appeals/1465705.html
An 18-year marriage case with four practice-relevant holdings. (1) Imputation to the payor affirmed: $11,200/year painting income imputed to a voluntarily retired firefighter with part-time painting history — a retiree is not exempt where he has the experience and ability to work. (2) No imputation to the dependent spouse: the court **declined** to impute paralegal-level income to the wife because her lower-paying secretary job's flex-time was necessary to care for their special-needs daughter — courts cannot require unreasonable steps toward self-support. (3) Indefinite alimony affirmed on unconscionable disparity after all reasonably expected self-sufficiency progress. (4) Methodology: the § 11-106 analysis was upheld even where it effectively equalized incomes; there is "no bright line" for alimony amounts, and the court need not recite a formal § 11-106(b) checklist so long as the record shows the mandated factors were considered.

### 16. Rock v. Rock, 86 Md. App. 598, 587 A.2d 1133 (Md. App. 1991)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, decided April 1, 1991.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md-app/86/case-pdfs/0598-01.pdf
Covers both alimony and monetary-award mechanics. On alimony, affirmed indefinite support, restating the governing standard: "Indefinite support may be warranted where it would be impractical to expect the economically dependent spouse to become self-supporting through further education or training or where rehabilitative spousal support for a limited period of time would result in gross inequity" (86 Md. App. at 609, quoting *Holston*); chancellor's discretion governs absent clear error or abuse. On property: save for certain employee benefits, a court may grant only **one** monetary award, and the court reversed a $2,200 assessment against the husband for the wife's waste of the common fund because it duplicated her loss and gave the husband a windfall — a warning that "waste" and dissipation must be counted once, not punitively.

### 17. Davis v. Petito, 425 Md. 191, 39 A.3d 96 (Md. 2012)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, filed February 27, 2012.** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-appeals/2012/30-11.html
The controlling Court of Appeals framework for counsel fees under Family Law § 12-103 (custody/support proceedings). § 12-103 is "an exception to the 'American rule'" permitting fee awards. Before awarding fees, the court **must** consider the three § 12-103(b) factors: (1) financial status of each party, (2) needs of each party, and (3) whether there was substantial justification for bringing, maintaining, or defending the proceeding. Under § 12-103(c), a finding of an **absence** of substantial justification — with no good cause to the contrary — **mandates** a fee award. "Substantial justification" relates to the **merits** of the party's position, not extraneous circumstances. Here the Court reversed a $30,773.54 fee award because the trial court treated the mother's pro bono representation as making her financially "better off" — an erroneous § 12-103(b) consideration — and remanded. (Also noting *Henriquez v. Henriquez*, 413 Md. 287 (2010): pro bono representation does not bar a fee award to the pro-bono-represented prevailing party.) Note: § 11-110 contains the parallel framework for alimony-proceeding fees.

### 18. Blaine v. Blaine, 336 Md. 49, 646 A.2d 413 (Md. 1994)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, 1994.** Verified via opinion text at https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/blaine-v-blaine-no-893477992 and the intermediate opinion at https://static.case.law/md-app/97/case-pdfs/0689-01.pdf
The Court held that Family Law § 11-107 authorizes a trial court to **extend** an alimony award for an indefinite term even though indefinite alimony was not awarded at divorce — affirming extension of $800/month alimony indefinitely where changed circumstances (the wife's inability to obtain employment in her retraining field despite diligent effort) plus the prospect of a "harsh and inequitable result" justified it. Reiterated the two statutory prongs for indefinite alimony — inability to become self-supporting (§ 11-106(c)(1)) or unconscionable disparity (§ 11-106(c)(2)) — and reaffirmed the "wide latitude" of trial courts (336 Md. at 67), while restating Maryland's baseline policy that "the prior concept of alimony as lifelong support … has largely been superseded by the modern view that the dependent spouse should be required to become self-supporting" (id. at 69). The 23% income-ratio affirmance is an unconscionable-disparity calibration point.

**Lane 2 issue-spots for counsel:** (1) Dissipation is the § 8-205 remedy for depleted assets — *Sharp* supplies the "treat as extant" remedy, *Omayaka* the purpose-focused proof standard and burden mechanics. (2) Indefinite alimony turns on the two § 11-106(c) prongs — *Tracey* (disparity despite self-sufficiency), *Blaine* (§ 11-107 extension), *Rock* (the two-prong restatement), *Digges*/*Crabill* (calibration and imputation). (3) Voluntary impoverishment/potential income cuts both ways — *Digges* (impute to payor), *Crabill* (don't impute where caregiving constrains the dependent spouse). (4) Fees require the § 12-103(b) factor findings — *Davis v. Petito*. (5) Don't double-count waste — *Rock*.

## LANE 3 — Interspousal Torts

### 19. Bozman v. Bozman, 376 Md. 461, 830 A.2d 450 (2003)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 105, Sept. Term 2002, opinion by Bell, C.J., decided August 12, 2003.** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-appeals/2003/105a02-1.html
William Bozman sued his ex-wife for malicious prosecution, alleging she fabricated criminal charges (stalking, harassment, protective-order violations) during their divorce. The Court **abrogated interspousal immunity in full as to intentional torts**, completing what *Boblitz v. Boblitz*, 296 Md. 242 (1983) began for negligence: "we abrogate the interspousal immunity rule, a vestige of the past, whose time has come and gone, **as to all cases alleging an intentional tort**," applied to the case at bar and to "all causes of action accruing after the date of the filing of this opinion." Together, *Boblitz* + *Bozman* = complete abrogation. Temporal limit: prospective from August 12, 2003 — not retroactive to already-accrued claims.

### 20. Lusby v. Lusby, 283 Md. 334, 390 A.2d 77 (Md. 1978)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, opinion by Smith, J.** Verified at https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/lusby-v-lusby-no-886948585
The wife sued her husband for damages from a gunpoint abduction: he pointed a high-powered rifle at her, had companions force her car off the road, struck her, tore her clothes off, and "forcefully and violently" raped her (aiding companions' attempted rape), then threatened to kill her if she reported it. The Court reversed dismissal on immunity grounds: "We shall here hold that under the facts and circumstances of this case, **amounting to an outrageous, intentional tort, a wife may sue her husband for damages**." *Lusby* is the closest Maryland appellate analog to a gunpoint/coerced-sexual-assault fact pattern — the first breach of immunity — but its exception was deliberately confined to "outrageous" intentional torts (see *Doe*); plead severity facts.

### 21. Doe v. Doe, 358 Md. 113, 747 A.2d 617 (Md. 2000)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 99, Sept. Term 1998, opinion by Eldridge, J., decided March 7, 2000.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md/358/case-pdfs/0113-01.pdf
A husband sued his wife for fraud and IIED based on her adultery and misrepresentation of paternity, joined with divorce/custody claims. The Court of Appeals reinstated dismissal on two grounds. First, *Lusby* was expressly limited to "outrageous" intentional torts — the narrow reading was correct; broad readings were erroneous. Second, the fraud/IIED counts were repackaged "criminal conversation" (the civil remedy for a wife's adultery), abolished in *Kline v. Ansell*, 287 Md. 585 (1980). Issue-spot: even without immunity, tort claims entangled with divorce face a viability/public-policy screen — courts look past labels to substance. Distinguish abuse torts (assault, battery, false imprisonment, IIED tied to physical abuse — the *Lusby* core) from marital-misconduct torts (adultery/paternity), which courts resist under the criminal-conversation abolition.

### 22. Bender v. Bender, 57 Md. App. 593, 471 A.2d 335 (Md. App. 1984), cert. denied, 300 Md. 152 (1984)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md-app/57/case-pdfs/0593-01.pdf
The husband sued his wife for conversion where the trial court found she directed their minor son to steal $93,500 from him; the parties were already divorced when the action was brought. The CSA affirmed, holding immunity "legally inappropriate" for an intentional tort under the circumstances, and that severity goes to damages, not liability. **Caveat**: *Bender*'s broad *Lusby* reading (any intentional tort defeats immunity) was **rejected by *Doe* (2000)** as authority; *Bozman* (2003) mooted the immunity debate anyway. Cite *Bender* only for the severity-to-damages proposition, with this caveat.

**Lane 3 issue-spots for counsel:** (1) Interspousal tort claims are viable — immunity fully abrogated (*Boblitz* negligence + *Bozman* intentional torts, claims accruing on/after Aug. 12, 2003). (2) *Lusby* is the closest factual analog for gunpoint/coerced-sexual-assault facts — plead severity. (3) Fraud/IIED theories entangled with marital misconduct face the *Doe* public-policy screen — keep abuse torts (assault, battery, false imprisonment) distinct from infidelity-based theories.

## LANE 4 — Wiretap / Interception (Cts. & Jud. Proc. §§ 10-401 et seq.)

Maryland is an **all-party-consent** jurisdiction: prohibition on willful interception (§ 10-402), exclusion of unlawfully intercepted communications in "any trial, hearing, or other proceeding" in the State (§ 10-405), and a civil damages action (§ 10-410). Maryland's Act is stricter than federal Title III (one-party consent) — **all parties** must consent.

### 23. Standiford v. Standiford, 89 Md. App. 326, 598 A.2d 495 (Md. App. 1991), cert. denied Feb. 21, 1992
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland.** Verified at https://static.case.law/md-app/89/case-pdfs/0326-01.pdf
The only verified **spousal wiretap** case. The husband (a police officer and sole telephone subscriber in the marital home) secretly listened to and recorded his wife's phone calls from 1984–1987 using hidden handsets, tape recorders, and timers. After their 1987 divorce, the ex-wife sued for invasion of privacy and violation of the Maryland Wiretapping Act; the jury awarded $12,500 actual, $25,000 punitive, $9,500 attorney's fees. The CSA affirmed across the board: (a) § 10-402 clearly prohibits **all** willful interceptions, with **no spousal exception** — sole-subscriber status did not exempt him; (b) § 10-410 civil remedies affirmed (actual/liquidated damages $100/day, punitive, fees); (c) §§ 10-405 and 10-410 harmonized — illegally intercepted communications **are** admissible in a § 10-410 civil action for the narrow purpose of proving the violation; (d) the recorder has no standing to object — § 10-410 protects only persons whose communications were intercepted. Issue-spot: no spousal exemption — secretly recording a spouse's calls carries criminal (§ 10-402(b), felony, up to 5 years) and civil (§ 10-410) exposure; the recorded spouse has a fee-shifting civil remedy. Conversely, counsel must warn an abuse victim before she secretly records the abuser: the recording itself may violate § 10-402, be excluded under § 10-405, and expose her to prosecution and a § 10-410 suit.

### 24. Deibler v. State, 365 Md. 185, 776 A.2d 657 (Md. 2001)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 125, Sept. Term 2000, opinion by Wilner, J., filed July 17, 2001.** Verified at https://mdcourts.gov/data/opinions/coa/2001/125a00.pdf (non-domestic case — caveat noted).
Deibler hid an audio camera in his friend's parents' bathroom and recorded the friend's aunt showering and her conversation. Conviction under § 10-402(a)(1) affirmed. Holding: "willfully" under § 10-402(a)(1) means **intentional or purposeful conduct** — it does **not** require knowledge that the interception is unlawful or a bad motive. Issue-spot: the criminal-exposure baseline for any domestic secret recording — "I didn't know it was illegal" is no defense; purposeful recording suffices.

### 25. Agnew v. State, No. 9, Sept. Term 2018 (Md. Nov. 20, 2018)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, opinion by Hotten, J.** Verified at https://mdcourts.gov/data/opinions/coa/2018/9a18.pdf and https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-appeals/2018/9-18.html (official Atlantic reporter cite not yet independently verified — caveat noted; non-domestic case — caveat noted).
Agnew secretly recorded his own cell-phone conversation on his iPhone voice-memo app without the other party's consent; the State offered the recording at his gun/drug trial. Affirmed admission: where a party participates in or consents to the recording, § 10-402 does not bar admission of the recording **against the consenting party**. The all-party-consent requirement protects the *nonconsenting* recorded person, not the recorder; the recorder cannot claim "aggrieved person" status (§§ 10-401(1), 10-408(i)) to suppress his own recording. Reaffirms *State v. Maddox*, 69 Md. App. 296 (1986). Issue-spot: the recorder is not the Act's protected class — a spouse who secretly records the other cannot use the Wiretap Act as a shield; under *Standiford* he is its target.

**Lane 4 issue-spots for counsel:** (1) No spousal exemption — *Standiford* is the controlling authority; secret spousal recording = felony exposure + fee-shifting civil suit for the victim. (2) "I didn't know it was illegal" fails — *Deibler*. (3) Warn any abuse victim before she records the abuser — the recording may itself violate § 10-402, be excluded under § 10-405, and boomerang. (4) No verified Maryland case was found on a spouse recording **in-person** conversations in the home (vs. telephone interception) — worth a Westlaw/counsel check.

## LANE 5 — Financial Exploitation of Older / Susceptible Adults

### 26. Tarray v. State, 410 Md. 594, 979 A.2d 729 (2009)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, filed September 14, 2009 (Greene, J.).** Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/opinions/coa/2009/149a08.pdf
Tarray was convicted of exploiting a vulnerable adult under Criminal Law § 8-801 for siphoning money and property from a cognitively impaired elderly man; convictions affirmed. On sufficiency, the victim's "between a rock and a hard place" testimony did not prove "undue influence" — undue influence requires domination amounting to force and coercion, overpowering the victim's will so he cannot act freely. But the evidence of "deception" was ample: excessive self-paid wages, bank accounts and a credit card opened in the victim's name without authorization, and taking present and future interests in the victim's home and truck. Also held the unit of prosecution for § 8-801 is per victim. Issue-spot: § 8-801 reaches family-member and caregiver exploitation, not just strangers, via three routes — deception, intimidation, undue influence.

### 27. Molina v. State, 244 Md. App. 67, 222 A.3d 222 (2019)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, filed December 23, 2019 (Leahy, J.).** Verified at https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2019/2537-17.html
Caregivers convicted of theft-scheme, two counts of financial exploitation of a vulnerable adult (§ 8-801(b)(1)) and of a person ≥68 (§ 8-801(b)(2)), and conspiracy, for extracting roughly $450,000+ from a 99-year-old dementia patient. Affirmed: evidence sufficed that defendants knew or should have known the victim was a vulnerable adult and knowingly and willfully exploited him by deception and undue influence. Vacated one of two conspiracy convictions — a single unlawful agreement supports only one conspiracy conviction. Also held the State could introduce evidence of the defendants' gambling losses and financial distress under the "special circumstances" nexus to motive. Issue-spot: the closest appellate analogue for caregiver exploitation of a cognitively impaired elder; validates motive evidence (gambling debts, financial desperation) as admissible.

### 28. Upman v. Clarke, 359 Md. 32, 753 A.2d 83 (2000)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 120, Sept. Term 1999 (opinion filed 2000).** Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/data/opinions/coa/2000/120a99.pdf
In paired will-caveat and trust-amendment litigation, the jury found the decedent had testamentary capacity and was not subject to undue influence, and the Court affirmed. On the trust amendments — where the defendants admitted a confidential relationship — the Court held a confidential relationship gives rise to a presumption of undue influence, but the trusted party's resulting burden is only a burden of **production** (Md. Rule 5-301), not persuasion: the burden of persuasion on undue influence remains with the contestant throughout. Also held that a principal–agent (power of attorney) relationship does not automatically create a presumed confidential relationship as a matter of law. Issue-spot: the burden mechanics for contesting suspect inter vivos transfers and POA-driven dispositions.

### 29. Figgins v. Cochrane, 403 Md. 392, 942 A.2d 736 (2008)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 46, Sept. Term 2007.** Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/data/opinions/coa/2008/46a07.pdf
Affirmed a judgment against a daughter who, as her elderly father's primary caregiver acting under his power of attorney, used the POA to deed his home to herself. A confidential relationship existed between caregiver-daughter and wheelchair-bound father, and she failed to rebut the presumption that the inter vivos transfer was the product of her undue influence — the donee bears the "heavy burden" of showing the transfer was fair and the grantor's free, uninfluenced act with full knowledge. Constructive trust imposed, returning the home to the estate. Also held the POA's gift section did not authorize a self-dealing transfer of the home to the agent. Issue-spot: the strongest Maryland precedent for POA abuse by a family caregiver — confidential relationship + failed rebuttal = voided transfer via constructive trust; POA gift language does not license self-dealing.

**Civil-remedy context (legislative, not case law):** Maryland has authorized the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division and the Securities Commissioner to bring *civil* damages actions for § 8-801 violations on behalf of victims and their estates (2016/2018 statutes; Commercial Law § 13-204 and Corporations & Associations § 11-209). Note: the task's Estates & Trusts §§ 13-601–13-607 framing did not map to a verified appellate decision — the verified exploitation law runs through Criminal Law § 8-801 and the undue-influence/confidential-relationship common law (*Upman*, *Figgins*). Counsel should confirm the current §§ 13-601–13-607 referent.

## LANE 6 — Revenge Porn / Nonconsensual Image Disclosure (Crim. Law § 3-809)

### 30. Gardener v. State, No. 215, Sept. Term 2022 — Appellate Court of Maryland, filed June 23, 2023 (Albright, J.) — UNREPORTED
Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/sites/default/files/unreported-opinions/0215s22.pdf
Affirmed a Baltimore City jury conviction under § 3-809. "Distribute" under § 3-809(a)(2) is broad — give, sell, transfer, disseminate, publish, upload, circulate, broadcast, make available, allow access — and requires no specific method. Circumstantial evidence sufficed to prove the defendant was the distributor: the victim's testimony that the defendant was the only other person with the images, plus motive, opportunity, and screenshots — no direct digital-forensic evidence required.

### 31. Flood v. State, No. 1694, Sept. Term 2023 — Appellate Court of Maryland, filed July 17, 2025 (Tang, J.) — UNREPORTED
Verified at https://www.mdcourts.gov/sites/default/files/unreported-opinions/1694s23.pdf
Affirmed a Baltimore City jury conviction for "distribution of a visual representation of another which exposes intimate parts or sexual activity" under § 3-809, arising from an ex-boyfriend's distribution of a sex video via a Facebook message containing a Dropbox link after a three-month relationship ended. The opinion restates the § 3-809 elements: a misdemeanor to knowingly distribute another's exposed intimate parts with intent to harm, harass, intimidate, threaten, or coerce, knowing or recklessly disregarding consent, where the victim reasonably expected the image would remain private. Shows how attribution battles (fake-profile defenses) play out at trial and on appeal.

### 32. Williams v. State, 232 Md. App. 342, 157 A.3d 398 (2017)
**Court of Special Appeals of Maryland, filed March 30, 2017 (Arthur, J.).** Verified at https://cases.justia.com/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2017-0537-16.pdf?ts=1490904148
**Important caveat: the defendant was acquitted of the § 3-809 charge.** The circuit court granted judgment of acquittal on the revenge-porn count (an ex-girlfriend's nude photo appeared on her Facebook account following a violent altercation); the jury convicted only of second-degree assault. The appeal turned on evidentiary issues and the court affirmed; the opinion discusses § 3-809 only as background. Useful only for its fact pattern (nonconsensual posting of a partner's intimate image in an IPV context) and as a boundary marker — it is not authority on § 3-809 elements or proof.

### 33. Williams v. State, 457 Md. 551, 179 A.3d 1006 (2018)
**Court of Appeals of Maryland, No. 25, Sept. Term 2017, opinion by Hotten, J., filed February 21, 2018.** Verified at http://mdcourts.gov/opinions/coa/2018/25a17.pdf (vLex's "Md. App." court tag is erroneous — the mdcourts.gov PDF confirms Court of Appeals).
On certiorari from the above, affirmed: a prior conviction is not irrelevant as a matter of law to a character witness's opinion merely because it predates the witness's relationship with the defendant or is 25+ years old. As above, the § 3-809 charge had been acquitted at trial; the opinion mentions § 3-809 only in the factual background and makes no § 3-809 law.

**Civil dimension — GENUINE GAP.** After genuine searching of Google Scholar, Justia, CourtListener, and mdcourts.gov, **no Maryland appellate decision — reported or unreported — was found applying § 3-809 conduct as the basis for civil liability/damages/injunctive relief, or applying the revenge-porn prong of Family Law § 4-501(b)(1)(vii) as the basis for a protective order.** *Vines v. Surage*, Nos. 439 & 442, Sept. Term 2024 — Appellate Court of Maryland, filed Nov. 22, 2024 (Zarnoch, J.), UNREPORTED (verified at http://mdcourts.gov/sites/default/files/unreported-opinions/0439s24.pdf) — quotes the § 4-501(b) "abuse" definition including the revenge-porn prong in boilerplate, but the protective order actually granted rested on assault/stalking facts, not revenge-porn conduct. It confirms the gap rather than filling it. **Legislative context:** SB 360 (2025 session) — "Revenge Porn – Civil Action and Criminal Offense" — proposed adding a civil cause of action; as of this research the civil remedy's enactment was not confirmed.
